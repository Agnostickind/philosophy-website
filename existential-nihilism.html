Existential Nihilism

Existential nihilism is the philosophical view that life has no objective, inherent, or ultimate meaning, purpose, or value. It is the form of nihilism most people have in mind in everyday speech, and it asks whether meaning must be discovered in the world, for instance in a divine plan or a cosmic purpose, or whether it can only be created by human beings. The thesis concerns the status of meaning itself, not merely whether particular people feel that their lives are meaningful, and philosophers disagree about whether the absence of objective meaning leaves any meaning at all.[1]

What Is Existential Nihilism?

Existential nihilism is the view that human life lacks objective meaning, purpose, or value of the kind that would exist independently of human attitudes. It denies that the universe was made for a purpose, that history has an intrinsic goal, or that any activity is significant in a way that does not depend on what people happen to care about. The thesis is a claim about the status of meaning, which is different from the observation that some individuals struggle to find purpose in their own lives.[1][2]

The term should not be confused with existentialismA, the philosophical tradition that asks how individuals should live when meaning is not given in advance. Existential nihilism is one of the positions that existentialist and absurdist thinkers confront. For the wider family of nihilist views, see the main nihilism page, and for the ethical form of nihilism see moral nihilism.[20]

History of Existential Nihilism

Also read: Explore more history History of philosophy traces how thinkers and philosophical traditions have explored questions about reality, knowledge, morality, and human existence across different periods and cultures. Read article →

The question of whether human life possesses meaning, purpose, or lasting significance has appeared in philosophical and religious thought across many centuries. However, the explicit position that existence has no inherent or objective meaning became especially prominent in modern European philosophy during the nineteenth century. This development was closely connected to changing intellectual conditions, including the declining authority of traditional religious explanations, the growing influence of scientific thought, and the difficulty of establishing universal moral and metaphysical foundations. As inherited beliefs became increasingly open to criticism, philosophers began confronting a difficult question: if the universe has no predetermined purpose, what gives human existence its significance? [2]

The history of existential nihilism is therefore not a straightforward progression of thinkers defending the same conclusion. Earlier traditions explored impermanence, suffering, the limits of human achievement, and the apparent absence of lasting satisfaction, while modern philosophers examined whether life itself could have an objective purpose. Some regarded this possibility as a source of despair, others treated it as a philosophical problem requiring a response, and some attempted to discover meaning through human freedom, religious commitment, or the acceptance of life's limitations. These different approaches gradually shaped the modern discussion of existential nihilism.

Ancient Roots

Although existential nihilism emerged as a more recognizable philosophical position in modern thought, questions about the transience and significance of human existence are much older. The Hebrew Bible's Book of Ecclesiastes, whose speaker repeatedly describes human striving as "vanity" or a "breath," reflects on the fleeting nature of pleasure, wealth, achievement, and worldly ambition. Its central concern is the difficulty of finding something permanent or ultimately satisfying within ordinary human pursuits. Nevertheless, Ecclesiastes is not an expression of existential nihilism in the modern sense. Its conclusion retains a religious framework, affirming reverence for God and the value of ordinary human experiences, and scholars interpret its philosophical and theological message in different ways. B [19]

Other ancient traditions approached related questions from different philosophical directions. Greek skeptical thinkers questioned whether human beings could establish certain knowledge about reality, while Buddhist traditions examined impermanence, suffering, and attachment to things that cannot provide lasting satisfaction. These positions should not be confused with the claim that life has no meaning: Buddhist philosophy, in particular, offers substantial ethical and spiritual frameworks rather than simply denying value. Their relevance lies in how they challenged assumptions about permanence, satisfaction, and the foundations of human experience. They provide important historical parallels to questions that would later become central to existential nihilism.

Schopenhauer and Tolstoy

During the nineteenth century, Arthur Schopenhauer developed one of the most influential philosophical accounts of human suffering and dissatisfaction. In The World as Will and Representation, he described the underlying reality of existence in terms of a blind, striving will that has no rational ultimate purpose. Human desires continually produce new forms of dissatisfaction: when a desire is fulfilled, another soon takes its place, while unfulfilled desires generate suffering. For Schopenhauer, this made ordinary happiness fragile and difficult to sustain. Although his philosophy is more accurately described as metaphysical pessimism than existential nihilism, it brought the problem of life's value into sharper focus by questioning whether existence could offer lasting satisfaction. [7]

Leo Tolstoy approached a related problem through personal experience rather than an abstract metaphysical system. In A Confession, he describes a profound spiritual and existential crisis that developed despite his literary success, social position, and material comfort. He became increasingly troubled by the question of what purpose human life could possess if death eventually erased every achievement. Even scientific knowledge and philosophical reasoning appeared unable to provide the kind of ultimate answer he was seeking. Tolstoy eventually found a resolution through religious faith, particularly his understanding of the faith and everyday life of ordinary people. His account illustrates how the absence of an apparent ultimate purpose can become a deeply personal crisis rather than merely an intellectual problem. [8]

Nietzsche and the Death of God

Friedrich Nietzsche gave the problem of nihilism one of its most influential modern formulations. In The Gay Science, particularly through the famous parable of the madman, he introduced the declaration that "God is dead." This statement did not simply announce the decline of religious belief. It described a broader cultural and philosophical crisis: the traditional Christian worldview had provided European society with a framework for understanding truth, morality, human purpose, and the place of existence within a larger cosmic order. As the authority of this framework weakened, the foundations supporting many inherited values also became uncertain. [6]

Nietzsche recognized that the collapse of established religious and moral foundations could produce nihilism: the experience that existence lacks an objective purpose and that previously accepted values no longer possess unquestioned authority. He distinguished between passive nihilism, which responds to this crisis through resignation and exhaustion, and more active responses that challenge inherited values and create the possibility of new ones. His philosophical project was therefore not an endorsement of meaninglessness. Rather, he sought to understand how human beings could confront the crisis of nihilism without surrendering to it. Ideas such as the revaluation of values and the affirmation of life became central to this undertaking. [17]

Twentieth-Century Developments

During the twentieth century, questions about meaninglessness, freedom, and the absence of predetermined purpose became central to several existentialist and related philosophical movements. The experience of modernity, political upheaval, war, and changing religious and social structures provided an important historical background for these discussions. However, existentialist thinkers did not all reach the same conclusions. Some examined the apparent absurdity of existence, while others explored whether human freedom could provide a basis for meaning in a world without predetermined purposes.

Albert Camus, in The Myth of Sisyphus, examined the absurd as the confrontation between the human desire for clarity and meaning and a world that offers no definitive answer. He rejected both suicide and what he regarded as philosophical escape from this confrontation, arguing instead for lucidity and revolt. Although frequently associated with existentialism, Camus resisted the label and developed his own philosophy of the absurd. [4]

Jean-Paul Sartre approached the problem through human freedom and responsibility. In Existentialism Is a Humanism, he argued that if there is no God who establishes a fixed human nature or predetermined moral purpose, individuals must define themselves through their choices and actions. His earlier novel Nausea also explores the unsettling contingency of existence, depicting a world in which things and human beings have no obvious justification for being as they are. Sartre's response was not that life is necessarily meaningless, but that meaning is not supplied in advance and must be formed through human existence. [5]

Later discussions also moved beyond continental existentialism. In analytic philosophy, Thomas Nagel examined the problem of life's apparent absurdity in his essay The Absurd. He questioned whether the tension between human seriousness and the possibility of viewing our lives from a detached perspective makes existence absurd. Rather than concluding that life is worthless, Nagel suggested that recognizing this tension can change how seriously we regard our own ambitions and concerns. His analysis helped give the philosophical problem of life's meaning a more precise form, ensuring that existential questions remained an important subject of debate in contemporary philosophy. [15] [3]

Objective and Subjective Meaning

The debate surrounding existential nihilism often begins with a distinction between objective and subjective meaning. Subjective meaning arises from an individual's experiences, commitments, relationships, desires, and personal sense of purpose. It depends on what a person cares about and considers worth pursuing. Objective meaning, by contrast, would possess value independently of any individual's preferences or feelings. For example, a person may find deep purpose in raising a family, pursuing knowledge, or creating art, but the philosophical question is whether these activities have significance beyond the value that human beings themselves attach to them. Existential nihilism generally denies that human existence possesses an inherent or objective purpose. However, this does not automatically settle whether subjective meaning is genuine or sufficient. The central disagreement is whether a life can be meaningful through human experiences and commitments even if the universe provides no ultimate justification for them.[1]

Supernaturalism

Supernaturalist theories argue that meaning ultimately depends on something beyond the natural world, most commonly God, a divine purpose, or the existence of an immortal soul. According to these views, human life gains its deepest significance through its relationship with a higher reality, whether by fulfilling a divine purpose, participating in a larger spiritual order, or continuing beyond physical death. From this perspective, temporary achievements and personal experiences may have value, but they cannot provide ultimate meaning unless they are connected to something enduring. The existential nihilist challenge becomes particularly relevant when these supernatural foundations are rejected. If there is no divine creator, predetermined purpose, or afterlife, supernaturalists and their critics disagree about whether anything remains capable of grounding objective meaning. However, not every religious philosopher accepts that meaning depends exclusively on an afterlife or a single predetermined purpose. [1]

Naturalism and Subjectivism

Naturalist approaches challenge the idea that meaning requires a supernatural foundation. They argue that meaningful existence can emerge within the natural world through human relationships, intellectual pursuits, creative activity, moral commitments, and contributions to the lives of others. Subjectivists emphasize the importance of personal engagement: an activity can make life meaningful because an individual genuinely cares about it. However, objective naturalists maintain that personal enthusiasm alone may not be sufficient; some pursuits possess genuine value beyond an individual's interest in them. For instance, a person may feel deeply engaged in an activity that produces no worthwhile result or involves harming others. This raises the question of whether meaning requires both personal involvement and something objectively valuable to be involved in. Philosopher Susan Wolf develops this approach through the idea that meaning arises when subjective attraction meets objective attractiveness, bringing together personal fulfilment and worthwhile activity. [9] [10]

The Nihilist Position

Existential nihilism takes the position that human existence has no inherent or objective meaning, purpose, or ultimate significance. Stronger forms of the position may also reject the possibility that subjective commitments and personal achievements provide genuine meaning. From this standpoint, relationships, ambitions, moral projects, and creative accomplishments may matter deeply to individuals without possessing any independent or ultimate justification. However, the argument depends heavily on what counts as meaningful existence. If meaning must be objective and independent of human attitudes, then subjective fulfilment cannot establish it. If personal engagement and worthwhile experiences are sufficient, however, the absence of a cosmic purpose does not necessarily make life meaningless. This is why the debate cannot be resolved simply by establishing that the universe has no predetermined goal. It also requires examining whether meaning must be discovered as an external truth or can emerge through the ways human beings live, choose, and relate to the world.[1]

Arguments for Existential Nihilism

Several philosophical arguments have been developed to question whether human existence possesses objective meaning or ultimate significance. They approach the problem from different directions: the vastness of the universe, the inevitability of death, and the apparent absence of any predetermined purpose behind existence. Although these arguments do not establish existential nihilism beyond dispute, they challenge the assumption that human activities, achievements, and experiences possess lasting significance simply because they matter to us. Their central concern is whether the meaning people find in their lives can withstand a more detached examination of existence itself. [1]

Cosmic Insignificance

The argument from cosmic insignificance begins with the enormous scale of the universe in comparison with human existence. Earth occupies an almost imperceptible part of the observable cosmos, while individual human lives span only a brief interval in its history. From the perspective of sub specie aeternitatis (from the standpoint of eternity), personal ambitions, political achievements, and everyday concerns can appear remarkably small. The argument suggests that if something must possess significance beyond a limited human perspective to count as objectively meaningful, then our place in the universe provides little reason to believe that such meaning exists. However, this reasoning raises an important philosophical question: does something need to be cosmically significant to possess genuine value? The fact that an experience or achievement occupies a small place in the universe does not necessarily demonstrate that it is meaningless to the people affected by it. The argument therefore exposes a tension between cosmic scale and human significance rather than conclusively establishing that the latter is absent. [3]

Death and Impermanence

The argument from death and impermanence questions whether anything can possess lasting meaning in a world where every human life eventually comes to an end. Relationships are separated by death, achievements may disappear, memories gradually fade, and entire civilizations can eventually be forgotten. From this perspective, even the most significant human accomplishments seem vulnerable to the passage of time. If meaning requires permanence, then everything produced within mortal existence appears incapable of satisfying that requirement. This concern has deep roots in religious, philosophical, and literary thought, particularly in Ecclesiastes, with its reflections on the transience of human striving, and in Leo Tolstoy's A Confession, where awareness of death leads him to question the ultimate value of his achievements. [8] [19] Nevertheless, the argument depends on whether permanence is actually necessary for meaning. A temporary act of kindness, a relationship, or an intellectual discovery may possess genuine significance even if it does not last forever. The philosophical difficulty is therefore not simply that everything ends, but whether something must endure indefinitely to matter in the first place.

The Absence of a Cosmic Purpose

The argument from the absence of a cosmic purpose holds that the universe does not appear to have been created or directed toward any predetermined goal. If there is no divine designer, universal plan, or external authority assigning human beings a particular purpose, then existence may have no objective destination or ultimate justification. This concern became especially influential in modern philosophy through Nietzsche's diagnosis of the "death of God," which described the weakening of the religious foundations that had traditionally supported European systems of meaning and morality. [6] Modern scientific naturalism also provides a context for this debate by explaining natural processes without necessarily appealing to supernatural intentions or purposes. [14] However, the absence of a universal plan does not automatically establish that individual lives cannot have meaning. That conclusion follows only if meaningful existence requires a purpose imposed from outside human life. Naturalist and existentialist approaches challenge this assumption by suggesting that meaning may emerge through human relationships, choices, responsibilities, and activities rather than being assigned by the universe itself.

Philosophical Responses

The recognition that human existence may lack a predetermined or universally established purpose does not necessarily lead to the conclusion that life is worthless. Several philosophers have confronted this problem while questioning what kind of meaning remains possible in the absence of an external foundation. Their responses differ considerably: some emphasize accepting the absurdity of existence, others locate meaning in individual freedom and responsibility, while religious approaches seek significance through faith and a relationship with the divine. Rather than treating meaninglessness as an unavoidable endpoint, these perspectives examine how human beings might respond to uncertainty and construct, discover, or affirm significance within their lives. [2]

Camus and Revolt

Albert Camus approached the problem of meaning through his philosophy of the absurd, which describes the confrontation between humanity's desire for clarity, order, and ultimate purpose and a world that offers no definitive answer to these demands. In The Myth of Sisyphus, he argued that recognizing this conflict should not lead to suicide or an escape into what he called philosophical suicide: abandoning clear reasoning through an unsupported leap toward transcendent meaning. Instead, Camus proposed lucidity, freedom, and revolt as ways of living without denying the absurd condition.

Revolt means continuing to live consciously and fully despite the absence of ultimate justification, rather than surrendering to despair or pretending that the problem has been resolved. His interpretation of Sisyphus, condemned to push a rock uphill only for it to roll back down, illustrates this attitude. Camus concludes that one must imagine Sisyphus happy, not because his struggle acquires a hidden cosmic purpose, but because his awareness and refusal to surrender define his response to an otherwise indifferent condition. [4] C

Sartre and the Creation of Meaning

Jean-Paul Sartre approached the absence of predetermined meaning through human freedom and responsibility. His principle that "existence precedes essence" means that human beings do not arrive in the world with a fixed nature or purpose that determines what they must become. Unlike objects designed for a particular function, individuals first exist and subsequently define themselves through their decisions, actions, and commitments.

This freedom makes people responsible not only for the direction of their own lives but also for the values expressed through their choices. Sartre described this condition as both liberating and unsettling because individuals cannot completely escape responsibility by appealing to destiny, predetermined human nature, or external authority. His concept of anguish reflects the awareness of this responsibility, while his criticism of bad faith concerns attempts to deny or avoid one's freedom. Meaning, from this perspective, is not something waiting to be discovered in a universal plan; it develops through the ways individuals choose to live and take responsibility for their existence. [5]

Nietzsche and Affirmation

Friedrich Nietzsche's response to nihilism involved overcoming the loss of traditional values through an affirmation of life rather than searching for another transcendent foundation. He questioned moral and religious systems that treated earthly existence as inferior to an imagined higher reality, arguing that the collapse of these inherited frameworks created the need to reconsider how values are formed. His idea of amor fati, or love of fate, expresses an attitude of accepting and affirming existence, including its suffering, uncertainty, and difficulties, rather than merely tolerating them.

The thought experiment of eternal recurrence intensifies this challenge by asking whether one could will the repetition of every moment of one's life, exactly as it has occurred, without wishing to change anything. These ideas are connected to Nietzsche's broader project of life affirmation and the revaluation of values. His response does not amount to simply creating arbitrary personal meaning; it involves developing the capacity to affirm existence without depending on inherited guarantees of purpose. The precise philosophical relationship between eternal recurrence, self-overcoming, and life affirmation remains a subject of scholarly interpretation. [17] [6]

Kierkegaard and Faith

Søren Kierkegaard approached questions of existence, uncertainty, and meaning through individual faith and religious commitment. Unlike thinkers who attempted to establish meaning through human freedom alone, Kierkegaard placed the individual's relationship with God at the centre of his understanding of existence. In Fear and Trembling, through his interpretation of Abraham and the biblical command to sacrifice Isaac, he explored the tension between universal ethical obligations and the demands of an individual relationship with the divine.

His account of faith emphasizes personal commitment and the difficulty of reducing religious existence to detached rational explanations. This does not mean that Kierkegaard regarded faith as an arbitrary decision or simply a rejection of reason. Rather, he examined the uncertainty, anxiety, and responsibility involved in committing oneself to a religious life. His philosophy offers a response to existential meaninglessness grounded not in objective proof of a cosmic purpose, but in the individual's passionate engagement with faith and the divine. [18]

Existential Nihilism vs Pessimism and Absurdism: Key Differences

Existential nihilism is often confused with pessimism and absurdism. Existential nihilism claims that life lacks objective meaning, pessimism claims that life contains more suffering than value, and absurdism describes the tension between the human demand for meaning and a silent world. The three can overlap but are logically distinct.

Criticisms of Existential Nihilism

Critics of existential nihilism challenge its underlying assumptions, its definition of meaning, and the conclusion that human existence is ultimately meaningless. A central objection is that the absence of a predetermined cosmic purpose does not necessarily eliminate the possibility of meaningful human experiences, worthwhile activities, or objective values. Some philosophers argue that meaning can arise through human relationships and commitments, while others maintain that certain things possess genuine value independently of individual preferences or religious beliefs. These disagreements reveal that denying an externally assigned purpose and denying all possible forms of meaning are two different philosophical claims. [1]

Meaning Without Objectivity

One of the most common objections is that meaningful existence does not necessarily require an objective purpose established by the universe or a supernatural authority. Subjectivists argue that human experiences such as love, friendship, intellectual curiosity, creative work, and personal commitment can provide genuine meaning because individuals care deeply about them. Objective naturalists go further by maintaining that certain activities, including helping others, pursuing knowledge, and reducing suffering, can possess genuine value even when their significance is not recognized or appreciated by everyone.

Philosopher Susan Wolf, for example, argues that meaning emerges through active engagement in projects that are also objectively worthwhile. From this perspective, a meaningful life need not depend on a cosmic plan. Defenders of existential nihilism may respond that personal satisfaction and worthwhile experiences cannot answer the deeper question of whether human existence has an ultimate purpose. The disagreement therefore concerns whether meaning must be independent of human existence or whether value within human life is sufficient to make existence meaningful. [9] [10]

Impermanence Does Not Mean Worthlessness

Another important criticism challenges the assumption that something must last forever to possess genuine value. Arguments based on death and impermanence often suggest that human achievements, relationships, and experiences lose their significance because they eventually come to an end. However, critics argue that duration and value are not necessarily the same thing. A friendship does not become worthless because it ends, just as an act of kindness does not lose its significance because the moment eventually passes.

Many things that people consider deeply valuable are temporary, and their importance may lie in the experiences they create, the people they affect, or the consequences they produce rather than in their permanence. The nihilist can still question whether these values possess ultimate or objective significance, but the fact that something is temporary does not, by itself, demonstrate that it is meaningless. This objection challenges the assumption that permanence is a necessary condition for meaning.

The Problem of Objective Value

Existential nihilism also faces objections concerning the nature of value itself. Even if the universe has no predetermined purpose, it does not automatically follow that nothing can be objectively good, worthwhile, or significant. For example, the suffering of another person may provide a reason for compassion and action regardless of whether the universe has assigned human beings a particular purpose. Moral realists argue that at least some moral truths are independent of individual opinions, cultural preferences, or religious commitments.

If such values exist, the absence of cosmic purpose would not necessarily eliminate every foundation for meaningful human activity. However, moral value and existential meaning are not identical philosophical questions: establishing that an action is morally right does not by itself prove that human existence has an ultimate purpose. The objection therefore challenges the move from the absence of universal purpose to the broader conclusion that nothing possesses genuine value.

Nagel's Reply to the Arguments

Philosopher Thomas Nagel challenged several familiar arguments about life's apparent meaninglessness, particularly those based on cosmic insignificance and the passage of time. In his essay The Absurd, he questioned the assumption that our lives become meaningless simply because they occupy an insignificant place in the universe or because everything we accomplish will eventually be forgotten. If the fact that nothing will matter in a million years makes present concerns meaningless, he observed, it is difficult to explain why the passage of that amount of time should change their significance.

For Nagel, the absurd arises from a different tension: human beings take their lives, decisions, and commitments seriously while also possessing the ability to step back and examine them from a detached perspective. This capacity exposes the difficulty of providing an ultimate justification for the seriousness with which we live. Nevertheless, Nagel did not consider this recognition a reason for despair. He suggested that irony could be an appropriate response, allowing individuals to acknowledge the apparent absurdity of existence without abandoning their ordinary concerns and commitments. [3]

The Psychological Perspective

Psychological research approaches the question of meaning from a different direction by examining how individuals experience purpose, coherence, and significance in their lives. Researchers have developed instruments such as the Meaning in Life Questionnaire to investigate the presence of meaning and the search for it as distinct aspects of human experience. This research explores how relationships, personal goals, social connections, and individual commitments contribute to a person's sense that life is meaningful.

However, psychological findings about experienced meaning should not be confused with a philosophical demonstration that objective meaning exists. They establish something about human experience rather than settling the metaphysical question of whether existence possesses an ultimate purpose. Equally, a persistent feeling that nothing matters may sometimes accompany emotional distress, depression, or other difficulties. Such experiences deserve appropriate attention and support rather than being treated exclusively as abstract philosophical conclusions. [12]

Influence and Everyday Usage

On Existentialism and Absurdism

The problem of meaning gave existentialism and absurdism their central questions. Existentialists took the absence of given meaning as the starting point for thinking about freedom, responsibility, and authenticity, and Camus's absurdism offered a distinctive attitude toward it. Scholars have even described existential nihilism, through Camus's opening question, as the one truly serious philosophical problem.[4][13]

On Literature and Theatre

Twentieth-century literature and theatre explored the sense of a world without given meaning. Sartre's Nausea, Camus's The Stranger, and the theatre of the absurd, with Beckett's Waiting for Godot as a landmark, staged characters who wait, search, or act in the absence of an explanation, and they shaped how many readers first meet the idea.[15][16]

On Psychology and Psychotherapy

Existential psychotherapy treats questions of meaning as part of the human condition. Irvin Yalom identified meaninglessness as one of four "ultimate concerns," alongside death, freedom, and isolation, and argued that therapists should engage with them directly instead of treating them only as symptoms. This approach shows how a philosophical problem can be taken up in clinical practice.[11]

In Daily Language and Online Culture

In everyday language, "existential nihilism" and "existential dread" are often used for a mood of pointlessness. Online culture has also popularized "optimistic nihilism," the idea that the lack of cosmic meaning frees people to choose their own purposesE. Philosophers caution that popular usage can blur the difference between a thesis about objective meaning, a mood, and a mental health condition, and that the philosophical discussion is more varied than the memes suggest.[2]

Frequently Asked Questions About Existential Nihilism

What is existential nihilism?

Existential nihilism is the philosophical view that life has no objective, inherent, or ultimate meaning, purpose, or value. It concerns the status of meaning itself, not just whether particular people feel that their lives are meaningful.

Does existential nihilism mean life is meaningless?

It depends on what counts as meaning. Existential nihilism denies objective or cosmic meaning, but many philosophers argue that subjective meaning, found in relationships, work, and commitments, is still genuine. Others hold that only objective meaning counts, which is where the disagreement lies.

What is the difference between existential nihilism and existentialism?

Existential nihilism is the claim that life lacks objective meaning, while existentialism is a philosophical tradition that asks how individuals should live and choose when meaning is not given in advance. Existentialists such as Sartre often respond to nihilistic possibilities rather than accepting nihilism.

What did Camus say about nihilism?

Camus analyzed the absurd, the clash between the human search for meaning and a silent world, but he rejected nihilism as a conclusion. In The Myth of Sisyphus and The Rebel he argued for lucid revolt, freedom, and passion in response to the absurd.

Is existential nihilism the same as depression?

No. Existential nihilism is a philosophical thesis about objective meaning, while depression is a mental health condition. A persistent feeling that nothing matters can also be a symptom of distress, and anyone affected by it can seek support from a health professional.

What is optimistic nihilism?

Optimistic nihilism is a popular, non-academic label for the idea that the absence of objective meaning frees people to choose their own purposes. It is close in content to some existentialist and subjectivist views.

Who are the main philosophers of existential nihilism?

Schopenhauer and Nietzsche shaped the nineteenth-century discussion, Camus and Sartre responded to it in the twentieth century, and Thomas Nagel and Thaddeus Metz developed analytic treatments. Few of them accepted existential nihilism as their own conclusion.
