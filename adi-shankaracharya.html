Adi Shankaracharya: Life, Philosophy, Advaita Vedanta, and Ideas

Explore the life and philosophy of Adi Shankaracharya, the influential Indian philosopher associated with Advaita Vedanta and the teaching that ultimate reality is non-dual Brahman.

Who Was Adi Shankaracharya?

Adi Shankaracharya, also known as Adi Shankara or simply Shankara, was one of the most influential philosophers in the history of Indian thought. He is most closely associated with Advaita Vedanta, a philosophical tradition that teaches a radical form of non-duality.

Shankara's philosophy centers on the nature of ultimate reality, consciousness, the self, knowledge, and liberation. In Advaita Vedanta, Brahman is understood as ultimate reality, while the deepest self, or Atman, is ultimately identical with Brahman.

Shankara is particularly important because he developed systematic philosophical interpretations of the Upanishads, the Bhagavad Gita, and the Brahma Sutras. His writings became foundational to later Advaita Vedanta and had a lasting influence on Indian philosophy.

Adi Shankaracharya: Quick Facts

  • Name — Adi Shankaracharya, Adi Shankara, or Shankara
  • Period — Generally associated with the early medieval period of Indian philosophy; his exact dates are disputed
  • Philosophical tradition — Advaita Vedanta
  • Known for — Non-dualism, Vedanta philosophy, commentaries on major Hindu philosophical texts, and teachings on knowledge and liberation
  • Central concept — Brahman as ultimate reality
  • View of the self — Atman is ultimately identical with Brahman
  • Important concepts — Brahman, Atman, Maya, ignorance, knowledge, and moksha
  • Major philosophical sources — Upanishads, Bhagavad Gita, and Brahma Sutras
  • Historical importance — One of the most influential philosophers in the development of classical Advaita Vedanta

Adi Shankaracharya and the History of Indian Philosophy

Shankara lived during a period when several philosophical and religious traditions were developing sophisticated accounts of reality, knowledge, consciousness, and liberation. Vedanta itself had developed from interpretations of the Upanishadic tradition long before Shankara.

It is therefore more accurate to describe Shankara as a major systematizer of Advaita Vedanta rather than simply calling him its founder. Earlier thinkers, including Gaudapada, played important roles in the development of the philosophical tradition that preceded him.

The historical evidence concerning Shankara's life is limited. Later biographical traditions describe journeys across India, philosophical debates, disciples, and the establishment of monastic institutions, but these accounts were written centuries after his lifetime and must be distinguished from the more securely documented philosophical evidence.

Who Was Adi Shankara in Indian Philosophy?

Adi Shankara was an Indian philosopher, commentator, and teacher whose work became central to the Advaita Vedanta interpretation of the Upanishads.

His philosophical project was concerned with explaining the nature of Brahman, the self, consciousness, knowledge, and liberation. He argued that ordinary experience is characterized by mistaken identification and distinctions that conceal the deeper non-dual nature of reality.

Shankara did not reject reasoning altogether. He used philosophical argument extensively, but held that the ultimate knowledge of Brahman depends upon the authority of the Upanishadic revelation rather than being established by independent reasoning alone.

What Is Advaita Vedanta According to Shankaracharya?

Advaita Vedanta literally refers to a form of Vedanta centered on non-duality. The Sanskrit term advaita means "not two."

Shankara's Advaita Vedanta teaches that ultimate reality is Brahman, which is non-dual and not limited by the distinctions found in ordinary experience.

The deepest self, or Atman, is not ultimately separate from Brahman. The apparent distinction between individual self and ultimate reality results from ignorance and mistaken identification.

This makes Advaita Vedanta fundamentally different from philosophical systems that regard individual selves and ultimate reality as permanently separate.

What Is Brahman in Shankaracharya's Philosophy?

In Shankara's philosophy, Brahman is ultimate reality. Brahman is not merely one object among other objects in the universe. It is the fundamental reality upon which ordinary experience depends.

Advaita Vedanta describes Brahman in terms of existence, consciousness, and fullness or bliss, although these descriptions should not be understood as ordinary properties added to Brahman.

Shankara's interpretation emphasizes that Brahman is non-dual. It is not one separate entity standing alongside other equally ultimate entities.

What Is Atman in Advaita Vedanta?

Atman refers to the self. In Shankara's Advaita Vedanta, the deepest nature of the self is not the individual ego, personality, body, or changing mental states.

The true self is understood as consciousness. At the ultimate level, this consciousness is not different from Brahman.

This is why one of the central ideas associated with Advaita Vedanta is the identity of Atman and Brahman. The realization of this identity is connected with liberation from ignorance and mistaken self-identification.

Atman Is Not Simply the Ego

Shankara distinguishes the deepest self from the ordinary sense of "I" associated with the body, emotions, memories, desires, and personal identity. These changing features belong to empirical experience rather than representing the ultimate nature of the self.

What Is Maya in Shankaracharya's Philosophy?

Maya is one of the concepts most commonly associated with Advaita Vedanta. However, it is often oversimplified as meaning that "the world does not exist."

Shankara's philosophy is more subtle. Ordinary experience takes place at an empirical level in which people, objects, actions, causes, and effects are experienced as distinct. Advaita Vedanta nevertheless maintains that ultimate reality is non-dual Brahman.

Maya is therefore connected with the appearance of plurality and the conditions under which reality is experienced through distinctions. The relationship between Brahman, Maya, ignorance, and the empirical world became an important subject of interpretation in later Advaita philosophy.

It is therefore misleading to reduce Shankara's position to the simple statement that "everything is an illusion." His philosophy distinguishes different levels or senses of reality and experience.

What Does Ignorance Mean in Advaita Vedanta?

Ignorance, often discussed through the Sanskrit concept avidya, is central to Shankara's account of human bondage.

The fundamental problem is not simply a lack of information. It is the mistaken identification of the self with things that are not the ultimate self, including the body, mind, emotions, and individual personality.

Shankara describes this problem in terms of superimposition: characteristics belonging to one thing are mistakenly attributed to another. Through this fundamental confusion, the individual experiences the self as limited and separate.

What Did Shankaracharya Teach About Knowledge?

For Shankara, knowledge is central to liberation. The problem of bondage arises from ignorance, so liberation requires the removal of that ignorance through knowledge of reality.

This knowledge is not merely intellectual information about Brahman. It involves a transformative recognition of one's true nature.

Shankara emphasizes a process involving listening to the teachings, reflecting upon them, and deeply contemplating their meaning. The prepared student develops the intellectual and ethical conditions necessary for this realization.

What Is Moksha in Shankaracharya's Philosophy?

Moksha, or liberation, is the ultimate goal of Advaita Vedanta.

Shankara understands liberation primarily in terms of knowledge. The realization of the self as Brahman removes the fundamental ignorance that produces mistaken identification and bondage.

Liberation is therefore not simply reaching a particular physical place or acquiring an extraordinary experience. It involves the recognition of what the self fundamentally is.

In this sense, Advaita Vedanta connects metaphysics, epistemology, and soteriology: understanding ultimate reality is directly connected with freedom from ignorance and suffering.

Shankaracharya on Consciousness and the Self

Consciousness occupies a central position in Shankara's philosophy. The deepest self is understood not as an object that consciousness observes but as the fundamental consciousness through which experience is possible.

Shankara argues that consciousness is not dependent upon particular objects in the way ordinary mental experiences are. The changing contents of experience come and go, while consciousness is understood as their underlying witness.

This account became particularly important in Advaita discussions of waking, dreaming, and deep sleep. Shankara uses these states to investigate the relationship between consciousness, the mind, and objects of experience.

Shankaracharya and the Upanishads

The Upanishads are fundamental sources for Shankara's philosophy. His interpretation of the Upanishads seeks to establish non-dual Brahman as their central philosophical teaching.

Shankara did not treat the Upanishads as isolated philosophical statements. He developed systematic interpretations intended to reconcile apparently different passages and explain their underlying philosophical meaning.

His commentaries became highly influential in the later development of Vedanta and helped establish a rigorous philosophical framework for interpreting the Upanishadic teachings.

Shankaracharya's Philosophy and the Bhagavad Gita

Shankara also wrote an influential commentary on the Bhagavad Gita. His interpretation connects the teachings of the Gita with the broader philosophical framework of Advaita Vedanta.

In his interpretation, knowledge of the self and Brahman occupies a central place, while disciplined action and other spiritual practices can prepare a person for liberating knowledge.

The Gita therefore became one of the major texts through which Shankara articulated his interpretation of Vedantic philosophy.

Shankaracharya and the Brahma Sutras

Another major work associated with Shankara is his commentary on the Brahma Sutras.

The Brahma Sutras attempt to organize and interpret philosophical teachings associated with the Upanishads. Shankara's commentary became one of the most important classical texts for Advaita Vedanta.

Together with the Upanishads and the Bhagavad Gita, the Brahma Sutras form the traditional Prasthanatrayi, or "triple canon," of Vedanta. Shankara's commentaries on these sources were central to his project of systematizing Advaita Vedanta.

What Did Adi Shankaracharya Write?

Shankara is traditionally credited with a substantial body of philosophical and devotional literature. However, determining the authorship of every work attributed to him is a complex scholarly question.

Major Philosophical Commentaries

  • Commentary on the Brahma Sutras
  • Commentary on the Bhagavad Gita
  • Commentaries on major Upanishads

These commentaries are particularly important because they provide direct evidence for Shankara's philosophical interpretation and his method of engaging with earlier Indian philosophical texts.

Other Works Attributed to Shankara

Numerous independent philosophical and devotional works have traditionally been attributed to Shankara. The authorship of some of these texts remains debated among scholars.

Shankaracharya's Method of Philosophical Inquiry

Shankara's philosophical method combines close interpretation of authoritative texts with rigorous reasoning. He engages extensively with rival philosophical schools and develops arguments in defense of his interpretation of Vedanta.

However, Shankara does not believe that ultimate non-duality can be established through independent logical reasoning alone. The Upanishadic texts provide the authoritative knowledge concerning Brahman, while reasoning helps clarify and defend that teaching.

This combination of scriptural interpretation and philosophical argument became a defining feature of classical Advaita Vedanta.

What Are the Qualifications for Knowledge in Advaita Vedanta?

Shankara emphasizes that philosophical knowledge requires a suitably prepared mind. The student must develop qualities that allow the teachings to be understood properly.

  • Discrimination — distinguishing the enduring from the transient.
  • Dispassion — reducing attachment to temporary objects of enjoyment.
  • Discipline — developing control of the mind and senses and cultivating appropriate virtues.
  • Desire for liberation — possessing a genuine aspiration for moksha.

These qualifications are part of Shankara's broader account of how a person becomes prepared for liberating knowledge.

Did Shankaracharya Reject Meditation and Religious Practice?

It would be inaccurate to describe Shankara as simply rejecting meditation, devotion, ethics, or disciplined action.

In his philosophical system, practices such as meditation, ethical discipline, devotion, and appropriate action can help prepare the mind for knowledge. However, Shankara gives liberating knowledge a uniquely important role because ignorance is ultimately removed through knowledge of the self as Brahman.

This distinction between preparatory practices and liberating knowledge is an important feature of Shankara's Advaita Vedanta.

What Does Non-Dualism Mean in Shankaracharya's Philosophy?

Non-dualism means that ultimate reality is not composed of two or more independent realities.

Shankara's Advaita Vedanta maintains that Brahman is ultimately non-dual. The apparent separation between individual selves and the ultimate reality of Brahman belongs to ordinary empirical experience, not to the highest level of reality.

This philosophical position is why Advaita Vedanta is often described as one of the most influential forms of philosophical non-dualism in Indian thought.

Shankaracharya and Other Indian Philosophical Schools

Shankara's philosophy developed within a highly competitive intellectual environment. Indian philosophers debated questions about reality, knowledge, perception, language, consciousness, God, the self, and liberation.

Advaita Vedanta was challenged by several other philosophical traditions, including Buddhist, Nyaya, Mimamsa, and other Vedanta thinkers.

Later Vedanta philosophers developed alternative interpretations of the relationship between Brahman, individual selves, and the world. Ramanuja, for example, developed Vishishtadvaita Vedanta, while Madhva developed Dvaita Vedanta.

Shankaracharya and Ramanuja: Advaita vs Vishishtadvaita

The philosophical relationship between Shankaracharya and Ramanuja is important in the history of Vedanta.

Shankara's Advaita Vedanta emphasizes the ultimate non-duality of Brahman and the identity of Atman with Brahman. Ramanuja's Vishishtadvaita Vedanta also affirms the unity of ultimate reality but interprets the relationship between God, individual selves, and the world differently.

Their disagreement illustrates the diversity of Vedanta philosophy and shows that Indian philosophical traditions developed through sustained debate rather than through a single universally accepted doctrine.

Shankaracharya and the Advaita Monastic Tradition

Shankara is traditionally associated with the development of monastic institutions and renunciant lineages connected with Advaita Vedanta.

Later traditions connect him with major monasteries, or mathas, across India and with the Dasanami order of renunciants.

The historical details concerning the establishment of particular institutions are difficult to verify in every case. Nevertheless, Shankara's name became deeply connected with monastic Advaita traditions that continue to exist today.

What Do We Actually Know About Adi Shankaracharya?

The historical study of Shankara requires separating philosophical writings from later biographical traditions.

Relatively Well-Attested

  • Shankara was a major philosopher associated with Advaita Vedanta.
  • His works include important commentaries on Vedantic philosophical texts.
  • His philosophy emphasizes the non-duality of Brahman and the ultimate identity of Atman and Brahman.
  • His philosophical writings had a major influence on later Advaita Vedanta.

Traditionally Attributed

  • Extensive journeys throughout India.
  • Debates with numerous philosophical opponents.
  • Establishment of particular monasteries across India.
  • Many devotional and philosophical works beyond his major commentaries.

Historically Uncertain

  • The exact dates of Shankara's birth and death.
  • Many details found in later hagiographical biographies.
  • The precise historical circumstances surrounding every monastery traditionally attributed to him.
  • The authorship of every work later attributed to Shankara.

Why Is Adi Shankaracharya Important?

Shankara is important because he provided one of the most influential philosophical interpretations of the Upanishadic tradition.

His Advaita Vedanta developed a sophisticated account of reality, consciousness, selfhood, knowledge, ignorance, and liberation.

His arguments influenced centuries of Indian philosophical debate and became foundational for later Advaita thinkers. His work also remains influential in contemporary discussions of consciousness and non-duality.

Adi Shankaracharya's Main Philosophical Ideas

Shankara's philosophy can be understood through several interconnected ideas.

Brahman as Ultimate Reality

Brahman is the ultimate reality and is understood as non-dual.

Atman and Brahman

The deepest self, Atman, is ultimately identical with Brahman.

Advaita or Non-Duality

Ultimate reality is not divided into independent, equally fundamental realities.

Ignorance and Superimposition

Human bondage results from ignorance and mistaken identification of the self with what is not ultimately the self.

Knowledge and Liberation

Liberating knowledge removes ignorance and reveals the true nature of the self.

Moksha

Liberation is the realization of the self's true nature as Brahman.

Adi Shankaracharya Explained Simply

Shankara's central philosophical question was: what is ultimately real, and what is the true nature of the self?

His answer, in Advaita Vedanta, is that ultimate reality is Brahman and that the deepest self, Atman, is not ultimately separate from Brahman.

  • Ultimate reality: Brahman.
  • True self: Atman.
  • Relationship: Atman is ultimately identical with Brahman.
  • Problem: Ignorance and mistaken identification.
  • Path to liberation: Knowledge of the true self and reality.
  • Goal: Moksha, or liberation.

Frequently Asked Questions About Adi Shankaracharya

Who was Adi Shankaracharya?

Adi Shankaracharya, also known as Adi Shankara, was a major Indian philosopher associated with Advaita Vedanta, a philosophical tradition centered on non-duality and the ultimate reality of Brahman.

What is Adi Shankaracharya famous for?

Shankara is famous for systematizing Advaita Vedanta and for his influential commentaries on the Upanishads, Bhagavad Gita, and Brahma Sutras.

What is Advaita Vedanta?

Advaita Vedanta is a non-dual philosophical tradition of Vedanta that teaches that ultimate reality is Brahman and that the deepest self, Atman, is ultimately identical with Brahman.

What did Shankaracharya believe about Brahman?

Shankara regarded Brahman as ultimate reality and interpreted the Upanishads as teaching the fundamental non-duality of Brahman.

What did Shankaracharya say about Atman?

In Advaita Vedanta, Shankara identifies the deepest self, Atman, with Brahman rather than treating the true self as an independently existing individual substance.

What is Maya according to Shankaracharya?

Maya is associated with the appearance of plurality and the conditions through which the empirical world is experienced. Shankara's philosophy should not be reduced to the simple claim that the world does not exist.

What is Moksha in Advaita Vedanta?

Moksha is liberation. In Shankara's philosophy, liberation is closely connected with knowledge that removes ignorance and reveals the true nature of the self as Brahman.

Did Shankaracharya write the Bhagavad Gita?

No. The Bhagavad Gita predates Shankara. Shankara wrote an influential commentary on the Bhagavad Gita.

Did Shankaracharya found Advaita Vedanta?

Shankara is more accurately described as a major systematizer of Advaita Vedanta rather than its absolute founder. Earlier thinkers, particularly Gaudapada, contributed to the development of the philosophical tradition.

When did Adi Shankaracharya live?

Shankara is generally placed around the early medieval period, often in the eighth century CE. However, his exact dates are disputed among scholars.

Why is Shankaracharya important in Indian philosophy?

Shankara is important because his systematic interpretation of Vedanta became one of the most influential philosophical traditions in Indian intellectual history.

What is the difference between Shankaracharya and Ramanuja?

Shankara's Advaita Vedanta teaches ultimate non-duality and identifies Atman with Brahman, while Ramanuja's Vishishtadvaita Vedanta gives a different account of the relationship between Brahman, individual selves, and the world.

Why Adi Shankaracharya Still Matters

Adi Shankaracharya remains one of the most important figures in the history of Indian philosophy because of the depth and influence of his interpretation of Advaita Vedanta.

His philosophical investigation of Brahman, Atman, consciousness, ignorance, knowledge, and liberation created a powerful framework for thinking about the relationship between the self and ultimate reality.

Whether studied as a philosopher of metaphysics, consciousness, epistemology, or liberation, Shankara's work continues to occupy a central position in discussions of classical Indian philosophy and non-dualism.
