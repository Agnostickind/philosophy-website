Rumi vs Ibn Arabi: Poetry and Metaphysics Compared

Two giants of medieval Sufism: one taught in Persian verse, the other in dense Arabic metaphysical prose.

By Kavya Jain · Published 2026-10-04 · Updated 2026-10-04 · Part of our complete guide: Rumi: Life, Philosophy & Masnavi Explained

Short answerRumi (1207–1273) and Ibn Arabi (1165–1240) both teach that creation manifests the divine and that love and the "perfect human" lead back to God. The difference is method: Ibn Arabi built a systematic metaphysics, mostly in Arabic prose, while Rumi taught through Persian poetry, story and the practice of love. Rumi vs Ibn Arabi at a glance

Rumi Ibn Arabi
Lived 1207–1273 1165–1240
Born / died Balkh / Konya Murcia (al-Andalus) / Damascus
Historical setting Thirteenth-century Persianate Islamic world; later life centered in Konya Twelfth- and thirteenth-century Islamic world; extensive travels across the Middle East and North Africa
Language and form Primarily Persian poetry, narrative, parable, lyric, and teaching Primarily Arabic prose and metaphysical exposition, with some poetry
Key works Masnavi-ye Ma'navi; Diwan-e Shams-e Tabrizi; Fihi Ma Fih Fusus al-Hikam; al-Futuhat al-Makkiyya
Central themes Divine love (ishq), spiritual transformation, longing, union, remembrance, and the relationship between lover and Beloved Divine names and attributes, existence, manifestation, the barzakh, spiritual knowledge, imagination, and the Perfect Human (al-insan al-kamil)
Approach to divine unity Expresses spiritual unity primarily through love, poetry, paradox, music, and transformation of the self Develops a systematic metaphysical account of the relationship between God, creation, divine names, and manifestation; later tradition strongly associated him with wahdat al-wujud
Fana and baqa Spiritual transformation involving the overcoming of the ego and continued life in God Discusses related transformations of the self through his broader metaphysics of spiritual realization and divine knowledge
Role of love Divine love is one of the most prominent vehicles of spiritual transformation Love is deeply connected with divine self-disclosure, knowledge, and the relationship between God and creation
Method Story, metaphor, lyric poetry, parable, dialogue, and lived spiritual practice Technical metaphysical vocabulary, scriptural interpretation, spiritual commentary, philosophical argument, and symbolic exposition
Important spiritual relationship His relationship with Shams of Tabriz profoundly shaped his later poetry and spiritual expression His writings draw on an extensive tradition of Islamic theology, Sufism, philosophy, and spiritual practice
Major influence Persian literature, Sufi poetry, devotional culture, and the Mevlevi tradition Sufi metaphysics, Islamic philosophy, Qur'anic interpretation, theology, and later traditions of philosophical Sufism
Institutional legacy The Mevlevi Order, established by his followers around his teachings and spiritual legacy A wide-ranging intellectual and spiritual legacy rather than a single order directly founded by him
Best known for Making profound Sufi ideas accessible through poetry, stories, images, and the language of divine love Developing one of the most influential and sophisticated metaphysical systems in the history of Sufi thought

In summary, Rumi and Ibn Arabi share a concern with spiritual transformation, divine reality, and the relationship between the human being and God, but they express these concerns in markedly different ways. Rumi is best approached through poetry, story, longing, love, and the transformation of the self, whereas Ibn Arabi is especially important for his systematic exploration of existence, divine names, manifestation, spiritual knowledge, and the Perfect Human. Their thought should not be treated as identical: Rumi's poetic language can express ideas that resemble themes found in Ibn Arabi, but Rumi was not simply a poetic version of Ibn Arabi's metaphysical system. Ibn Arabi was also older by more than forty years, and although later Sufi traditions brought their thought into close conversation, it is important not to assume a direct personal intellectual relationship between the two. The strongest comparison is therefore one of convergence and difference: both explore the transformation of ordinary consciousness and the relationship between the human and the divine, while Rumi tends to make love and poetic experience the dominant language of that journey and Ibn Arabi develops a more explicitly metaphysical framework for understanding it.

What Rumi and Ibn Arabi share

Both read the world as a theatre of divine self-disclosure and both place the human being at its centre. Both also treat love as a mode of knowing that goes beyond argument, and both draw on the Quran and earlier Sufi tradition. Many themes, such as longing for the source, the dissolving of the ego and the unity behind multiplicity, appear in both.

Where they differ: system versus song

Ibn Arabi defines terms, distinguishes levels of reality and argues across long prose works. Rumi rarely defines; he tells stories, shifts voice and lets images carry the teaching. The phrase "unity of being" (wahdat al-wujud) is usually linked to Ibn Arabi's school, although later followers popularized it as a label. For Rumi the driving force is love, and the goal is lived transformation rather than a finished doctrine.

Was there a historical link?

Stories that the young Rumi met Ibn Arabi in Damascus are doubtful. The better-documented link is Sadr al-Din al-Qunawi, Ibn Arabi's leading disciple, who lived in Konya in Rumi's time, and tradition describes contact between him and Rumi's circle. This gave Ibn Arabi's ideas a route into the world Rumi inhabited.

How scholars read the two together

Some scholars, notably William Chittick, interpret Rumi's poetry with the vocabulary of Ibn Arabi's school. Others caution against treating Rumi as a systematic follower, because his main concern is love and experience rather than technical metaphysics. Both views are useful; the safest approach is to read Rumi first as a poet and then bring in Ibn Arabi for comparison. For another contrast see Suhrawardi and the comparison table in our Rumi guide.

Frequently asked questions

Did Rumi meet Ibn Arabi?

Tradition says they may have met in Damascus, but this is doubtful. The documented connection runs through Sadr al-Din al-Qunawi, Ibn Arabi's disciple, who lived in Konya.

Was Rumi influenced by Ibn Arabi?

Scholars disagree. They share many mystical themes, and Ibn Arabi's school was present in Konya, but Rumi did not write in a systematic metaphysical style.

Who is easier to read, Rumi or Ibn Arabi?

Rumi, because he teaches through stories and images. Ibn Arabi's prose assumes knowledge of a technical vocabulary.
