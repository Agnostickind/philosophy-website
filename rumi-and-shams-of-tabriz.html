Rumi and Shams of Tabriz: The Story Explained

The meeting that turned a respected jurist into an ecstatic poet, and the disappearance that shaped his greatest verse.

Short answerShams of Tabriz was a wandering Sufi whom Rumi met in Konya in 1244. Their intense spiritual companionship (suhbat) turned Rumi from a respected jurist into an ecstatic poet. Shams disappeared around 1247, and later tradition says he was killed. Rumi's grief produced the Diwan-e Shams-e Tabrizi. See the full Rumi guide.

Who was Shams of Tabriz?

Shams al-Din Muhammad of Tabriz, usually known as Shams of Tabriz, was a thirteenth-century Sufi mystic whose encounter with Jalal al-Din Rumi became one of the most important relationships in the history of Persian mystical literature. Shams appears to have lived as a wandering dervish rather than as the head of an established Sufi institution. Accounts associated with him portray a man who was intensely concerned with direct spiritual experience and who could be deliberately confrontational toward conventional religious learning. His questions and conversations were intended to challenge intellectual pride and redirect attention from second-hand knowledge toward an immediate awareness of God. Unlike Rumi, who had already established himself as a respected religious scholar in Konya, Shams did not leave behind a large systematic philosophical or theological work.

Much of what can be reconstructed about Shams comes indirectly through sources connected with Rumi and through later Sufi biographical literature. The Maqalat, or "Discourses" attributed to Shams, preserves conversations, reflections, criticisms, and recollections associated with him, although its textual history requires careful handling. William C. Chittick's translation, Me and Rumi, has made this material accessible to modern English-language readers. Later works such as Shams al-Din Aflaki's Manaqib al-Arifin provide many stories about Shams and Rumi, but Aflaki was writing considerably later and within a devotional environment that naturally emphasized extraordinary events and spiritual significance. Consequently, Shams is historically important but biographically difficult to reconstruct. The strongest historical point is not that every story told about him is reliable, but that his encounter with Rumi had a profound and demonstrable effect on Rumi's intellectual, spiritual, and poetic life.

What was Shams to Rumi?

Shams is often described simply as Rumi's "teacher," but that description can be misleading if it suggests a conventional classroom relationship. Rumi was already an accomplished Islamic jurist, preacher, and religious scholar before meeting Shams. The significance of Shams was therefore not that he introduced Rumi to religion for the first time. Rather, their relationship appears to have transformed the way Rumi experienced and expressed his existing religious and mystical concerns. Shams challenged the boundaries between formal learning and direct spiritual experience, encouraging an approach in which religious knowledge had to become personally transformative rather than remaining merely intellectual.

The relationship was consequently unusual even within the broader world of Sufi companionship. Shams became a catalyst for Rumi's intense poetic production, while Rumi became the person through whom Shams's memory acquired an enormous literary afterlife. Rumi's later writings repeatedly invoke Shams not merely as a remembered historical companion but as a spiritual presence and symbol. This is why descriptions of Shams as "Rumi's friend," "teacher," "spiritual companion," or "mystical catalyst" each capture part of the relationship but none completely defines it. Later readers have also interpreted the relationship through modern ideas of friendship, mentorship, or romantic love. Those interpretations can be interesting, but they should not automatically be projected onto a thirteenth-century Sufi relationship without considering its religious and literary context.

Timeline of the relationship between Shams and Rumi

The chronology of Shams and Rumi's relationship is relatively compact but historically significant. The two are traditionally said to have met in Konya in 1244, when Rumi was already a prominent religious scholar and teacher. Their encounter was followed by an unusually intense period of companionship. Rumi increasingly withdrew from some of his ordinary public activities, while his relationship with Shams became a source of concern and resentment among people around him. Shams subsequently left Konya, returned after efforts by Rumi's family and companions to reconcile the two, and then disappeared permanently around 1247.

Exact dates and details should be treated cautiously because the surviving narratives were written down after the events and sometimes combine historical recollection with devotional storytelling. The broad sequence is much more secure than the precise circumstances of each episode: meeting, intense companionship, separation, return, renewed conflict, and final disappearance. This distinction is important when reading popular accounts of Shams. A detailed story about a particular conversation or confrontation may come from a later literary source rather than from a contemporary record.

Major stages in the relationship between Shams of Tabriz and Rumi
When Event Historical significance
1244 Shams meets Rumi in Konya and the two begin an unusually intense period of companionship. The encounter becomes the turning point associated with Rumi's later mystical and poetic transformation.
c. 1246 Shams leaves Konya amid growing tension surrounding his relationship with Rumi. The first separation reveals the intensity of the relationship and the opposition it generated within Rumi's social circle.
c. 1246 Rumi's son Sultan Walad travels to persuade Shams to return. Shams returns to Konya, demonstrating that the separation was not initially permanent.
c. 1247 Shams disappears permanently from Rumi's life. The disappearance becomes a central event in Rumi's later poetry and in the development of the Shams tradition.

The timeline is important because it shows that Shams's historical importance cannot be separated from the sequence of presence and absence that followed his meeting with Rumi. His first departure did not end the relationship; Rumi's family and companions were involved in bringing him back. The second disappearance, however, became permanent. Rumi's response was not simply to preserve memories of a lost friend. His poetry repeatedly turned absence itself into a spiritual experience. Shams could therefore appear in Rumi's poetry as both a remembered individual and a symbol of the beloved whose disappearance intensified longing for the divine.

It is also useful to distinguish chronology from legend. Sources written later can provide valuable evidence about how the Rumi community remembered Shams, but they do not necessarily provide a verbatim record of what happened in 1244 or 1247. For historical purposes, the safest approach is to accept the broad chronology while marking disputed details as traditional or uncertain. This is especially important for claims about Shams's final days, the identities of people supposedly responsible for his disappearance, and the exact locations associated with his burial.

What happened to Shams of Tabriz?

Shams's final disappearance is one of the greatest unresolved questions in the history of Rumi. The traditional accounts generally connect his disappearance with hostility toward his close relationship with Rumi. Some later narratives claim that members of Rumi's circle or household became so jealous of Shams's influence that they arranged or participated in his killing. These stories became an important part of the later hagiographical tradition, but they should not be treated as a securely documented murder case. The surviving accounts were produced after the events and differ in their details, making it difficult to establish exactly what happened.

Several traditions also associate Shams with different places of burial or disappearance, including Konya and Khoy in present-day Iran. These competing traditions illustrate the distinction between historical certainty and sacred memory. What can be stated with considerably greater confidence is that Shams disappeared from Rumi's life around 1247 and did not return. Rumi's subsequent writings provide powerful evidence for the emotional and spiritual consequences of that absence, but poetry cannot by itself establish the precise circumstances of Shams's death. The most responsible historical conclusion is therefore that Shams disappeared under circumstances that remain uncertain, while the murder narrative belongs primarily to later tradition.

What did Shams teach Rumi?

Shams did not leave behind a philosophical system comparable to the systematic works of major Islamic philosophers. His importance lies more in his emphasis on lived spiritual experience than in a formal doctrine. The sayings and conversations associated with him repeatedly criticize religious learning when it becomes an end in itself. For Shams, knowledge was valuable when it transformed the person who possessed it. This helps explain why his encounter with Rumi was so significant: Rumi was already deeply learned in Islamic law and theology, but his relationship with Shams encouraged a much more intense poetic and mystical expression of ideas about divine love, longing, presence, and spiritual transformation.

It would therefore be too simple to say that Shams "taught Rumi Sufism." Rumi had already encountered Sufi thought and practice before their meeting. A better description is that Shams became a catalyst who challenged Rumi to embody and experience ideas that had previously existed within his religious and intellectual life. This distinction also helps explain why Rumi's later poetry cannot simply be treated as a transcript of Shams's teachings. Rumi transformed the experience of their relationship into an enormous poetic body of work, while Shams's own voice survives in a much smaller and more difficult textual tradition.

How Shams changed Rumi's poetry

The most visible consequence of Shams's presence in Rumi's life was the extraordinary transformation of Rumi's literary activity. Rumi had been known primarily as a religious scholar and teacher, but after his encounter with Shams he produced an enormous amount of mystical poetry. The Divan-e Shams-e Tabrizi, also known as the Divan-e Kabir, contains lyric poems in which Shams's name appears repeatedly. Rumi could address Shams directly, identify himself with Shams, or use Shams's name as a poetic signature. This does not mean that every poem in the collection should be read as a straightforward biographical account of their relationship. Shams became part of Rumi's symbolic and mystical language.

After Shams's disappearance, absence became one of the central emotional structures of Rumi's poetry. Longing for the absent beloved could express longing for Shams, but it could also become a way of expressing the soul's longing for God. In this sense, Shams occupies an unusual position between historical person and mystical symbol. Rumi's poetry gradually transforms personal loss into a broader account of separation, love, longing, and reunion with the divine. This is one reason Shams remains essential for understanding Rumi: the relationship did not merely provide material for a biography; it became part of the vocabulary through which Rumi expressed mystical experience. See also Rumi's philosophy of divine love.

How reliable are the stories about Shams?

Stories about Shams should be read on more than one level. Some sources preserve valuable memories of a historical Sufi who met Rumi in Konya and profoundly influenced him. Other accounts belong to the later hagiographical tradition, where the purpose was not simply to reconstruct events but to demonstrate the extraordinary spiritual status of saints and mystics. A miraculous story can therefore be historically uncertain while still being important evidence for how later Sufis understood Shams and Rumi.

A useful distinction is between historical evidence, later tradition, and interpretation. The meeting of Shams and Rumi, the importance of their companionship, the later disappearance of Shams, and Rumi's enormous poetic response are central features of the historical tradition. Precise conversations, dramatic confrontations, miraculous episodes, and detailed accounts of Shams's death require more caution. Modern scholarship therefore tends to preserve the significance of the relationship while resisting the temptation to turn every later story into a verified biographical fact.

Why is Shams of Tabriz important?

Shams is important not because he left behind a large philosophical system, but because of the transformation associated with his relationship with Rumi. His appearance in Rumi's life coincided with a dramatic change in Rumi's literary expression, and his disappearance became one of the deepest sources of longing in Rumi's poetry. Through Rumi, Shams became one of the most recognizable figures in the history of Persian Sufism, even though comparatively little can be established about his life with the same confidence available for Rumi.

His legacy also raises an important question about how intellectual and spiritual influence works. A person does not necessarily need to leave behind a systematic treatise to transform another thinker. Shams's significance lies partly in conversation, companionship, provocation, and the changes those experiences produced in Rumi. For readers studying Rumi, understanding Shams therefore provides a bridge between Rumi's biography and his poetry: the historical encounter helps explain why themes of longing, absence, divine love, and the beloved become so powerful in Rumi's later work.

Frequently asked questions

Who was Shams of Tabriz to Rumi?

Shams was a wandering Sufi mystic whose intense companionship with Rumi became a major turning point in Rumi's spiritual and poetic life. Calling him simply Rumi's teacher is incomplete because Rumi was already an accomplished religious scholar before their meeting.

Did Shams of Tabriz teach Rumi Sufism?

Not in the sense of introducing Rumi to Sufism for the first time. Rumi was already familiar with Islamic theology, law, and Sufi thought. Shams appears to have acted more as a spiritual catalyst, challenging Rumi to experience and express religious knowledge more directly and intensely.

Did Rumi's followers kill Shams?

Later traditions claim that Shams was killed by people hostile to his relationship with Rumi, but the evidence is not strong enough to establish the precise circumstances of his disappearance with certainty.

Why did Shams disappear?

The exact reason for Shams's final disappearance is unknown. Sources describe tensions surrounding his relationship with Rumi, but the detailed accounts of what happened afterward are late and sometimes contradictory.

Why is Rumi's Divan named after Shams?

The Divan-e Shams-e Tabrizi contains many poems in which Rumi invokes Shams directly or uses his name as a poetic signature. Shams thus became both the historical beloved addressed in the poems and a powerful mystical symbol within Rumi's poetry.

Was Shams of Tabriz a philosopher?

Shams is better described as a Sufi mystic and spiritual thinker than as a systematic philosopher. The material associated with him consists largely of conversations, sayings, reflections, and criticisms rather than a structured philosophical system.
