Nihilism vs Existentialism

Nihilism and existentialism are two philosophical approaches that confront fundamental questions about human existence, meaning, value and the place of individuals in the world. Nihilism questions whether life possesses an inherent purpose or whether objective values have any ultimate foundation. Existentialism, by contrast, investigates how human beings exist, choose, take responsibility and give direction to their lives. Although the two traditions overlap in their rejection or examination of inherited certainties, existentialism does not necessarily conclude that nothing matters. Its central concern is what it means to live and act when existence cannot simply be explained by predetermined answers. [1]

Difference Between Nihilism and Existentialism?

The main difference between nihilism and existentialism concerns what follows when traditional explanations of life, morality and purpose are questioned. Nihilism is generally associated with the denial or rejection of meaning, objective value or other foundations within a particular domain. Existentialism is a philosophical tradition concerned with the conditions of human existence and the ways individuals confront freedom, uncertainty, choice and responsibility.

The distinction becomes clearer when the two are treated as different responses to a shared problem. If the universe does not provide human beings with an obvious purpose, does that mean life is ultimately meaningless? Or does the absence of a predetermined purpose make individual choice and responsibility more important? Nihilism and existentialism approach these questions differently, although neither term represents one completely uniform doctrine. [2]

A particularly important distinction is between existential nihilism and existentialism. Existential nihilism holds that human life has no inherent or objective meaning. Existentialism, however, includes philosophical positions that examine how meaning, identity and responsibility emerge through human existence. Some existentialists accept that meaning is not predetermined, but they do not necessarily conclude that human projects are worthless.

This comparison therefore requires more than placing two labels against one another. Their historical origins, philosophical assumptions and individual thinkers must also be considered.

What Is Nihilism and Existentialism?

What Is Nihilism?

Nihilism is a family of philosophical positions that deny, reject or radically question particular foundations of human thought and experience. Depending on the position being discussed, nihilism may concern the existence of objective moral values, the possibility of knowledge, the inherent meaning of life or the fundamental composition of reality. The term comes from the Latin nihil, meaning nothing, but it does not describe one universal argument that everything is literally nonexistent. [3]

Existential nihilism is especially relevant to this comparison. It holds that human existence has no inherent, universal or objective purpose. The universe does not necessarily provide a final explanation for why human beings exist, and life may have no meaning built into it independently of human interpretation or commitment.

This position should not automatically be confused with depression, pessimism or a personal feeling of hopelessness. A philosophical argument about objective meaning is different from an individual's emotional experience. Nor does denying objective meaning logically establish that people cannot care about relationships, activities or personal goals.

What Is Existentialism?

Existentialism is a philosophical movement and broader intellectual tradition concerned with human existence, individuality, freedom, anxiety, responsibility, authenticity and the search for meaning. It developed through different thinkers who did not always share the same religious, metaphysical or ethical commitments.[a]

Many existentialist philosophies begin with the concrete individual rather than an abstract definition of human nature. They examine what it means to exist in a particular situation, to make choices under uncertainty and to accept responsibility for the lives those choices shape. For some thinkers, meaning is not a ready-made feature of the universe but something connected with human projects and commitments. [4]

Existentialism is not necessarily atheistic. Søren Kierkegaard developed a deeply religious philosophy of individual existence, faith and commitment, while Jean-Paul Sartre defended an explicitly atheistic existentialism. Their differences demonstrate why existentialism should not be reduced to one fixed belief about God, morality or meaning.

Nihilism vs Existentialism: Key Differences

The most useful way to compare nihilism and existentialism is to examine the questions each addresses and the conclusions different philosophers draw. The following table presents broad tendencies rather than universal rules, since both traditions contain significant internal disagreements.

Philosophical Question Nihilism Existentialism
Central concern Whether meaning, value, knowledge or other foundations exist in an objective sense. What it means to exist as an individual and how one should live, choose and take responsibility.
Meaning of life Existential nihilism denies that life has inherent or objective meaning. Some forms understand meaning as emerging through choices, projects, relationships or commitments.
Human freedom Not a defining commitment shared by every form of nihilism. A major concern, particularly in the philosophies of Sartre and Beauvoir.
Responsibility Depends on the form of nihilism and its implications for value and obligation. Often central to the question of how individuals should live and act.
Morality Moral nihilism denies objective moral facts or certain foundations of moral judgment. Existentialist ethics examines responsibility, freedom, authenticity and relationships with others.
God and religion Some forms challenge religious or metaphysical foundations, but nihilism is not one uniform position on religion. Includes both religious and atheistic approaches.
Human existence May be understood as lacking inherent purpose or ultimate justification. Examines the lived experience of being human, including uncertainty, anxiety and choice.
Possible response Recognition or acceptance of the absence of objective foundations. Commitment, choice, authenticity, responsibility or revolt, depending on the thinker.
Associated thinkers Nietzsche as a diagnostician of nihilism, along with writers and philosophers who examine its different forms. Kierkegaard, Sartre, Beauvoir, Heidegger and other thinkers associated with existential philosophy.

The distinction is not that nihilism is always pessimistic while existentialism is always optimistic. Existentialism can be deeply concerned with anxiety, death, alienation and the absence of ultimate certainty. Its difference lies in the philosophical questions it asks and the responses it develops, not simply in its emotional tone.

The Question of Meaning: Does Life Have a Purpose?

The question of meaning is where nihilism and existentialism come into their closest contact. Both confront the possibility that the universe does not provide an obvious, predetermined purpose for human life. Their philosophical approaches, however, are not identical.

Meaning from the Perspective of Nihilism

Existential nihilism argues that life has no inherent or objective meaning. Human beings may search for a universal purpose, but the existence of such a purpose cannot simply be assumed. The universe does not necessarily contain an objective explanation of why individuals exist or what their lives are ultimately for.

The conclusion concerns the status of meaning itself. If a person argues that life has no meaning independently of human beliefs, that does not automatically prove that every experience is worthless. It instead raises a further question: whether subjective meaning, personal attachment and human commitment can count as genuine meaning when they lack an objective foundation.[b]

Meaning from the Perspective of Existentialism

Existentialist approaches often shift attention from discovering a universal purpose to examining the ways individuals live and make commitments. In Sartre's existentialism, human beings are not born with a fixed essence that completely determines who they must become. His famous formulation that existence precedes essence expresses the idea that individuals first exist and subsequently shape themselves through their actions and choices. [5]

This does not mean that people can invent themselves without limitations. Human beings live within particular circumstances, relationships, histories and material conditions. Their freedom is exercised within situations they did not entirely choose. Existentialist philosophy therefore examines the tension between freedom and facticity: the conditions and facts that shape an individual's situation.

Meaning may consequently arise through the projects people pursue, the responsibilities they accept and the relationships they sustain. Whether this constitutes a sufficient answer to the problem of objective meaning remains a philosophical question rather than an uncontested conclusion.

Freedom, Choice and Responsibility

Freedom is one of the defining concerns of existentialist philosophy. It is particularly important in Sartre's account of human existence, where individuals cannot completely escape the responsibility involved in choosing how to act. Even refusing to make a decision can become a way of responding to a situation.

Freedom in Existentialism

Sartre argues that human beings are responsible for the meanings and values they establish through their choices. His account of freedom is demanding because it does not allow individuals to explain every action through a fixed human nature or predetermined essence. People are shaped by their circumstances, but they also participate in shaping who they become.

This responsibility is connected with Sartre's concept of anguish. Recognising freedom means recognising that choices are not merely mechanical responses to external instructions. Individuals must confront the uncertainty and consequences of their decisions. [6]

Freedom in Relation to Nihilism

Nihilism does not offer one universal theory of freedom. Its different forms address different questions. A person who rejects objective moral values, for example, has not thereby established that human beings possess unlimited freedom or that every action is equally defensible.

Nevertheless, the absence of an objective purpose can raise questions about how people should direct their lives. If no universal goal is prescribed, individuals may have to examine the reasons behind their commitments. This is one point at which nihilistic questions can become the starting point for existentialist reflection.

Morality and Values: Are Right and Wrong Objective?

Another important difference concerns morality. Nihilism can challenge the existence of objective moral facts, while existentialist philosophers examine how individuals should understand their responsibilities and relationships when inherited moral systems are questioned.

Moral Nihilism

Moral nihilism is the position that objective moral facts or values do not exist. One influential version is moral error theory, defended by J. L. Mackie. According to this position, ordinary moral judgments presuppose objective values, but the world contains no such values. Consequently, moral claims that attempt to describe objective moral facts systematically fail. [7]

Moral nihilism should not be confused with the simple belief that people should behave selfishly or that cruelty is desirable. Denying objective moral facts does not automatically settle questions about social cooperation, human suffering, personal commitments or the practical reasons people have for acting.

Existentialist ethics approaches morality through freedom, responsibility and the concrete circumstances of human life. Sartre's philosophy connects moral responsibility with the recognition that choices help shape the values through which people live.

Simone de Beauvoir develops an important relational dimension of existentialist ethics. In The Ethics of Ambiguity, she examines freedom in relation to other people and argues against understanding individual freedom as something isolated from the freedom of others. [8]

Existentialist ethics is not a single moral theory. Its thinkers disagree about the grounds and structure of ethical obligation. Nevertheless, responsibility and the situation of concrete individuals remain central concerns.

Historical Development of Nihilism and Existentialism

Nihilism and existentialism did not emerge as two perfectly separate movements at the same historical moment. Their development involved different philosophical debates, literary traditions and responses to changes in European intellectual life.

The Development of Nihilism

The term nihilism acquired an important philosophical meaning in the late eighteenth century. Friedrich Heinrich Jacobi used it in his 1799 open letter to Fichte, criticising what he regarded as the consequences of German idealism.

During the nineteenth century, the word gained prominence in Russia. Ivan Turgenev's novel Fathers and Sons (1862) popularised the term through its character Bazarov. Russian nihilism became associated with the rejection of inherited authority, religious conventions and traditional social structures. [9]

Nietzsche later gave nihilism a much broader philosophical significance. He examined the crisis that emerges when traditional religious and moral foundations lose their authority, particularly in European culture.

The Development of Existentialism

Existentialist thought has important nineteenth-century roots in Søren Kierkegaard and Friedrich Nietzsche. Kierkegaard examined subjective existence, anxiety, choice and religious commitment. Nietzsche questioned inherited values and investigated the consequences of the decline of traditional religious foundations.

During the twentieth century, existential themes developed through the work of Martin Heidegger, Karl Jaspers, Jean-Paul Sartre and Simone de Beauvoir, among others. Existentialism became particularly influential in European philosophy and literature following the Second World War.

The term itself was used in different ways, and not every philosopher associated with existential questions accepted the label. Heidegger, for example, rejected being classified as an existentialist in the ordinary sense, despite the enormous influence of Being and Time on later existential thought. [10]

Nietzsche: Nihilism as a Crisis of Values

Friedrich Nietzsche occupies an especially important position in discussions of nihilism and existentialism. His philosophy examines what happens when the beliefs and values that once gave European society a sense of direction lose their authority. Rather than simply defending nihilism, Nietzsche investigates its origins, consequences and possible overcoming.

The Death of God

Nietzsche's declaration that God is dead appears most famously in The Gay Science. The statement is not merely an argument about whether a supernatural being exists. It is a cultural diagnosis concerning the declining authority of Christianity and the moral and metaphysical framework that had shaped European life.

If the foundations of traditional morality lose their credibility, people must confront a difficult question: what can give values their authority when their former foundations are no longer accepted? Nietzsche sees this as one of the central problems of modern nihilism. [13]

Active and Passive Nihilism

Nietzsche's notebooks contain discussions of active and passive nihilism. These distinctions are useful for understanding different responses to the collapse of inherited values, although the material was assembled and published posthumously and should be read with appropriate textual caution.

Passive nihilism describes a condition of exhaustion, resignation or declining vitality in which individuals struggle to find reasons to affirm existence. Active nihilism, by contrast, can involve the destruction of established values that have lost their authority. It may clear the ground for transformation, but destruction alone does not complete the task of creating or affirming values. [14]

Overcoming Nihilism

Nietzsche's project involves more than recognising that traditional foundations have weakened. He explores the possibility of affirming life and re-evaluating values rather than remaining trapped in resignation. His ideas of self-overcoming and the eternal recurrence challenge individuals to examine whether they can affirm their existence without relying on an external guarantee of purpose.

This is one reason Nietzsche influenced later existentialist thinkers. He did not provide the same philosophical system as Sartre or Kierkegaard, but his investigation of values, individuality and the problem of meaning helped shape modern existential debates.[d]

Kierkegaard: Individual Existence, Anxiety and Commitment

Søren Kierkegaard is widely regarded as a major precursor of existentialism. His philosophy focuses on the individual who must confront uncertainty, make choices and take responsibility for becoming a particular kind of self.

In works such as Either/Or and Fear and Trembling, Kierkegaard examines different ways of living and the significance of personal commitment. He is particularly concerned with the difference between treating life as an abstract intellectual problem and actually existing through decisions that involve risk and responsibility.

Kierkegaard's religious existential thought also demonstrates why existentialism cannot be equated with atheism or nihilism. His account of faith does not depend on the idea that life has no purpose. Instead, he investigates the individual's relationship with God and the demands of a personal religious commitment. [12]

His philosophy therefore offers a different response to uncertainty from existential nihilism. Rather than concluding that existence lacks all ultimate significance, Kierkegaard explores how the individual confronts uncertainty through faith, choice and inward commitment.

Sartre: Freedom, Responsibility and the Creation of Meaning

Jean-Paul Sartre is one of the most influential representatives of twentieth-century existentialism. His philosophy begins from an atheistic position and examines what human existence means in a world without a divinely predetermined human essence.

Existence Precedes Essence

Sartre's famous proposition that existence precedes essence means that human beings do not arrive in the world with a fully determined purpose or nature that dictates everything they must become. Instead, individuals shape themselves through actions and choices.

This does not mean that people can become absolutely anything they imagine. Human freedom operates within concrete situations, including physical limitations, social conditions and historical circumstances. Sartre's philosophy explores the tension between these conditions and the individual's responsibility for responding to them. [17]

Bad Faith and Self-Deception

Sartre's concept of bad faith describes attempts to escape the responsibility associated with human freedom. An individual may treat a social role, external expectation or fixed identity as though it completely determines who they are. In doing so, the individual avoids confronting the freedom involved in responding to their circumstances.

This is a distinctly existentialist concern. The absence of a predetermined essence does not lead Sartre to conclude that nothing matters. Rather, it intensifies the responsibility involved in living and choosing.

Albert Camus: Absurdity, Revolt and the Problem of Meaning

Albert Camus is frequently discussed alongside existentialist philosophers, although he rejected the existentialist label.[e] His philosophy of the absurd is nevertheless essential to understanding how twentieth-century thinkers responded to the problem of meaning.

The Absurd Condition

In The Myth of Sisyphus, Camus describes the absurd as emerging from the conflict between the human desire for clarity, unity and meaning and the world's failure to provide the answers people seek. The absurd is not simply a property of the universe or an emotional state. It arises through the confrontation between human longing and the world's silence. [15]

Revolt Against Nihilistic Resignation

Camus rejects the idea that recognising the absurd requires surrendering to meaninglessness. He examines the possibility of living with uncertainty without inventing false guarantees or escaping the problem through an appeal to absolute certainty.

His answer is associated with revolt: a continued affirmation of life in the face of the absurd. The image of Sisyphus, condemned to push a rock repeatedly up a mountain, becomes a philosophical figure for confronting an apparently meaningless condition without surrendering one's awareness or freedom.

Camus therefore provides an important contrast to nihilistic resignation. His philosophy does not establish a universal purpose for life, but it explores how a person might live without one.

Where Nihilism and Existentialism Overlap

Despite their differences, nihilism and existentialism share several philosophical concerns. Both can challenge the assumption that life comes with an obvious, universally accepted purpose. Both examine what happens when inherited beliefs and traditional systems of value lose their unquestioned authority.

The Absence of Predetermined Meaning

Existential nihilism and some forms of existentialism begin with the possibility that the universe does not contain a predetermined purpose for human beings. This shared starting point can make the two positions appear identical. However, accepting that purpose is not built into existence does not settle what individuals should do with their lives.

Questioning Inherited Values

Nietzsche's critique of traditional morality illustrates the relationship between the two traditions. His analysis exposes the problem of values whose former foundations have lost their authority. Later existentialist thinkers explore related questions concerning freedom, authenticity and the responsibilities involved in choosing a way of life.

Different Philosophical Responses

The overlap is therefore best understood as a shared field of questions rather than a shared conclusion. Nihilism may deny a particular kind of meaning or value. Existentialism asks how human beings exist and act in relation to the conditions of their lives. One can recognise the problem raised by nihilism without accepting nihilism as the final philosophical answer.

Criticisms and Philosophical Problems

Neither nihilism nor existentialism is free from philosophical difficulties. Their arguments raise questions about the status of meaning, the foundations of morality and whether individual commitment can provide a sufficient response to the absence of objective purpose.

Criticisms of Nihilism

One criticism concerns the relationship between objective and subjective meaning. Even if life has no universal purpose, it does not immediately follow that personal relationships, intellectual achievements or creative projects are worthless. A critic may argue that nihilism moves too quickly from the absence of cosmic meaning to a broader conclusion about the significance of human life.

Another difficulty concerns moral nihilism. Critics question whether denying objective moral facts undermines the possibility of meaningful moral criticism, ethical reform or rational disagreement about human conduct. Defenders of moral anti-realism respond in different ways, and not all of them accept the same conclusions.

Criticisms of Existentialism

Existentialism faces a different challenge: whether personal choice and commitment can provide an adequate account of meaning and ethical responsibility. Critics may ask why an individual commitment should count as valuable simply because someone chooses it.

Another objection concerns the extent of human freedom. Individuals do not choose all the circumstances into which they are born. Poverty, political oppression, social structures and physical limitations can restrict the possibilities available to them. An adequate existential philosophy must therefore explain how freedom operates within real conditions rather than treating every individual as equally unconstrained.

Can Subjective Meaning Answer the Problem of Objective Meaning?

This remains one of the central philosophical disagreements. A person may find meaning in friendship, love, knowledge or creative work, but whether such meaning answers the demand for an objective and universal purpose depends on what is meant by meaning in the first place.

Existentialist responses often argue that the absence of a universal guarantee does not make human commitments insignificant. Critics may still question whether this provides the kind of ultimate justification that existential nihilism denies.

Nihilism vs Existentialism in Everyday Life

The philosophical distinction can be illustrated through ordinary situations. These examples are simplified illustrations, not definitions of how every nihilist or existentialist must think or behave.

Choosing a Career

Imagine someone questioning whether professional success has any inherent significance. An existential nihilistic perspective might question whether career achievement possesses any objective or universal purpose. An existentialist response might instead examine why the individual wants a particular career, what commitments it expresses and how the choice shapes their life.

Relationships and Human Commitments

Consider a person who questions whether love has an eternal or universal purpose. Recognising the absence of a guaranteed cosmic explanation does not necessarily mean that a relationship cannot be meaningful. An existentialist approach may focus on the choices, care and responsibilities through which people sustain their relationships.

Making an Ethical Decision

When confronted with an ethical decision, moral nihilism raises questions about whether objective moral facts exist. Existentialist thought, particularly in Beauvoir's work, examines the responsibilities involved in acting freely in a world shared with others. The two approaches therefore raise different questions about the grounds and consequences of moral action.

Conclusion: Are Nihilism and Existentialism the Same?

Nihilism and existentialism are closely related through their concern with meaning, value and human existence, but they are not the same philosophical position. Nihilism questions or denies particular foundations, including the possibility of inherent meaning or objective moral value. Existentialism examines how individuals live, choose and accept responsibility within the conditions of their existence.

The difference becomes particularly important when existential nihilism is compared with Sartre's philosophy. Both confront the absence of a predetermined human purpose, but Sartre argues that people shape themselves through their actions and must take responsibility for their choices. Camus offers another response through his philosophy of the absurd and revolt, while Kierkegaard explores existence through faith and individual commitment.

Ultimately, existentialism is not simply nihilism with a more optimistic conclusion. It is a broader philosophical investigation into what it means to be human. It asks whether freedom, responsibility and commitment can matter even when existence does not provide ready-made answers. The disagreement between these traditions remains significant because it concerns not only whether life has meaning, but also what kind of meaning human beings can reasonably claim.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the main difference between nihilism and existentialism?

Nihilism questions or denies objective meaning, value or foundations in a particular domain. Existentialism examines human existence, freedom, responsibility and how people live and make choices within their circumstances.

Is existentialism a form of nihilism?

No. Existentialism is not inherently nihilistic. Some existentialist thinkers confront the absence of predetermined meaning but explore freedom, responsibility and human commitments rather than concluding that nothing matters.

Are existential nihilism and existentialism the same?

No. Existential nihilism holds that life has no inherent or objective meaning. Existentialism is a broader philosophical tradition concerned with existence, freedom, choice and responsibility.

Was Nietzsche a nihilist?

Nietzsche analysed nihilism as a crisis in European values and investigated ways of overcoming it. He is generally better understood as a diagnostician and critic of nihilism than as a straightforward advocate of it.

Was Nietzsche an existentialist?

Nietzsche strongly influenced later existentialist philosophy, but he is not usually classified as an existentialist in the strict historical sense. His work belongs to a different nineteenth-century philosophical context.

Did Camus believe that life was meaningless?

Camus examined the absurd conflict between the human desire for meaning and the world's silence. However, he rejected nihilistic resignation and explored revolt and continued engagement with life.

Does existentialism believe that people create their own meaning?

Some existentialist philosophies, especially Sartre's, connect meaning with human choices and commitments. Other thinkers, including Kierkegaard, understand existence through religious commitment. Existentialism is therefore not one uniform doctrine.

Which came first, nihilism or existentialism?

The term nihilism received a major philosophical use in Jacobi's 1799 letter to Fichte. Existentialist thought has important nineteenth-century roots in Kierkegaard and Nietzsche, although the movement became especially influential in the twentieth century.

Can someone reject nihilism without believing in religion?

Yes. A person can reject existential nihilism while remaining an atheist. Secular existentialist philosophies, including Sartre's, explore meaning, freedom and responsibility without relying on religious foundations.
