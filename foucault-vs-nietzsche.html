Foucault vs Nietzsche: Genealogy, Power and Truth Compared

Explore the intellectual relationship between Friedrich Nietzsche and Michel Foucault through their approaches to genealogy, power, truth, and history. This comparison examines how Foucault drew on Nietzsche’s genealogical method while developing his own analysis of knowledge, discourse, power relations, and the historical formation of values.

Short answerFoucault borrowed genealogy from Nietzsche: a history that shows how values and truths arose from contingent struggles. Late in life he even called himself simply a Nietzschean. But Foucault's power relations are not Nietzsche's metaphysical “will to power”, and Foucault studies institutions where Nietzsche studies culture and psychology.

Foucault vs Nietzsche at a glance

Nietzsche Foucault
Method Genealogy of morals (On the Genealogy of Morality, 1887) Genealogy of prisons, sexuality, the subject. See genealogy vs archaeology
Power “Will to power” as a basic drive Relations of power, not a substance or drive
Truth Perspectivism; truth tied to life and valuation Regimes of truth produced by institutions
Morality Attacks Christian-European morality Analyses how norms and subjects are produced
Focus Culture, psychology, values Institutions, practices, bodies
The self Self-overcoming, creating oneself Late “care of the self” and aesthetics of existence

Foucault's relationship to Nietzsche is especially important because Nietzsche's genealogical method strongly influenced Foucault's own approach to history. In On the Genealogy of Morality (1887), Nietzsche investigates the historical emergence of moral concepts such as good, evil, guilt, punishment, and asceticism rather than assuming that they express timeless or universal values. Foucault adapts this genealogical orientation to different objects, examining the historical formation of institutions, practices, forms of knowledge, bodies, and subjects. His genealogies of punishment and sexuality similarly ask how apparently natural or self-evident categories came to exist through contingent historical processes. However, Foucault's genealogy is not simply a repetition of Nietzsche's method: he places greater emphasis on institutions, administrative practices, disciplinary mechanisms, and the relationship between power and knowledge. His approach is therefore closely indebted to Nietzsche while developing genealogy for the analysis of modern social and political practices. See genealogy vs archaeology.

Nietzsche and Foucault also share a suspicion of universal explanations of truth, morality, and the human subject, but they develop this concern in different directions. Nietzsche's perspectivism challenges the assumption that knowledge can always be separated from interpretation, valuation, and the conditions of life. His analysis of morality examines how values emerge from struggles, interpretations, and changing relations of force. Foucault similarly investigates how historically specific regimes of truth establish what counts as legitimate knowledge and how individuals become recognizable as particular kinds of subjects. Their accounts of the self also converge in important ways: Nietzsche emphasizes self-overcoming and the creation of one's values, while Foucault's later work examines the care of the self and the possibility of shaping one's existence through practices of self-formation. The important difference is that Nietzsche's project is strongly concerned with the transformation and evaluation of values, whereas Foucault generally analyzes the historical practices and power relations through which individuals are formed and can work on themselves.

The shared method: genealogy

For Foucault, genealogy is a historical method that traces the contingent emergence and development of practices, values, institutions, and forms of knowledge rather than searching for one original source or timeless essence. Foucault develops this approach explicitly through his reading of Nietzsche, particularly in his 1971 essay “Nietzsche, Genealogy, History.” Instead of asking when a concept first appeared in a pure or complete form, genealogy examines the different events, conflicts, accidents, interpretations, institutional changes, and struggles through which something came to take its present form. This means that concepts that appear natural or universal can be shown to have particular historical conditions of emergence. Genealogy therefore challenges narratives of continuous progress by drawing attention to discontinuities, reversals, forgotten struggles, and the unintended consequences of historical developments.

Foucault applies this genealogical approach to modern institutions and practices, rather than limiting it to the history of moral concepts. In Discipline and Punish, for example, he examines the historical transformation of punishment and the emergence of the modern prison, connecting changes in penal practices with broader developments in surveillance, discipline, examination, and normalization. The point is not simply to identify the original cause of the prison, but to reconstruct the network of practices and power relations that made the modern penal system possible and intelligible. In this respect, Foucault follows Nietzsche's rejection of simple origin stories while redirecting genealogy toward questions of institutions, bodies, knowledge, and subject formation. See the Discipline and Punish summary.

Will to power vs power relations

Nietzsche's “will to power” and Foucault's concept of power relations both reject the idea that power can be understood simply as a possession held by a sovereign authority, but they refer to importantly different ideas. In Nietzsche's writings, particularly his later philosophy, the will to power is associated with the drives, forces, and tendencies through which living beings strive to expand, express, organize, overcome, interpret, and transform themselves and their circumstances. It is therefore broader than political domination and is connected to Nietzsche's account of life, struggle, valuation, and self-overcoming. Foucault does not adopt the will to power as a universal psychological or biological principle. Instead, he analyzes power relations as historically specific relationships in which conduct is shaped, directed, constrained, enabled, or transformed by other forms of conduct. Power for Foucault is not a substance that someone simply owns; it is exercised through relationships, practices, institutions, and techniques such as surveillance, examination, discipline, and normalization. These relations can operate at many levels, from interactions between individuals to larger institutional arrangements, and they are not necessarily reducible to physical coercion. Foucault also insists that power relations are accompanied by possibilities of resistance: where power is exercised, forms of resistance can emerge within the same field of relations. This makes his concept more empirical and historically oriented than a general theory of an underlying drive. His account of power is closely connected to power/knowledge, through which he examines how practices of knowing and mechanisms of power interact in the formation of subjects and accepted forms of truth.

Truth, morality and the self

Nietzsche and Foucault both question the idea that truth, morality, and the human self can be understood as timeless and universally fixed, but they pursue this critique in different ways. Nietzsche challenges established European moral values by asking how concepts such as good, evil, guilt, responsibility, and virtue emerged and what kinds of human beings and forms of life those values encourage. His genealogical analysis of morality is therefore also evaluative: he does not merely describe the historical development of values but asks whether particular values enhance or diminish human flourishing, strength, creativity, and self-overcoming. His perspectivism likewise emphasizes that knowledge is connected to interpretation and perspective rather than being detached from the conditions under which it is produced. Foucault shares the suspicion of supposedly universal categories but is generally more cautious about replacing them with a universal system of his own. Rather than establishing a final moral standard from which all forms of domination can be judged, he investigates how historically specific practices, institutions, and forms of knowledge establish norms and shape individuals as particular kinds of subjects. His question is often not simply whether a norm is true or false, but how it became authoritative, what practices sustain it, and what effects it has on people's conduct and understanding of themselves.

Their approaches to the self nevertheless come closer in Foucault's later work, where he examines practices through which people actively form themselves. Nietzsche's ideal of self-overcoming involves resisting inherited values and participating in the creation and transformation of one's own mode of life rather than simply conforming to established moral expectations. Foucault's later studies of ancient Greek and Roman ethics similarly examine the care of the self, ethical self-formation, and what he calls an aesthetics of existence. These practices concern how individuals work upon themselves through reflection, discipline, conduct, and relationships with others. Foucault does not simply reproduce Nietzsche's philosophy, however: his historical analysis focuses on the specific ethical practices and forms of subject formation found in different societies. The connection between the two is therefore strongest in their shared interest in questioning inherited values and examining how people become particular kinds of selves, while their projects differ in the extent to which they explicitly evaluate competing ways of living.

How Foucault described his debt

Foucault repeatedly acknowledged a deep intellectual debt to Nietzsche, particularly to Nietzsche's use of genealogy, his criticism of conventional accounts of history and morality, and his analysis of the relationship between knowledge, interpretation, and power. In interviews during the late 1970s and early 1980s, Foucault sometimes described himself as a Nietzschean and emphasized that Nietzsche had been one of the thinkers who most directly shaped his philosophical work. He regarded Nietzsche's texts as resources for developing a different way of doing history: rather than searching for timeless origins or universal foundations, the historian could investigate the contingent struggles, practices, and transformations through which present forms of thought emerged. This influence is particularly visible in Foucault's genealogical studies of punishment, sexuality, morality, and subject formation. At the same time, describing Foucault simply as a “Nietzschean” can obscure the substantial differences between their projects. Foucault adapted Nietzsche's genealogical method to twentieth-century questions about institutions, disciplinary power, government, knowledge, and modern subjectivity, and he did not simply adopt Nietzsche's claims about the will to power, morality, or human nature. His statements about being Nietzschean are therefore best understood as an acknowledgment of intellectual influence rather than as a claim that his philosophy was identical to Nietzsche's. Frequently asked questions

Was Foucault influenced by Nietzsche?

Yes. Genealogy, his critique of truth and morality, and his history-of-the-present approach all draw on Nietzsche.

What is the difference between Foucault's power and Nietzsche's will to power?

Nietzsche treats will to power as a basic drive of life. Foucault treats power as historical relations that can be studied, and he avoids a general theory.

Did Foucault call himself a Nietzschean?

In interviews in the early 1980s he described himself as simply a Nietzschean.

Which book shows Foucault's debt to Nietzsche?

The essay “Nietzsche, Genealogy, History” (1971) and Discipline and Punish (1975).
