Søren Kierkegaard: Philosophy, Existentialism, Anxiety, and Faith

Discover Søren Kierkegaard's philosophy of existence, existentialism, anxiety, despair, freedom, subjectivity, the leap of faith, the stages of life, Christianity, major works, and influence on modern philosophy.

Who Was Søren Kierkegaard?

Søren Kierkegaard (1813–1855) was a Danish philosopher, theologian, writer, and religious thinker whose work became one of the major foundations of modern existential philosophy. He is widely regarded as a central precursor of existentialism because of his intense focus on the individual, personal choice, anxiety, despair, faith, and the problem of how a human being should live.

Kierkegaard believed that philosophy often became too abstract when it attempted to explain reality through large intellectual systems. Human existence, he argued, cannot be completely understood as an abstract concept because every individual must personally make choices, face uncertainty, accept responsibility, and determine how to live.

His philosophy explored some of the most personal questions in human life: What does it mean to become oneself? Why does freedom produce anxiety? What is despair? Can faith be rationally proven? Kierkegaard approached these questions through philosophy, literature, theology, psychology, and a distinctive style of writing that often used pseudonymous authors.

Søren Kierkegaard's Early Life and Historical Background

Søren Kierkegaard was born in Copenhagen, Denmark, in 1813. He lived during a period in which European philosophy was strongly influenced by German idealism, especially the philosophy of Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel. At the same time, questions concerning Christianity, modern society, individuality, and the meaning of human existence were becoming increasingly important.

Kierkegaard studied theology and philosophy at the University of Copenhagen. His intellectual development was deeply shaped by Christianity, classical philosophy, German idealism, literature, and the philosophical debates of his own time. He became especially concerned with the relationship between abstract philosophical thought and the concrete life of an individual person.

Much of Kierkegaard's life was spent writing in Copenhagen, where he produced an extraordinary number of philosophical and religious works. Although he was not widely recognized internationally during his lifetime, his writings later became enormously influential in philosophy, theology, psychology, literature, and existentialism.

Why Is Søren Kierkegaard Called the Father of Existentialism?

Søren Kierkegaard is often called the father of existentialism because his philosophy placed individual human existence at the center of philosophical investigation. Rather than beginning primarily with abstract questions about reality, he asked what it means for a particular human being to exist, choose, suffer, believe, and become a self.

Kierkegaard did not create the later twentieth-century philosophical movement known as existentialism in its complete form. Thinkers such as Martin Heidegger, Jean-Paul Sartre, Albert Camus, and Simone de Beauvoir developed very different forms of existential philosophy. Nevertheless, many of the central existential themes already appear powerfully in Kierkegaard's writings.

These themes include freedom, possibility, anxiety, despair, individuality, responsibility, authenticity, and the difficulty of finding meaning in an uncertain world. Kierkegaard believed that existence is not something a person merely observes from outside; it is something each individual must personally live.

His importance to existentialism therefore comes from his insistence that philosophy must take the concrete individual seriously. A philosophical theory may describe humanity in general, but it cannot make an individual's decisions or live that person's life. Existence, for Kierkegaard, always involves a personal dimension.

What Was Søren Kierkegaard's Philosophy?

Søren Kierkegaard's philosophy was concerned above all with the problem of human existence. He investigated how people become individuals, how they make choices, why they experience anxiety and despair, and how they can relate to ethical and religious questions that cannot always be resolved through objective certainty.

Kierkegaard was suspicious of philosophical systems that attempted to explain every aspect of reality from a single abstract perspective. He argued that even the most comprehensive intellectual system cannot replace the lived experience of an individual who must act within time, uncertainty, and personal responsibility.

His philosophy therefore combines existential reflection with psychology, ethics, theology, and literary experimentation. Kierkegaard often wrote in ways designed to make readers confront their own assumptions rather than simply receive a final philosophical doctrine from an authoritative teacher.

Kierkegaard and the Importance of the Individual

The individual occupies a central position in Kierkegaard's philosophy. He believed that modern society and abstract philosophical thinking could encourage people to forget their own personal responsibility by thinking only in terms of humanity, society, history, institutions, or large intellectual systems.

For Kierkegaard, becoming an individual does not mean simply doing whatever one wants. Individuality requires self-reflection, choice, commitment, and responsibility. A person must confront the question of how to live rather than hiding permanently behind social conventions or the opinions of others.

This emphasis on the individual became one of Kierkegaard's most influential contributions to modern philosophy. Later existential thinkers continued to investigate the tension between the individual and society, personal freedom and social pressure, and authentic choice and conformity.

Søren Kierkegaard and Hegel: Why Did Kierkegaard Criticize Hegel?

Kierkegaard's criticism of Hegel is one of the most important aspects of his philosophical identity. Hegel developed an ambitious philosophical system that attempted to understand reality, history, consciousness, and human development through a comprehensive dialectical process.

Kierkegaard believed that systematic philosophy could become too detached from concrete human existence. A philosophical system may describe the development of history or explain universal concepts, but the individual person still faces immediate questions concerning choice, suffering, responsibility, death, and personal commitment.

His objection was not simply that philosophical thinking is useless. Rather, Kierkegaard argued that there is a difference between understanding existence as an intellectual concept and actually existing. No system can live on behalf of the individual who must make decisions in the uncertainty of everyday life.

This disagreement helped establish one of the central themes of existential philosophy: human existence contains dimensions that cannot be completely captured by abstract, objective, or systematic thought. Philosophy must therefore remain attentive to the concrete situation of the individual.

Kierkegaard and Subjectivity: What Does "Truth Is Subjectivity" Mean?

One of Kierkegaard's most famous ideas is commonly summarized by the phrase "truth is subjectivity." This statement is often misunderstood as meaning that there are no objective facts or that every personal opinion is equally true. Kierkegaard's argument is more concerned with the relationship between truth and human existence.

Some truths can be understood objectively. Mathematical and historical questions, for example, can often be investigated through evidence, logic, and shared methods of inquiry. But questions concerning how a person should live, what they should commit themselves to, and how they relate personally to faith involve an existential dimension.

Kierkegaard emphasized the importance of appropriation, meaning the way an individual takes a truth into their own life. A person may possess extensive knowledge about ethical or religious ideas while remaining personally indifferent to them. Existential truth therefore concerns how a person exists in relation to what they understand.

Kierkegaard's Three Stages of Life

One of the best-known aspects of Kierkegaard's philosophy is his description of different stages or spheres of existence. These are commonly described as the aesthetic, ethical, and religious stages. They should not always be understood as a simple historical sequence through which every person automatically progresses.

Instead, the stages represent different ways of understanding life, making choices, and relating to oneself and the world. Each stage involves a distinctive set of values and commitments, and the transition between them can require a profound personal decision.

1. The Aesthetic Stage

The aesthetic stage is associated with immediate experience, pleasure, novelty, imagination, and the search for interesting possibilities. The aesthetic individual may avoid permanent commitment in order to preserve freedom and continue moving between different experiences.

2. The Ethical Stage

The ethical stage involves responsibility, commitment, and the recognition that a person must take ownership of their actions. Instead of constantly pursuing new experiences, the individual begins to understand life through continuity, duty, and deliberate choice.

3. The Religious Stage

The religious stage concerns an individual's relationship with God and the demands of faith. For Kierkegaard, religious existence cannot simply be reduced to social morality or objective knowledge because faith involves inwardness, commitment, uncertainty, and paradox.

Kierkegaard's Aesthetic Stage of Life

In Kierkegaard's account, the aesthetic stage is characterized by a life oriented toward immediate enjoyment, interesting experiences, and the pursuit of possibility. The aesthetic individual may value beauty, pleasure, romance, entertainment, and intellectual stimulation while resisting commitments that appear to limit personal freedom.

Kierkegaard did not suggest that pleasure or beauty are inherently wrong. His concern was that a life based entirely on changing experiences can become unstable. What is exciting today may become boring tomorrow, creating a continuous search for something new.

This problem of boredom and dissatisfaction reveals an important existential tension. A person who constantly avoids commitment may preserve many possibilities, but may also fail to develop a coherent identity. The aesthetic life therefore raises the question of whether endless possibility can provide lasting meaning.

Kierkegaard's Ethical Stage of Life

The ethical stage represents a movement toward responsibility and commitment. The individual recognizes that a meaningful life cannot consist entirely of temporary experiences and begins to understand the importance of making decisions that create continuity and responsibility.

Ethical existence involves taking responsibility for one's actions and accepting that freedom is connected with commitment. A person becomes more fully responsible not by refusing to choose, but by recognizing that even avoiding a decision can itself become a form of decision.

Kierkegaard's ethical stage is therefore closely connected with the development of the self. Through commitment, responsibility, and deliberate action, the individual attempts to become a coherent person rather than merely drifting between changing desires and possibilities.

Kierkegaard's Religious Stage of Life

The religious stage is the most distinctive and controversial aspect of Kierkegaard's philosophy. It concerns an individual's relationship with God and cannot, in his view, be reduced entirely to social morality, institutional religion, or objective philosophical knowledge.

Kierkegaard believed that religious faith involves a personal and inward commitment. Faith is not simply the acceptance of information that has been objectively proven beyond all doubt. Instead, it confronts the individual with uncertainty, paradox, and the need for personal commitment.

His account of religious existence became central to Christian existentialism. At the same time, his writings have influenced nonreligious existential thinkers because of their powerful analysis of choice, uncertainty, individuality, and the difficulty of becoming oneself.

What Was Kierkegaard's Concept of Anxiety?

Anxiety is one of the most influential concepts in Kierkegaard's philosophy. He did not understand anxiety simply as an ordinary fear of a particular object. Fear usually has a recognizable object, but existential anxiety can arise from the awareness of possibility and freedom.

Kierkegaard famously connected anxiety with what is often translated as the "dizziness of freedom." Human beings possess the ability to choose among possibilities, and this freedom can be both empowering and unsettling because every important choice may change the direction of a person's life.

Anxiety therefore reveals something fundamental about human existence. A person is not completely fixed like an object in nature; human beings can imagine possibilities, reject one path, choose another, and reflect upon what they might become. Possibility creates freedom, but it also creates uncertainty.

Kierkegaard's analysis of anxiety became extremely influential in existential philosophy and psychology. Later thinkers continued to explore the idea that anxiety can reveal the instability and openness of human existence rather than being merely an accidental emotional disturbance.

Kierkegaard's Philosophy of Despair

Despair is another central concept in Kierkegaard's philosophy, especially in The Sickness Unto Death. He understood despair not simply as an ordinary feeling of sadness but as a deeper problem involving a person's relationship with the self.

Human beings, according to Kierkegaard, are capable of reflecting upon themselves and imagining what they might become. This makes it possible for a person to experience conflict between who they are, who they want to be, and who they believe they ought to become.

Despair can therefore appear in different forms. A person may not want to be the self they actually are, or may attempt to construct an identity without accepting the deeper conditions of their existence. Kierkegaard was interested in the ways people can avoid confronting the difficult task of becoming themselves.

His philosophy of despair has remained influential because it connects psychological suffering with philosophical questions about identity, responsibility, selfhood, and meaning. It asks whether a person can truly understand themselves without confronting the possibilities and contradictions within their own existence.

What Is the Self According to Kierkegaard?

Kierkegaard's philosophy of the self is closely connected with his analysis of despair. He did not regard the self as merely a fixed object that exists in exactly the same form throughout life. Becoming a self is an ongoing task involving reflection, possibility, choice, and personal responsibility.

A human being experiences tensions between different aspects of existence: possibility and necessity, freedom and limitation, the finite and the infinite. The self must somehow relate to these dimensions rather than permanently escaping into only one side of human existence.

The task of becoming oneself is therefore difficult. It requires a person to confront both freedom and limitation, aspiration and reality. Kierkegaard believed that an authentic relationship with the self requires more than simply following social expectations or pursuing temporary desires.

Kierkegaard's Philosophy of Freedom and Choice

Freedom plays a central role in Kierkegaard's understanding of human existence. Human beings are capable of imagining different possibilities and making choices that influence their lives. This ability creates responsibility because a person cannot always blame external circumstances for every aspect of their existence.

At the same time, Kierkegaard did not present freedom as a simple source of happiness. Freedom can create anxiety because choosing one possibility often means abandoning others. Important decisions cannot always be made with complete certainty about their future consequences.

Kierkegaard's philosophy therefore connects freedom with uncertainty. To exist as a free individual is to live without possessing complete control over the future. A person must still act and commit themselves even though perfect knowledge is unavailable.

What Is Kierkegaard's Leap of Faith?

The idea of the leap of faith is closely associated with Søren Kierkegaard, although the popular expression can sometimes oversimplify his philosophy. Kierkegaard was deeply concerned with the problem of religious commitment when objective certainty is not available.

Faith, in this context, cannot simply be treated as the conclusion of a mathematical proof. The individual encounters uncertainty and must decide how to relate to questions that cannot be completely settled through detached objective reasoning.

The leap of faith therefore represents a form of commitment made in the presence of uncertainty. It does not mean that every irrational belief should automatically be accepted. Rather, Kierkegaard's religious philosophy investigates the limits of objective proof when confronting existential and religious questions.

This concept became influential far beyond religious philosophy. Existential thinkers have often explored the broader problem of how human beings make important commitments when they cannot predict the future or possess complete certainty about the consequences of their decisions.

Søren Kierkegaard's Fear and Trembling Explained

Fear and Trembling is one of Kierkegaard's most famous works and examines the biblical story of Abraham and Isaac. The work investigates the difficult relationship between faith, ethics, obedience, and the individual relationship with God.

Abraham and Isaac

Kierkegaard focuses on Abraham's willingness to obey God's command to sacrifice Isaac. The story creates a profound philosophical problem because ordinary ethical reasoning appears to condemn the killing of an innocent person, while Abraham's religious commitment appears to place him in a relationship with a higher divine command.

The Teleological Suspension of the Ethical

Kierkegaard explores whether there can be situations in which the individual stands in a relationship with God that cannot be fully explained through universal ethical categories. This idea is one of the most controversial aspects of his philosophy and should not be understood as a general permission to ignore morality.

The Knight of Faith

The work also develops the image of the knight of faith, an individual who makes a profound commitment without possessing the kind of objective certainty that would remove all risk. The knight of faith represents Kierkegaard's interest in inwardness, commitment, paradox, and personal existence.

Kierkegaard's Philosophy of Faith and Christianity

Christianity was central to Kierkegaard's intellectual life. However, he was deeply critical of forms of Christianity that he believed had become socially comfortable or merely conventional. He distinguished between personally lived faith and belonging to Christianity simply because it was part of the culture.

Kierkegaard emphasized the difficulty of genuine religious commitment. For him, faith was not merely a collection of doctrines learned from society. It required inwardness and a personal relationship with religious questions that could not simply be solved by public opinion or institutional authority.

His criticism of established Christendom made Kierkegaard a controversial figure during his lifetime. Yet it also contributed to his lasting influence on philosophy of religion and Christian existentialism, where personal commitment and individual existence remain central themes.

What Did Kierkegaard Mean by "The Crowd Is Untruth"?

Kierkegaard was strongly concerned about the power of the crowd and public opinion. His criticism was not simply directed against cooperation or society itself. Instead, he worried that individuals could lose their sense of personal responsibility when they hide behind anonymous groups.

Within a crowd, a person may feel less personally responsible for their actions because responsibility appears to belong to everyone and therefore to no one in particular. Kierkegaard believed that this anonymity could encourage conformity and discourage serious personal reflection.

His criticism remains relevant to modern discussions about mass media, social conformity, public opinion, and digital culture. Kierkegaard's central question is whether an individual can preserve personal responsibility while living within a society that constantly pressures people to think and act collectively.

Why Did Søren Kierkegaard Use Pseudonyms?

One of the most unusual features of Kierkegaard's writing is his extensive use of pseudonyms. Many of his major works were published under fictional names, and these pseudonymous authors often presented philosophical perspectives that should not simply be identified directly with Kierkegaard's own final position.

This method allowed Kierkegaard to explore different forms of existence from within. Instead of simply telling readers what to believe, he could create literary voices that represented aesthetic, ethical, skeptical, or religious perspectives and allow the tensions between those positions to become visible.

Kierkegaard described his broader method as a form of indirect communication. The purpose was often to encourage readers to think for themselves and confront their own existential situation rather than passively accepting a philosophical conclusion.

Major Works of Søren Kierkegaard

Søren Kierkegaard produced a large and diverse body of philosophical, theological, literary, and psychological writing. His works often examine the same fundamental problems from different perspectives, including choice, existence, anxiety, despair, ethics, faith, and the relationship between the individual and society.

  • Either/Or — A major work exploring aesthetic and ethical forms of existence, choice, commitment, and the problem of how a person should live.
  • Fear and Trembling — A philosophical examination of Abraham, faith, ethics, sacrifice, and the paradoxes of religious existence.
  • The Concept of Anxiety — A major analysis of anxiety, freedom, possibility, and the psychological conditions of human existence.
  • Philosophical Fragments — A work examining truth, learning, religious revelation, and the relationship between human understanding and Christianity.
  • Concluding Unscientific Postscript — A major exploration of subjectivity, objective knowledge, existence, and what it means to become personally committed to truth.
  • The Sickness Unto Death — Kierkegaard's influential analysis of despair, selfhood, and the problem of becoming oneself.
  • Works of Love — A philosophical and religious investigation of love, duty, the neighbor, and ethical relationships.

Søren Kierkegaard's Main Philosophical Ideas

1. The Individual

Kierkegaard believed that each person must confront their own existence. Social systems, philosophical theories, and public opinion cannot completely remove an individual's responsibility for the choices they make.

2. Freedom and Anxiety

Freedom creates possibility, but possibility also creates anxiety. Human beings must choose without possessing complete knowledge of the future, making freedom both empowering and deeply unsettling.

3. Despair and the Self

Despair can emerge when a person fails to establish a proper relationship with themselves. Becoming a self is therefore not an automatic process but an existential task involving reflection and responsibility.

4. Subjectivity and Existential Truth

Kierkegaard emphasized that some of life's most important questions concern not only what a person knows objectively but how they exist in relation to what they understand and believe.

5. Faith and Commitment

Faith involves commitment under conditions of uncertainty. Kierkegaard explored the limits of objective proof and the personal risks involved when an individual commits themselves to a religious life.

6. Becoming Oneself

Human existence involves the difficult task of becoming oneself. Kierkegaard believed that people can lose themselves through conformity, avoidance, despair, or an unwillingness to confront the responsibilities of freedom.

Søren Kierkegaard and Friedrich Nietzsche

Søren Kierkegaard and Friedrich Nietzsche are both major nineteenth-century thinkers who criticized conformity and challenged individuals to examine the values by which they live. Both philosophers became important influences on later existential and modern thought.

However, their philosophical conclusions were profoundly different. Kierkegaard's philosophy was deeply Christian and explored the individual's relationship with God, whereas Nietzsche developed a radical critique of Christianity and traditional morality.

Their comparison reveals an important feature of existential thought: similar questions about individuality, meaning, and conformity can lead philosophers toward radically different answers. Both remain essential figures for understanding the development of modern philosophy.

Søren Kierkegaard and Jean-Paul Sartre

Kierkegaard had a major influence on the intellectual development of existentialism, and later thinkers such as Jean-Paul Sartre developed many existential themes in new directions. Both philosophers emphasized freedom, choice, responsibility, anxiety, and the importance of individual existence.

The major difference between them concerns religion. Kierkegaard's existential philosophy is fundamentally connected with Christianity and faith, while Sartre developed an explicitly atheistic form of existentialism in which human beings must create meaning without relying upon a divine foundation.

Despite this difference, Kierkegaard's exploration of uncertainty and personal responsibility helped prepare the intellectual ground for later existential philosophy. Sartre transformed many of these concerns into a different philosophical framework suited to the twentieth century.

Søren Kierkegaard's Influence on Existentialism and Philosophy

Kierkegaard's influence became increasingly important after his death. His writings contributed significantly to the development of existential philosophy, existential theology, psychology, and modern literary thought. His focus on the individual offered an alternative to philosophical traditions centered primarily on universal systems.

Thinkers associated with existentialism, including Martin Heidegger, Karl Jaspers, Jean-Paul Sartre, Albert Camus, and Simone de Beauvoir, developed their own philosophical systems and should not simply be considered followers of Kierkegaard. Nevertheless, many later debates about existence were shaped by questions he had explored earlier.

Kierkegaard also influenced Christian theology, especially thinkers concerned with faith, individuality, and the limits of rational certainty. His analysis of anxiety and despair later became important to existential psychology and psychotherapy.

Today, Kierkegaard remains relevant because his philosophy addresses problems that continue to affect modern life: conformity, anxiety, identity, uncertainty, personal responsibility, religious belief, and the difficult question of how an individual should live.

Søren Kierkegaard's Philosophy Explained Simply

Kierkegaard's philosophy begins with the idea that being human is not simply about possessing knowledge. Every person must live their own life, make choices, face uncertainty, and eventually confront the question of who they are and what they are becoming.

  • Why do we experience anxiety? — Because freedom gives us possibilities, and possibilities create uncertainty about what we should choose.
  • What is despair? — Despair is a deeper problem involving a person's relationship with themselves and their failure or difficulty in becoming who they are.
  • What are the three stages of life? — Kierkegaard describes aesthetic, ethical, and religious ways of existing, each involving different forms of value and commitment.
  • What is the leap of faith? — Faith involves commitment under conditions where complete objective certainty is unavailable.
  • What does becoming yourself mean? — It means confronting freedom, responsibility, possibility, and the difficult task of forming a meaningful relationship with oneself.

Why Is Søren Kierkegaard Important in the History of Philosophy?

Søren Kierkegaard is important because he redirected philosophical attention toward the concrete experience of individual existence. At a time when many philosophers were constructing large systematic accounts of reality and history, Kierkegaard emphasized the person who must actually live within uncertainty and make personal decisions.

His analysis of anxiety, despair, freedom, selfhood, and faith introduced themes that later became central to existentialism. Kierkegaard showed that philosophy could investigate not only abstract concepts but also the psychological and existential conditions of living as a human being.

His influence extends beyond philosophy into theology, psychology, literature, and cultural criticism. Kierkegaard remains one of the most important thinkers for anyone interested in existentialism, philosophy of religion, the problem of individuality, and the meaning of human freedom.

Frequently Asked Questions About Søren Kierkegaard

Who was Søren Kierkegaard?

Søren Kierkegaard was a Danish philosopher, theologian, writer, and religious thinker who lived from 1813 to 1855. He is widely regarded as one of the most important precursors of existentialism because of his focus on individuality, freedom, anxiety, despair, and faith.

Why is Kierkegaard called the father of existentialism?

Kierkegaard is called the father of existentialism because he made individual human existence a central philosophical concern. His work explored freedom, choice, anxiety, despair, responsibility, and the problem of becoming oneself long before existentialism developed into a major philosophical movement.

What was Søren Kierkegaard's philosophy?

Kierkegaard's philosophy focused on human existence, subjectivity, freedom, anxiety, despair, selfhood, ethics, and religious faith. He argued that abstract philosophical systems cannot completely explain the concrete life of an individual who must personally make choices and accept responsibility.

What did Kierkegaard mean by anxiety?

Kierkegaard connected anxiety with freedom and possibility. Unlike ordinary fear, existential anxiety does not necessarily have one specific external object because it can arise from the awareness that human beings are free to choose among different possible futures.

What is Kierkegaard's concept of despair?

Kierkegaard understood despair as a problem involving the relationship between a person and the self. A person may experience despair by refusing to become who they are, attempting to escape themselves, or failing to establish a meaningful relationship with their existence.

What is the leap of faith?

The leap of faith refers to the problem of making a religious commitment without possessing complete objective certainty. Kierkegaard explored how faith involves personal commitment and risk rather than functioning like a purely logical or mathematical conclusion.

What are Kierkegaard's three stages of life?

Kierkegaard described aesthetic, ethical, and religious stages or spheres of existence. The aesthetic stage emphasizes experience and possibility, the ethical stage emphasizes responsibility and commitment, and the religious stage concerns an individual's relationship with God and faith.

What did Kierkegaard mean by "truth is subjectivity"?

Kierkegaard did not mean that objective facts are unimportant. His point was that existential and religious questions also concern how an individual personally exists in relation to truth, rather than merely possessing detached information about it.

Why did Kierkegaard criticize Hegel?

Kierkegaard believed that large philosophical systems could overlook the concrete reality of individual existence. A system may explain universal ideas or historical development, but it cannot make an individual's choices or remove the uncertainty involved in living.

What is Fear and Trembling about?

Fear and Trembling examines the biblical story of Abraham and Isaac. Kierkegaard uses the story to investigate difficult questions concerning faith, ethics, sacrifice, religious commitment, and the possibility of an individual's relationship with God.

Was Søren Kierkegaard a Christian philosopher?

Yes. Christianity was central to Kierkegaard's philosophy and writing. He was deeply concerned with the meaning of faith and the individual's relationship with God, although he strongly criticized forms of Christianity that he believed had become merely conventional or socially comfortable.

How did Kierkegaard influence existentialism?

Kierkegaard influenced existentialism by developing philosophical ideas about freedom, anxiety, despair, individuality, responsibility, and the concrete experience of human existence. Later philosophers developed these themes in different religious and nonreligious directions.

Why Søren Kierkegaard Still Matters

Søren Kierkegaard remains one of the most important philosophers of individual existence. His writings challenge people to consider what it means to make choices, accept responsibility, confront anxiety, experience despair, and develop a meaningful relationship with themselves.

His philosophy also remains powerful because it resists easy answers. Kierkegaard did not believe that the deepest problems of human life could always be solved through an abstract system or objective formula. The individual must still live, choose, and act within uncertainty.

Through his philosophy of anxiety, freedom, subjectivity, despair, and faith, Kierkegaard helped transform the direction of modern thought. Understanding his work also helps explain the intellectual foundations of existentialism and the continuing philosophical search for meaning, authenticity, and personal responsibility.
