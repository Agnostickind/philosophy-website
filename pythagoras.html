Pythagoras: Life, Philosophy and Pythagoreanism

Discover the life and philosophy of Pythagoras of Samos, the ancient Greek thinker associated with the Pythagorean way of life, the immortality and transmigration of the soul, the philosophical importance of number, and one of the most influential intellectual traditions of the ancient world.

Who Was Pythagoras?

Pythagoras of Samos was an ancient Greek philosopher traditionally dated to approximately 570–490 BCE. He was born on the island of Samos in the eastern Aegean and later established himself at Croton in southern Italy, where he attracted followers who adopted a distinctive philosophical, religious, and disciplined way of life.

Pythagoras is one of the most famous figures in ancient philosophy, mathematics, and the history of ideas. Yet the historical Pythagoras is difficult to reconstruct. Unlike Plato or Aristotle, he left behind no surviving writings, and no detailed contemporary account of his philosophy exists.

His name is popularly associated with the Pythagorean theorem, but ancient evidence suggests that his reputation during and shortly after his lifetime was connected at least as strongly with the fate of the soul, religious practices, discipline, and his distinctive community. The mathematical achievements associated with Pythagoreanism cannot always be assigned with certainty to Pythagoras himself.

For this reason, understanding Pythagoras requires distinguishing between the historical Pythagoras, the early Pythagorean community, and the many later traditions that developed around his name.

Pythagoras and the World of Ancient Greece

Pythagoras lived during a period of major intellectual development in the Greek world. Earlier Ionian thinkers such as Thales of Miletus had become associated with attempts to understand the natural world through principles and explanations concerning nature.

Pythagoras developed in a different direction. His tradition connected philosophical inquiry with religious practice, moral discipline, the purification of the soul, and numerical relationships. His followers formed a community in which philosophy was not simply a subject of intellectual discussion but also a way of living.

Pythagoras therefore occupies an unusual position in ancient Greek philosophy. He is associated both with philosophical ideas about reality and with a highly structured religious and ethical way of life.

Life of Pythagoras

Pythagoras was born on the island of Samos, near the western coast of Asia Minor. Ancient sources provide different stories about his family, education, travels, and teachers, and many of these accounts were written long after his lifetime.

Some ancient traditions describe journeys to places such as Egypt, Phoenicia, and Babylonia. These stories are difficult to verify, and modern historians disagree about how much confidence should be placed in them. The later tendency to portray Pythagoras as someone who had received all ancient wisdom from distant civilizations also contributed to the legendary image that developed around him.

A relatively important tradition, reported through later writers, connects Pythagoras' departure from Samos with the rule of Polycrates. He subsequently travelled to southern Italy and settled at Croton, probably around 530 BCE.

It was at Croton that Pythagoras appears to have established the community that became the center of his influence. Followers adopted a distinctive way of life involving discipline, religious practices, dietary rules, and philosophical teachings.

Pythagoras in Croton and the Pythagorean Community

Croton, a Greek city in southern Italy, became the most important center of Pythagorean activity. Pythagoras attracted a community of followers who treated his teachings as part of a complete way of life.

The Pythagorean community was not simply a mathematical school in the modern sense. Its members followed religious practices, ethical rules, dietary restrictions, and forms of communal discipline.

Ancient evidence also suggests that the Pythagorean movement had a political dimension. Pythagorean communities became influential in several Greek cities of southern Italy, although the precise political views of Pythagoras himself are difficult to reconstruct.

The combination of philosophy, religion, discipline, and community is essential for understanding Pythagoras. Reducing him to the mathematical theorem that bears his name leaves out a major part of the historical evidence about why he was influential in antiquity.

What Did Pythagoras Believe?

Reconstructing the philosophy of Pythagoras is difficult because no philosophical writings by him survive. Our knowledge comes from later reports, references by other philosophers, and evidence concerning the Pythagorean communities that developed after him.

Among the ideas most strongly associated with the early Pythagorean tradition are the immortality of the soul, the transmigration of the soul, religious purification, strict self-discipline, and the belief that numerical relationships have profound significance for the structure of reality.

However, it is important to distinguish Pythagoras from later Pythagoreans. Some doctrines that are often described as "Pythagorean" were developed by philosophers who lived generations after Pythagoras.

Pythagoras and the Immortality of the Soul

One of the most important ideas associated with Pythagoras is that the soul is immortal. According to the Pythagorean tradition, human existence is not limited to a single lifetime.

Pythagoras is also associated with the doctrine known as metempsychosis, or the transmigration of the soul. According to this belief, after death the soul can enter another living body.

This idea was unusual and influential within Greek thought. The soul was not understood merely as something that disappeared when the body died. Instead, the soul possessed a continuing existence and could undergo a series of lives.

The belief also had an ethical consequence. If the soul continues beyond death, then the way a person lives becomes important to the condition and future of the soul.

What Is Metempsychosis?

Metempsychosis is the philosophical and religious idea that the soul can move from one living body to another after death. In the Pythagorean tradition, this could include the transmigration of a human soul into an animal.

This belief helps explain several aspects of the Pythagorean way of life. If living beings may possess souls that have passed through different forms, then harming animals becomes morally and religiously significant.

The doctrine was therefore connected with ideas about purification, self-control, and diet. Pythagorean practices cannot be fully understood without considering the relationship between the body, the soul, and the possibility of the soul's continued existence.

Ancient sources disagree about many details of Pythagorean beliefs, so specific claims about the mechanics or duration of reincarnation should not automatically be presented as the personal teaching of Pythagoras.

The Pythagorean Way of Life

Pythagorean philosophy was closely connected with a disciplined way of life. Philosophy was not simply something to be studied intellectually; it involved practices intended to shape the character and purify the soul.

Self-Discipline

Pythagorean communities emphasized control over desires and a disciplined daily life. The purpose was not simply social obedience but the cultivation of a life ordered toward philosophical and religious goals.

Dietary Practices

Ancient sources associate Pythagoreanism with dietary restrictions, including abstention from certain foods. Vegetarianism is strongly associated with later descriptions of Pythagorean life, although ancient evidence concerning precisely what Pythagoras himself ate is not uniform.

Religious Practice

Religious rites and purification were important components of the Pythagorean tradition. The philosophical life was therefore closely connected with beliefs about the soul and its condition after death.

Pythagoras, Numbers, and the Nature of Reality

Pythagoreanism became famous for giving number a fundamental role in understanding the world. Later Pythagoreans argued that numerical relationships could reveal the structure and order of reality.

Aristotle reports that the Pythagoreans were impressed by the similarities they observed between things and numbers and came to regard numbers, or numerical principles, as fundamental to reality.

This idea is more philosophical than the simple statement that "everything is made of numbers." The Pythagorean tradition explored how order, proportion, limit, and numerical relationships might explain the structure of things.

It is important, however, to avoid automatically attributing the complete later Pythagorean theory of number to Pythagoras himself. Much of the detailed philosophical system known from ancient sources belongs to Pythagoreans who lived after him.

Pythagoras, Music, Number, and Harmony

Music played an important role in the development of Pythagorean thought. The Pythagorean tradition recognized that musical consonance can be described through simple numerical ratios.

The relationship between number and musical harmony became philosophically significant because it suggested that mathematical relationships could correspond to order perceived in the physical world.

This connection between number, proportion, and harmony became one of the most influential themes associated with Pythagoreanism. Later philosophers expanded the idea into broader theories concerning the order of the cosmos.

Stories describing Pythagoras as personally discovering every aspect of musical mathematics should nevertheless be treated cautiously because the historical evidence is too limited to establish exactly which discoveries belonged to him.

Did Pythagoras Believe in the Harmony of the Spheres?

The famous expression "harmony of the spheres" describes the later philosophical idea that the movements of celestial bodies are related to mathematical and musical harmony.

Pythagorean traditions associated the cosmos with numerical relationships and musical proportion, and ancient evidence connects Pythagorean thought with the idea of a harmonious cosmic order.

However, the familiar image of planets moving through literal musical spheres should not simply be presented as a direct quotation from Pythagoras. The detailed doctrine developed over time, and later Pythagorean and Platonic traditions contributed to the form in which the idea became famous.

Pythagoras and Mathematics

Pythagoras is probably most famous today for mathematics. His name is permanently attached to the Pythagorean theorem, one of the fundamental results of geometry.

Yet the historical question is more complicated than the popular story suggests. The theorem was known in mathematical traditions before the lifetime of Pythagoras, including ancient Babylonian mathematics.

There is also no surviving work by Pythagoras that demonstrates that he discovered or proved the theorem himself. Because Pythagorean communities practiced secrecy and later followers often attributed teachings to Pythagoras, it is difficult to separate his own mathematical work from that of later Pythagoreans.

The Pythagorean Theorem

The theorem states that for a right-angled triangle, the square of the hypotenuse is equal to the sum of the squares of the two shorter sides:

a² + b² = c²

The mathematical result was known before Pythagoras, but later Greek mathematical traditions connected it strongly with the Pythagoreans. Whether Pythagoras himself supplied the first proof remains uncertain.

Did Pythagoras Discover the Pythagorean Theorem?

We cannot establish that Pythagoras himself discovered or proved the theorem.

The mathematical relationship now called the Pythagorean theorem was already known in ancient Babylonian mathematics long before Pythagoras. Ancient mathematical traditions therefore show that the relationship itself did not originate with the Greek philosopher.

Later Greek tradition gave Pythagoras and the Pythagoreans an important role in the history of the theorem. Some historians have suggested that Pythagoras or his followers may have developed a proof, but the evidence does not allow us to identify a particular original proof by Pythagoras with confidence.

The safest historical statement is that the theorem is associated with Pythagoras and the Pythagorean mathematical tradition, while his personal contribution remains uncertain.

What Mathematics Is Associated with Pythagoreanism?

The Pythagorean tradition became associated with important developments in mathematics, especially the study of numerical relationships, geometry, proportion, and the mathematical structure of harmony.

Geometry

Pythagorean mathematics is associated with geometric reasoning and with the use of mathematical relationships to understand geometrical figures.

Numbers and Proportion

Pythagorean thinkers investigated properties of numbers and treated relationships between numbers as philosophically significant.

Irrational Quantities

The discovery that some geometrical magnitudes cannot be represented as ratios of whole numbers became an important development in the Pythagorean tradition. The discovery is associated with the Pythagoreans as a group, but it should not automatically be attributed to Pythagoras personally.

Mathematics as a Way of Understanding Order

The deeper importance of Pythagorean mathematics was philosophical: mathematical relationships appeared to reveal patterns of order, proportion, and structure within the world.

Pythagorean Symbols, Rules, and Secrecy

Ancient accounts describe the Pythagorean community as having rules, symbols, sayings, and practices that were not always openly explained to outsiders.

Some traditions describe rules concerning food, conduct, religious observances, and personal discipline. Other ancient sources preserve short symbolic sayings whose precise meaning is difficult to establish.

The famous story that Pythagoreans were forbidden to eat beans is an example of a tradition that became closely connected with Pythagoras. Ancient authors give different explanations for the rule, and the historical evidence does not allow us to state with confidence exactly what Pythagoras himself taught about beans.

The secrecy of the community also created a major historical problem: later generations could not always determine whether a particular doctrine originated with Pythagoras or with one of his followers.

Pythagoras, Religion, and Philosophy

Pythagoreanism cannot be understood solely as a theory about mathematics or nature. Religion and philosophy were closely connected within the Pythagorean way of life.

The purification of the soul, religious ritual, ethical discipline, and beliefs concerning life after death were central themes associated with Pythagoras.

This makes Pythagoras different from the modern image of a philosopher working only through abstract argument. For Pythagoras and his followers, philosophical understanding was connected with the transformation of the person who pursued it.

His tradition therefore occupies an important place between philosophy, religion, ethics, and mathematics in the intellectual history of ancient Greece.

Pythagoras' Influence on Plato and Greek Philosophy

Pythagorean ideas became important in the development of later Greek philosophy. The Pythagorean emphasis on mathematical order, the soul, purification, and the structure of reality provided themes that later philosophers could develop in different directions.

Plato was particularly influenced by mathematical and Pythagorean traditions, although Plato's philosophy cannot simply be identified with Pythagoreanism.

Later Pythagoreans, especially Philolaus and Archytas, developed mathematical and cosmological ideas in much greater detail than can be securely attributed to Pythagoras himself.

Pythagoreanism therefore became one of the important intellectual traditions that contributed to the development of later Greek philosophy, particularly discussions of mathematics, reality, the soul, and cosmic order.

What Is Pythagoreanism?

Pythagoreanism refers both to the philosophical and religious way of life associated with Pythagoras and to the later philosophical tradition developed by his followers.

This distinction is essential. The Pythagorean movement continued for generations after Pythagoras' death, and later thinkers developed mathematical, cosmological, metaphysical, and ethical theories that are often collectively called Pythagorean.

Consequently, statements such as "Pythagoras believed that everything is number" can be misleading if they are presented without qualification. Aristotle's detailed discussion of numbers concerns Pythagoreans as a group, while the surviving evidence for Pythagoras himself is much more limited.

Important Pythagorean Thinkers

  • Pythagoras — Founder and central figure of the tradition.
  • Philolaus — An important fifth-century Pythagorean associated with mathematical and cosmological theories.
  • Archytas — A later Pythagorean philosopher, mathematician, and political figure.

Did Pythagoras Write Any Books?

No surviving writings by Pythagoras are known.

This is one of the most important facts to understand when studying his philosophy. There is no surviving book, treatise, or philosophical dialogue that can be read as Pythagoras' own presentation of his ideas.

Later authors sometimes attributed writings to Pythagoras, but scholars have identified a number of these texts as pseudo-Pythagorean works—that is, writings composed later and falsely presented under his name.

Because of this, historians reconstruct Pythagoras by comparing early evidence and later reports rather than by reading a surviving work written by him.

How Do We Know About Pythagoras?

Our knowledge of Pythagoras comes from a complicated collection of ancient testimonies. Some of the earliest evidence comes from references by other Greek thinkers, while fuller biographies were written much later.

Herodotus, Plato, and Aristotle provide important evidence for the broader intellectual world surrounding Pythagoras and the Pythagoreans, although none provides a complete biography of Pythagoras.

Later authors such as Aristoxenus, Dicaearchus, Diogenes Laertius, Porphyry, and Iamblichus preserve additional information about Pythagorean life and traditions.

The problem is that these sources were written at different times and do not always agree. Some later biographies also portray Pythagoras as a miraculous or semi-divine figure.

Historical study therefore requires separating relatively early evidence from later legendary material.

What Do We Actually Know About Pythagoras?

Pythagoras is an excellent example of the difficulty of studying ancient philosophers. His influence was enormous, but the evidence about his personal philosophy is limited.

Relatively Well-Attested

  • Pythagoras was associated with Samos.
  • He later moved to Croton in southern Italy.
  • He established a community of followers with a distinctive way of life.
  • He was associated with beliefs concerning the immortality and transmigration of the soul.
  • He became a highly influential figure in the development of the Pythagorean tradition.

Traditionally Attributed

  • The discovery or proof of the Pythagorean theorem.
  • Numerous mathematical discoveries.
  • Detailed theories concerning cosmic harmony.
  • Extensive travels and learning from Egypt and the Near East.

Uncertain or Disputed

  • The exact mathematical achievements of Pythagoras himself.
  • The precise form of his cosmological ideas.
  • The exact rules followed by his original community.
  • Whether many later Pythagorean doctrines can be traced directly to Pythagoras.
  • The precise circumstances of his death.

How Did Pythagoras Die?

The circumstances of Pythagoras' death are uncertain. Ancient sources preserve several different accounts, which disagree about the events that led to his death.

One relatively important historical tradition connects political violence and attacks on the Pythagorean community at Croton with Pythagoras' departure from the city. According to this tradition, he eventually settled at Metapontum in southern Italy.

Later sources give different explanations for his death, including stories involving conflict, starvation, and the famous story of a bean field. These accounts should not be treated as equally reliable historical facts.

The safest conclusion is that Pythagoras died around 490 BCE, probably at Metapontum, while the exact circumstances remain disputed.

Why Is Pythagoras Important in Philosophy?

Pythagoras is important because he helped establish a distinctive vision in which philosophy, mathematics, religion, ethics, and the soul were closely connected.

Earlier Greek natural philosophers had focused heavily on questions about the fundamental nature of the physical world. Pythagorean traditions introduced a different emphasis: the possibility that mathematical relationships, moral order, and the purification of the soul were all connected to a deeper order of reality.

His influence was therefore not limited to mathematics. Pythagorean thought contributed themes that became important in later discussions of metaphysics, cosmology, ethics, mathematics, music, and the philosophy of the soul.

Pythagoras Explained Simply

Pythagoras was an ancient Greek philosopher who founded a distinctive community in southern Italy. His philosophy connected the disciplined life of the individual with ideas about the soul, religion, number, and the order of the cosmos.

He is famous today mainly because of the Pythagorean theorem, but his historical importance goes far beyond geometry.

Frequently Asked Questions About Pythagoras

Who was Pythagoras?

Pythagoras of Samos was an ancient Greek philosopher traditionally dated to approximately 570–490 BCE. He later established a philosophical and religious community at Croton in southern Italy.

What is Pythagoras famous for?

Pythagoras is famous today for the Pythagorean theorem, but historically he was also known for his teachings concerning the soul, religious practices, disciplined living, and the Pythagorean community.

What did Pythagoras believe?

Pythagoras is strongly associated with the immortality and transmigration of the soul, religious purification, disciplined living, and a philosophical tradition that gave importance to number and harmony.

What is Pythagoreanism?

Pythagoreanism is the philosophical and religious tradition associated with Pythagoras and the later thinkers who followed his teachings. Because the tradition continued for centuries, not every Pythagorean doctrine can be attributed directly to Pythagoras.

Did Pythagoras believe in reincarnation?

Pythagoras is traditionally associated with metempsychosis, the belief that the soul survives death and can pass into another living body. This is one of the ideas most strongly connected with the early Pythagorean tradition.

What is metempsychosis?

Metempsychosis is the transmigration of the soul after death from one living body to another. In Pythagorean tradition, the process could include movement between human and animal forms.

Did Pythagoras discover the Pythagorean theorem?

It cannot be established that Pythagoras personally discovered or proved the theorem. The mathematical relationship was known in ancient Babylonian mathematics before his lifetime. The theorem became strongly associated with Pythagoras and the Pythagorean mathematical tradition.

What is the Pythagorean theorem?

The Pythagorean theorem states that in a right-angled triangle, a² + b² = c², where c is the hypotenuse and a and b are the other two sides.

Did Pythagoras write any books?

No surviving writings by Pythagoras are known. Later works were sometimes attributed to him, but many such texts are regarded as later pseudo-Pythagorean writings.

Did Pythagoras believe that everything was made of numbers?

Pythagoreanism became strongly associated with the idea that numbers and numerical relationships are fundamental to reality. However, the detailed doctrine is primarily known from later Pythagorean thinkers, so it should not automatically be presented as a complete theory personally developed by Pythagoras.

Did Pythagoras believe in the harmony of the spheres?

Pythagorean traditions connected the cosmos with mathematical ratios and harmony. The familiar detailed doctrine known as the "harmony of the spheres" developed through later Pythagorean and Platonic traditions and cannot simply be treated as a surviving statement written by Pythagoras.

Where did Pythagoras live?

Pythagoras was born on Samos and later moved to Croton in southern Italy, where he established his influential community. He is also traditionally associated with Metapontum toward the end of his life.

Who were the Pythagoreans?

The Pythagoreans were followers of Pythagoras and members of the broader philosophical tradition that developed around him. Later Pythagoreans included thinkers such as Philolaus and Archytas.

Did Pythagoras influence Plato?

Pythagorean traditions were important to the intellectual environment in which Plato developed his philosophy. Pythagorean ideas concerning mathematics, the soul, and cosmic order became especially important in later interpretations of Plato, although Plato's philosophy should not simply be identified with Pythagoreanism.

Why is Pythagoras important today?

Pythagoras remains important because his name stands at the intersection of mathematics and philosophy. His tradition connected numerical order, music, ethics, religious practice, the soul, and the structure of the cosmos, influencing later Greek philosophy and the history of mathematics.

Why Pythagoras Still Matters

Pythagoras remains one of the most fascinating figures in ancient Greek philosophy because his legacy cannot be reduced to a single discipline. He stands at the meeting point of philosophy, mathematics, religion, ethics, music, and theories of the soul.

His historical image is complicated by the fact that he left no surviving writings and that later generations attributed many doctrines and discoveries to him. For that reason, studying Pythagoras also teaches an important lesson about the history of philosophy: the person we remember and the historical person we can reconstruct are not always identical.

Whether considering the immortality of the soul, the importance of numerical relationships, the connection between mathematics and harmony, or the disciplined philosophical life, Pythagoreanism helped establish questions that continued to influence Greek philosophy for centuries.

Pythagoras' greatest historical importance may therefore lie not simply in a theorem, but in the idea that the order of the world, the order of the soul, and the order of human life might be connected.
