Foucault's Power/Knowledge Explained With Examples

Michel Foucault’s concept of power/knowledge explains how power shapes what societies accept as true, while knowledge and institutions help organize and exercise power. This guide examines the relationship through practical examples from education, medicine, surveillance, law, and everyday social life.

Short answerPower/knowledge is Foucault's idea that power and knowledge are bound together: systems of power produce and use knowledge, and knowledge in turn gives power its reach. It differs from the slogan “knowledge is power” because Foucault claims power does not just use knowledge, it helps create what counts as truth.

What does power/knowledge mean?

Foucault's concept of power/knowledge means that power and knowledge are closely interconnected: knowledge helps produce and exercise power, while power relations influence what knowledge is produced, accepted, and treated as true. Developed across works such as Discipline and Punish (1975), The History of Sexuality, Volume 1 (1976), and the interviews collected in Power/Knowledge, the concept challenges the idea that knowledge exists independently of social and institutional relationships. Foucault argues that power does not operate only through laws, commands, or physical force. It also operates through practices such as observation, examination, classification, measurement, and documentation. These practices produce knowledge about individuals while simultaneously making them easier for institutions to monitor, compare, categorize, and regulate. Schools, hospitals, prisons, courts, and other institutions can therefore generate knowledge about people while also exercising disciplinary power over them.

Foucault does not mean that knowledge is simply false or that every form of knowledge is deliberately manipulated. Instead, he examines the historical and institutional conditions that determine what is recognized as legitimate knowledge, who has the authority to produce it, which methods are considered valid, and which statements become accepted as true. He describes these interconnected practices as part of a regime of truth. This relationship between power and knowledge is especially important to his analysis of the Panopticon. Continuous observation allows institutions to collect information about individuals, assess their conduct against established norms, and classify them accordingly. At the same time, the possibility of being observed can lead individuals to monitor and regulate their own behaviour. Power therefore operates not only by directly forcing people to obey, but also by shaping how they are observed, understood, evaluated, and encouraged to govern themselves. See the wider context in our guide to Foucault.

Three core claims

Foucault's analysis of power rests on three closely connected claims: power is productive rather than merely repressive, power is relational rather than simply possessed, and what a society accepts as truth is shaped by historically specific regimes of truth. These claims distinguish Foucault's account of power from a traditional view in which power is understood primarily as something possessed by a sovereign, government, ruling class, or institution and used to prohibit or punish behaviour.

  • Power is productive, not only repressive. Foucault argues that power does more than prohibit, censor, or punish. It also produces forms of knowledge, social categories, norms, skills, identities, and accepted ways of behaving. Institutions such as schools, prisons, hospitals, and workplaces can organize behaviour through examinations, classifications, records, routines, and standards of normality. In this sense, disciplinary power helps produce particular kinds of individuals who learn to understand and regulate themselves according to institutional expectations.
  • Power is relational. Foucault does not treat power simply as an object that one person or group permanently possesses. Power is exercised through relationships and networks involving institutions, officials, experts, teachers, doctors, prison staff, and individuals themselves. These relationships can change according to the situation, and individuals are not merely passive recipients of power. The Panopticon illustrates this principle: surveillance can operate through an institutional arrangement in which the possibility of observation influences behaviour even when no direct command or physical force is being used.
  • Truth is tied to "regimes of truth". Foucault argues that societies develop institutional procedures, accepted methods, professional authorities, and rules through which certain statements become recognized as true or legitimate. A regime of truth does not mean that all facts are invented or that truth is simply whatever those in power declare it to be. Rather, it refers to the historical systems through which societies produce, authorize, circulate, and distinguish accepted knowledge from claims regarded as false, illegitimate, or unscientific. Institutions therefore play an important role in determining who can speak with authority and which forms of knowledge are given social legitimacy.

These three claims are connected to Foucault's broader argument that where there is power, there is resistance. Resistance is not necessarily outside power relations; it arises within the same networks through which power operates. Individuals and groups can challenge classifications, norms, institutions, and accepted forms of knowledge, meaning that power relations are never completely fixed or closed.

Examples of power/knowledge

Foucault's concept of power/knowledge can be seen in many modern institutions where the production of knowledge is connected to the classification, supervision, and regulation of individuals. Psychiatry, medicine, prisons, schools, and the study of sexuality illustrate how observing and describing people can simultaneously create forms of knowledge and enable institutions to exercise power. In each case, Foucault is interested not simply in whether the knowledge produced is scientifically correct or incorrect, but in the institutional practices through which people become objects of observation, examination, comparison, and classification. In Madness and Civilization (1961), for example, he examined the historical separation and classification of madness and the institutional practices surrounding confinement. In The Birth of the Clinic (1963), he analyzed the development of clinical medicine and the clinical gaze, through which the patient became an object of systematic medical observation and documentation. These examples show how specialized knowledge can shape the categories through which individuals are understood while also supporting institutional practices for managing them.

Field How knowledge and power interact
Psychiatry Medical and psychiatric knowledge classifies forms of mental illness and abnormality, while psychiatric institutions can use these classifications to organize diagnosis, treatment, and confinement. Foucault examines the historical formation of these practices in Madness and Civilization (1961).
Medicine The clinical gaze organizes patients as objects of observation, examination, diagnosis, and medical documentation. The production of clinical knowledge is therefore connected to institutional practices for examining and treating patients, as discussed in The Birth of the Clinic (1963).
Prisons and criminology Observation, examination, case records, and criminological classifications produce knowledge about offenders. Foucault argues that these practices contribute to the construction of the "delinquent" as a particular social and institutional category, which can then become the object of continuing surveillance and correction.
Schools Examinations combine observation, documentation, comparison, and judgment. Students can be measured against established standards, ranked, classified, and identified as meeting or falling short of institutional norms. The examination therefore functions as both a method of producing knowledge about individuals and a technique of disciplinary power.
Sexuality In The History of Sexuality, Volume 1 (1976), Foucault examined how medical, psychiatric, legal, and other forms of expert discourse classified and discussed sexual behaviour and identities. These practices did not merely describe sexuality; they also contributed to the ways in which sexuality became an object of knowledge, regulation, and self-understanding.

These examples share a common mechanism: institutions make individuals visible, generate information about them, organize that information into categories, and use those categories to guide intervention or judgment. The resulting knowledge can then feed back into further observation and classification. A student's examination record can influence how the student is evaluated; a prisoner's case file can shape how an offender is classified; and a patient's medical record can determine how a condition is diagnosed and treated. Foucault's point is not that these practices are identical or that all institutional knowledge is illegitimate. Rather, he asks how particular forms of knowledge become connected to institutional authority and how those connections shape the ways individuals are understood and governed. This is the practical meaning of power/knowledge: knowledge can become an instrument through which conduct is organized, while the exercise of power creates conditions in which new knowledge about individuals is continuously produced.

The link to discipline

Power/knowledge is linked to discipline because disciplinary institutions produce detailed knowledge about individuals through observation, examination, classification, and record-keeping, and then use that knowledge to regulate their conduct. In Foucault's analysis, modern disciplinary power does not depend primarily on spectacular punishment or direct physical force; instead, it works through ordinary institutional practices that make individuals visible, measurable, and comparable. Schools keep attendance records, examination results, and evaluations; hospitals maintain medical histories and diagnostic records; prisons create files on prisoners and document their behaviour; and other organizations similarly collect information that can be used to assess individuals against established norms. Observation therefore produces information, while that information allows individuals to be classified, evaluated, corrected, or subjected to further supervision. The individual becomes a documented and knowable subject whose behaviour can be monitored and compared over time. In this sense, disciplinary power and knowledge reinforce one another: institutions gain knowledge by observing people, and that knowledge makes it possible to exercise more detailed and continuous forms of discipline.

Foucault's Panopticon provides the clearest illustration of this relationship because its architectural arrangement makes observation potentially continuous while keeping the observed individual visible. The important effect is not simply that an authority watches people, but that the possibility of observation can encourage individuals to monitor and regulate their own behaviour. Surveillance generates information about conduct; that information can be used to classify and judge individuals against established norms; and those judgments can lead to correction, further supervision, or additional observation. The process can therefore become a continuous cycle: observation produces knowledge, knowledge supports discipline, and discipline produces further knowledge about the individual. This is why the Panopticon matters beyond prison architecture in Foucault's analysis. It represents a broader model of disciplinary power in which visibility becomes a means of control and individuals can gradually internalize the expectations against which they are observed. Rather than requiring constant physical force, disciplinary power can operate by arranging conditions in which people regulate themselves because they know they may be observed, examined, evaluated, and recorded.

Common misunderstandings

“Foucault says truth does not exist.” This is a common simplification of Foucault's argument. Foucault does not simply claim that there is no truth or that every statement is equally true. His concern is with the historical and institutional processes through which particular claims become accepted as true, authoritative, or scientifically legitimate. He examines who is authorized to produce knowledge, which methods are accepted as valid, which institutions circulate particular claims, and how societies distinguish accepted knowledge from claims regarded as false or illegitimate. His concept of a regime of truth therefore refers to the institutional practices, procedures, authorities, and rules through which truth is produced, organized, and circulated within a society. The focus is not simply on whether an individual statement corresponds to reality, but on the conditions that allow certain forms of knowledge to acquire authority and influence how people understand themselves and others.

“Power is just oppression.” Foucault's understanding of power is broader than the idea that power simply suppresses or oppresses individuals. He argues that power can be productive: it produces categories, norms, forms of knowledge, identities, institutions, and patterns of behaviour while also restricting certain possibilities. Disciplinary power, for example, does not merely punish people who break rules; through observation, examination, classification, and normalization it helps establish standards against which people understand and regulate themselves. Power is also relational, meaning that it operates through networks of social relationships rather than belonging permanently to a single ruler or institution. Importantly, Foucault argues that where there is power, there is resistance. Resistance is therefore not something completely outside power relations but emerges within them, allowing individuals and groups to challenge established norms, classifications, practices, and forms of authority.

“Power/knowledge is the same as ‘knowledge is power’.” Although the two phrases sound similar, they express different ideas. The familiar saying “knowledge is power” generally suggests that possessing information or expertise gives a person an advantage that can then be used to exercise power. Foucault's concept of power/knowledge makes a stronger and more interconnected claim: power relations help shape the production of knowledge, while knowledge provides ways of observing, classifying, governing, and regulating people. For example, an examination does not merely give an institution information about a student; it creates a documented record through which the student can be compared, ranked, classified, and evaluated against a norm. Similarly, medical, psychiatric, or criminological practices can produce categories through which individuals are understood and managed. Power and knowledge are therefore not two completely separate things, with knowledge simply serving as a weapon that someone already possesses. Foucault examines the historical systems in which the production of knowledge and the exercise of power continually interact and reinforce one another.

Frequently asked questions

What is power/knowledge in simple words?

The idea that who has power shapes what counts as knowledge, and what counts as knowledge in turn supports certain kinds of power.

Where did Foucault introduce power/knowledge?

Mainly in Discipline and Punish (1975) and The History of Sexuality, Vol. 1 (1976), with interviews in Power/Knowledge.

Is power/knowledge the same as ‘knowledge is power’?

No. The older slogan says knowledge gives power. Foucault argues that power and knowledge are mutually constitutive.

What is a regime of truth?

The set of institutions, rules and practices that decide what is accepted as true in a society.
