What Nietzsche Would Make of Doomscrolling

Nietzsche never saw a smartphone. But his diagnosis of nihilism raises an unsettling question about what happens when distraction becomes a way of living.

A necessary distinction: Nietzsche did not write about smartphones, social media, algorithms, or doomscrolling. The comparison in this essay is therefore interpretive rather than historical. The question is whether some of Nietzsche's concepts—especially nihilism, affirmation, and self-overcoming—help us think about a contemporary habit of endless digital distraction.

The Line Everyone Thinks They Understand

Nietzsche 's most famous sentence is also one of his most frequently simplified: "God is dead." It is easy to turn the phrase into a victory slogan for atheism, as though Nietzsche were announcing that science had defeated religion and that the intellectual problem was now finished. The passage is much darker. In The Gay Science , the declaration appears in the speech of a madman who enters a marketplace and asks where God has gone. The scene is not written in the tone of a celebration. It is a cultural alarm.

Nietzsche's point is not merely that fewer people believe in God. His concern is what follows when a religious and metaphysical framework that once organized questions of truth, morality, purpose, and ultimate value loses its authority. A civilization can continue using inherited moral language long after the intellectual foundations that once supported it have weakened. The resulting problem is therefore not simply theological. It is a problem of valuation: if the old source of value no longer commands belief, what gives our values their authority?

What the Madman Is Actually Saying

The famous madman in The Gay Science §125 does something important: he describes the death of God as an event whose consequences have not yet been fully understood by the people around him. The crowd is not ready for the announcement. Nietzsche's dramatic language therefore points beyond a private change of belief toward a historical transformation in the conditions under which European culture understands meaning and value.

This is why "God is dead" should not be reduced to the proposition "God does not exist." Nietzsche is also interested in the collapse of a framework that had supplied an apparently objective order of values. Once that framework becomes unstable, the danger is not automatically freedom. It can also be disorientation, resentment, exhaustion, or nihilism. The difficult philosophical task begins after the inherited answer stops functioning.

The Nietzschean question is not simply whether an old belief has disappeared. It is what kind of person, culture, and value-system can emerge once it has.

Nihilism Is More Than "Nothing Matters"

Nihilism in Nietzsche is not best understood as a casual feeling of boredom or the claim that every individual activity is pointless. It names a crisis in valuation: the highest values can lose their force, while the world no longer appears to possess the kind of overarching meaning those values once promised. Nietzsche treats this crisis as something to be confronted rather than hidden beneath reassuring formulas.

That distinction matters because nihilism can become psychological as well as intellectual. If inherited values no longer persuade but no new orientation has been developed, a person can remain caught between disbelief and dependence on the very standards they no longer fully accept. Nietzsche's response is not to pretend that the crisis does not exist. His larger project asks what forms of valuation and life-affirmation could become possible after the collapse of unquestioned absolutes.

Why the Infinite Feed Makes a Nietzschean Comparison

Now consider the modern phenomenon of doomscrolling: repeatedly moving through an apparently endless stream of alarming, trivial, entertaining, or emotionally provocative material, often without a clearly chosen purpose or natural stopping point. The comparison with Nietzsche becomes interesting when the feed is treated not as the cause of nihilism, but as a possible way of responding to discomfort, uncertainty, and the absence of a satisfying answer to the question of what matters.

An endless feed can provide stimulation without requiring commitment. There is always another story, another argument, another image, another notification, or another piece of commentary. The user can remain occupied while postponing the harder questions: What deserves my attention? What am I trying to build? Which values actually organize my life? What am I willing to sacrifice for something I consider worthwhile?

That is where the Nietzschean analogy becomes philosophically useful. The point is not that scrolling is nihilism. It is that distraction can become a way of avoiding the work of valuation. A person does not need to consciously believe that "nothing matters" in order to live in a way that continually avoids deciding what matters.

Nietzsche Did Not Predict the Algorithm

It would be a mistake to claim that Nietzsche somehow foresaw social media, smartphones, or algorithmic recommendation systems. He lived in the nineteenth century and was responding to very different intellectual and cultural conditions. The modern attention economy has its own technological, economic, and psychological history, none of which can simply be read out of Nietzsche.

What can be borrowed is a philosophical method: examine the values and habits hidden inside an apparently ordinary practice. A feed is not philosophically interesting merely because it is new technology. It becomes interesting when we ask what repeated use of that technology trains us to expect from experience: constant novelty, immediate stimulation, endless commentary, rapid judgment, or the avoidance of silence.

This is an application of Nietzschean ideas to a contemporary phenomenon, not a claim about Nietzsche's intentions. The distinction matters if the philosophy is going to remain historically responsible.

Passive Nihilism and the Problem of Drift

Nietzsche's discussion of nihilism includes a distinction between more passive and more active responses to the collapse of inherited values. A passive response can take the form of exhaustion, resignation, distraction, or the desire to make the problem disappear rather than confronting it. An active response, by contrast, involves struggle with the problem of valuation itself: the individual does not simply wait for an old source of meaning to return.

Doomscrolling becomes relevant here only as an analogy. Imagine a person who feels restless or uncertain and automatically reaches for a feed whenever the discomfort appears. The feed does not answer the underlying question. It simply makes it possible to postpone the question for another few minutes, then another few minutes after that. The problem is therefore not the existence of distraction. Nietzsche's challenge is sharper: what happens when distraction becomes the habitual answer to the experience of uncertainty?

The important word is habit . A single evening of mindless scrolling tells us very little about a person's philosophy. A repeated pattern that steadily displaces reflection, creation, friendship, study, work, or deliberate choice is philosophically more revealing because it begins to shape the person doing it.

Self-Overcoming Is Harder Than Deleting an App

Nietzsche's alternative to passive resignation is not simply "be productive." His language of self-overcoming points toward a more demanding transformation: the individual must work on the forces, habits, inherited assumptions, and values that shape how life is lived. The goal is not to become a perfectly optimized machine. It is to become capable of giving form to one's existence rather than merely inheriting every value from the surrounding culture.

That makes the familiar anti-phone conclusion too shallow. Removing one app can change a behavior without answering the deeper question of what the freed time is for. If the silence created by the missing feed is immediately filled with another form of passive consumption, the underlying problem has not necessarily changed. Nietzsche's philosophy pushes further: what are you going to do with the freedom created when distraction no longer decides what deserves your attention?

In this sense, self-overcoming is not a productivity hack. It is a question of formation. The issue is what kind of person your repeated choices are making you. A philosophy of self-overcoming therefore asks not only what you consume, but what you practice, what you endure, what you create, and what you repeatedly choose to affirm.

Amor Fati and the Demand for Affirmation

Another Nietzschean idea helps sharpen the contrast: amor fati , often translated as "love of fate." The phrase expresses an affirmative orientation toward one's life rather than a constant wish to escape the conditions in which one finds oneself. It belongs to Nietzsche's broader concern with affirmation: can a person say yes to life without needing reality to become painless, perfect, or guaranteed by some higher order?

Doomscrolling can sometimes function as the opposite kind of movement—not because every use of a phone is an escape, but because endless stimulation can make it easy to avoid being alone with an experience that is unfinished, difficult, boring, or uncertain. The feed keeps supplying something else before the previous thought has had time to become a judgment, a project, or a decision.

A Nietzschean response would therefore not be "never distract yourself." It would be more demanding: notice whether distraction is serving your life or preventing you from engaging with it. Affirmation requires contact with the world as it is, including difficulty and uncertainty. It cannot be achieved simply by replacing one comforting slogan with another.

What a Nietzschean Response Might Look Like

If the comparison is taken seriously, the answer is not a universal digital detox. Nietzsche does not provide a nineteenth-century theory of app limits, and importing one into his philosophy would create a doctrine he never wrote. What his thought can provide is a set of questions for examining one's own habits.

  • What am I using distraction to avoid? Is the feed relieving ordinary boredom, or repeatedly helping me avoid a difficult decision?
  • What values determine my attention? Am I choosing what deserves attention, or simply responding to whatever arrives most forcefully?
  • What am I creating? Does my daily attention leave room for study, friendship, art, work, reflection, or another activity I genuinely value?
  • Can I tolerate silence? If every unoccupied moment has to be filled, what experiences or thoughts never get the chance to develop?
  • What kind of person is the habit producing? Nietzsche repeatedly turns philosophical attention toward the formation of character rather than isolated opinions.

These questions turn the phone from the villain of the story into an object of philosophical examination. The technology matters, but the deeper issue is the relationship between attention, habit, value, and agency.

The Deeper Question: What Are You Training Yourself to Value?

This is where Nietzsche becomes more uncomfortable than the usual "put your phone away" advice. The problem is not simply that digital platforms steal time. It is that repeated attention can participate in the formation of desire. What we return to again and again becomes familiar; what becomes familiar can begin to feel important; and what feels important can start to organize our priorities.

Nietzsche's philosophy is deeply concerned with this relationship between values, habits, drives, and forms of life. He does not treat the individual as a perfectly transparent rational chooser who simply decides what to value once and for all. Human beings are shaped by forces they do not always understand. That makes the ordinary act of choosing what to attend to philosophically significant.

The strongest version of the doomscrolling question is therefore not "Is social media bad?" It is: what kind of life is being practiced when distraction becomes the easiest response to uncertainty? That question can be asked without pretending Nietzsche had the answer to modern technology. It also avoids the opposite mistake of treating every moment of digital leisure as evidence of nihilism.

Nietzsche's Challenge to the Infinite Feed

Nietzsche's "God is dead" is ultimately a challenge about what happens after an inherited source of meaning loses its unquestioned authority. Nihilism is one possible response. Resignation and distraction can be others. Nietzsche's own philosophical project pushes toward a more difficult possibility: the creation, transformation, and affirmation of values capable of giving shape to a life.

Doomscrolling is not a nineteenth-century example of nihilism, and Nietzsche did not secretly predict the smartphone. But his philosophy gives us a useful lens for examining why an endless stream can become more than entertainment. If a feed repeatedly allows us to avoid deciding what matters, then the philosophical issue is no longer the phone itself. It is whether we are still actively participating in the formation of our lives.

The question Nietzsche leaves us with is therefore harder than "How much screen time is too much?" It is closer to this: what are your habits of attention teaching you to value?

Frequently Asked Questions

What did Nietzsche mean by "God is dead"?

Nietzsche was describing a cultural and philosophical crisis, not merely celebrating atheism. The phrase points to the loss of authority of a shared religious framework that had supplied important answers about meaning, morality, and ultimate value. The resulting problem is what human beings will do when those inherited foundations no longer command the same credibility.

Is "God is dead" the same as saying that God does not exist?

No. The statement can include Nietzsche's rejection of traditional Christian belief, but its philosophical force is broader. In The Gay Science §125, the madman's announcement concerns the cultural consequences of the collapse of a previously authoritative framework of value. It is therefore a diagnosis of a historical condition as much as a theological claim.

What is nihilism according to Nietzsche?

Nihilism concerns a crisis in value in which previously highest or organizing values lose their authority or appear unable to provide a convincing justification for meaning. Nietzsche treats nihilism as a serious cultural problem rather than simply the mood of an individual who feels bored or pessimistic.

Would Nietzsche say doomscrolling is nihilism?

We cannot know that, because Nietzsche never discussed smartphones, social media, or algorithmic feeds. A more defensible claim is that doomscrolling can sometimes be interpreted through Nietzsche's account of passive responses to nihilism: endless distraction may become a way of postponing questions about value rather than confronting them. That is a modern application of Nietzsche, not a direct quotation of his view.

What is the difference between passive and active nihilism?

In Nietzsche's discussion, passive responses to nihilism involve exhaustion, resignation, or retreat in the face of the collapse of inherited values. Active responses involve confronting the crisis and participating in the difficult work of transformation and revaluation. The distinction is more demanding than simply replacing negative thoughts with positive ones.

What does amor fati mean in Nietzsche's philosophy?

Amor fati means "love of fate." It expresses an affirmative stance toward one's life and circumstances rather than wishing reality away or treating suffering as evidence that life itself is defective. It fits Nietzsche's wider concern with life-affirmation and the possibility of saying yes to existence without relying on an escape into another world.
