Absurdism

Absurdism starts with an awkward fact: people need their lives to mean something, and the universe doesn't seem to care either way. The philosophy took its clearest shape in the work of Albert Camus in the 1940s, though writers had been circling the same problem for a century before him. Its answer is stubborn rather than comforting. Don't pretend the silence is an answer, don't give up on living because of it, and find a way to carry on with your eyes open.[1]

What Is Absurdism?

Absurdism is the view that the human longing for meaning, order and purpose runs into a universe that stays silent about all three. The "absurd" isn't located in the world alone, and it isn't located in us alone. It's the gap between what we keep asking for and what we actually get back. Camus put it simply: the absurd is born from the confrontation between the human need and the unreasonable silence of the world.[1] Take away either side and the problem disappears. A creature that didn't care about meaning wouldn't be bothered, and a universe that handed out answers wouldn't leave us hanging.

What makes absurdism different from a lot of gloomy philosophy is what it asks you to do next. It rejects the leap to religion or ideology that would close the gap, and it rejects despair and suicide that would walk away from it. What's left is revolt: keep living, keep wanting meaning, refuse to fake it, and find a kind of freedom in that honesty. Camus even suggested we should picture Sisyphus as happy.[1][2] Whether that's convincing is something people argue about, and we'll get to that.

In this sense, absurdism is less about finding a final explanation for existence and more about learning to live without one. It does not promise that suffering will make sense, that every question has an answer, or that life will eventually reveal some hidden purpose. Instead, it asks us to face those uncertainties without turning away from them. We may never settle the questions that trouble us, but we can still love, create, explore, and take responsibility for the lives we have. That tension between the need for answers and the decision to live without them is where absurdism finds its meaning.

History and Etymology of Absurdism

Also read: Explore more history History of philosophy traces how thinkers and philosophical traditions have explored questions about reality, knowledge, morality, and human existence across different periods and cultures. Read article →

Absurdism has a short history as a named philosophy and a long one as a feeling. The word itself comes from music before it comes from thought, and the problem it describes is much older than the twentieth century. The sections below follow the word, then the ideas that fed into it, then the moment in the 1940s when it finally got a clear voice.

Etymological Origins of the Word Absurd

"Absurd" comes from the Latin absurdus, which meant something like out of tune, discordant, or senseless. The root usually given is surdus, "deaf" or "dull to sound," so the original picture is almost literally a note that doesn't fit.[3] English borrowed it in the sixteenth century, where it settled into the everyday sense we still use: ridiculous, illogical, contrary to reason.

That musical background is oddly useful. When philosophers talk about the absurd, they're describing a mismatch, two things that ought to harmonise and don't. A mind that wants reasons meets a world that gives none, and the result is a clash rather than a tidy contradiction. Camus gave the word a technical meaning, but it still carries that sense of something sounding wrong.A

Philosophical Origins and Early Influences

Nobody founded absurdism the way Zeno founded Stoicism. It grew out of earlier thinkers who bumped into the same wall. Søren Kierkegaard, writing in nineteenth-century Denmark, argued that human reason can't reach the certainty faith asks for, and that believing anyway takes a leap against the evidence.[6] Dostoevsky, in Notes from Underground, gave us a narrator who hates the idea that life should be tidy and rational, and who would rather act out of spite than be reduced to a sum.[10] Neither man was an absurdist, but both mapped parts of the ground.

The other big ingredient was the general shock of modern life. Science had pushed humans out of the centre of the universe, religion no longer settled every question for everyone, and the wars and mass politics of the early twentieth century made reassuring stories harder to believe. Existentialist writers were already asking how to live without a ready-made purpose. Absurdism took that question and gave it a sharper, more stubborn answer.[15]

The Emergence of Absurdism in the 20th Century

The decisive year is 1942. In occupied France, Albert Camus published both The Myth of Sisyphus, his essay on the absurd, and The Stranger, a novel about a man who lives it without ever naming it.[1][7] The essay opens with a startling claim: the one truly serious philosophical problem is whether life is worth living. From there Camus works out what honesty about the absurd requires. This is the point where absurdism becomes something you can name and argue with.

After the war the mood spread to the stage. Beckett, Ionesco and others wrote plays in which conversation breaks down, plots go nowhere, and people wait for something that never arrives. In 1960 the critic Martin Esslin gave this movement its label, the "Theatre of the Absurd," and his book of the following year made the term stick.[5] Then in 1971 the American philosopher Thomas Nagel published an essay called "The Absurd" that pulled the idea into mainstream analytic philosophy, though with a more relaxed conclusion than Camus's.[4]

1950

Ionesco's The Bald Soprano opens in Paris, an early landmark of what would later be called the Theatre of the Absurd.[13]

1951

Camus publishes The Rebel, moving from the personal question of the absurd to the political question of revolt.[9]

1957

Camus is awarded the Nobel Prize in Literature at the age of 44.[7]

Camus dies in a car accident in January 1960. Martin Esslin names the "Theatre of the Absurd" the same year, and his book on the subject follows in 1961.[5]

1971

Thomas Nagel's essay "The Absurd" brings the idea into academic philosophy, arguing that the absurd can be met with irony rather than despair.[4]

Major Thinkers and Writers Associated with Absurdism

Also read: Explore more thinkers Philosophical thinkers are the individuals whose ideas have shaped the study of reality, knowledge, ethics, politics, and human existence across different cultures and periods. Read article →

Absurdism is an unusual school because so many of its key figures were novelists and playwrights rather than professional philosophers. That's not an accident. The absurd is something you feel before you argue about it, and stories can make you feel it in a way a syllogism can't. Only Camus tried to build a full philosophy around it. The others are included because they explored the same collision from different angles, and several of them would have rejected the label if you'd offered it to them.

Albert Camus

Camus (1913–1960) is the centre of the whole topic. Born into a poor family in French Algeria, he worked as a journalist and editor, joined the Resistance press in occupied France, and became one of the best-known writers in postwar Europe. His essay The Myth of Sisyphus lays out the philosophy of the absurd, while novels like The Stranger and The Plague show it at work in ordinary lives.[1][7]

He also kept refusing the title "existentialist," even though the press kept applying it.B His concerns were more earthy than abstract: sunlight, the sea, physical life, and the stubborn dignity of people who keep going. Later he moved from the absurd toward questions of solidarity and political revolt, which is where The Rebel comes in. His page on this site covers his life in more detail. Read about Albert Camus.

Søren Kierkegaard

Kierkegaard (1813–1855) is the odd one out, because he was a deeply committed Christian. What links him to absurdism is his honesty about how strange belief is. In works like Fear and Trembling, he describes faith as something that can't be justified by reason and that means giving up on certainty, as in the story of Abraham told to sacrifice his son.[6] He uses the word "absurd" himself to describe that demand.

Camus read Kierkegaard closely and admired the way he refused easy comfort. But he parted ways at the crucial point. For Kierkegaard the absurd is a doorway to God, while Camus saw that same step as a kind of "philosophical suicide," an escape that gives up on the tension instead of living inside it.[1]C So Kierkegaard supplies the problem, and Camus rejects his solution.

Fyodor Dostoevsky

Dostoevsky (1821–1881) never wrote about "the absurd" as a philosophy, but his fiction is full of people stuck in it. The narrator of Notes from Underground is a petty, brilliant, self-lacerating man who insists on his right to act irrationally, just to prove that he's not a piano key being played by the laws of nature.[10] In The Brothers Karamazov, Ivan's argument about innocent suffering poses the problem of meaning in its harshest form.

Camus admired him enormously and even adapted The Possessed for the stage. Still, Dostoevsky is best described as an influence rather than a member of the school. His characters wrestle with absurd questions, yet he himself kept searching for an answer in Christian faith, which puts him closer to Kierkegaard than to Camus.D

Franz Kafka

Kafka (1883–1924) wrote stories where the rules are real but nobody will tell you what they are. In The Trial, Josef K. is arrested one morning, never told what he's accused of, and gradually pulled into a process that has its own relentless logic and no explanation.[11] In The Metamorphosis, a man wakes up as an insect and his family's main worry is how it affects the household budget. The tone is calm, and that calm is what makes it unsettling.

Camus wrote an essay on Kafka, "Hope and the Absurd in the Work of Franz Kafka," included as an appendix to The Myth of Sisyphus. In it he reads Kafka as a master of the absurd, while worrying that Kafka's work leaves a door open for hope that Camus thought undermined it.[1] Whether you agree or not, the word "Kafkaesque" has become our shorthand for exactly this feeling of faceless, pointless bureaucracy.

Samuel Beckett

Beckett (1906–1989), an Irish writer who lived mostly in France, is best known for Waiting for Godot, first staged in Paris in 1953.[12] Two men wait by a tree for someone named Godot, who never comes. They bicker, joke, consider leaving, and stay. Very little "happens," and yet audiences have found the play unexpectedly moving, partly because the waiting feels so familiar.

Beckett was reluctant to explain what his work meant, and he wasn't a philosopher of the absurd in Camus's sense. But Godot and later works like Endgame present the human situation with almost no decoration: language that fails, routines that continue anyway, and a stubborn, comic persistence. One of the most quoted lines in his later fiction, "I can't go on, I'll go on," sums up that attitude better than any commentary.

Eugène Ionesco

Ionesco (1909–1994), a Romanian-French playwright, began writing after becoming fascinated by how empty the stock phrases in an English language textbook were. That became The Bald Soprano (1950), where two couples trade polite nonsense and the conversation slides into pure noise.[13] Language, in his plays, stops being a bridge between people and becomes a kind of machinery running on its own.

His later play Rhinoceros pushes this into political territory: one by one the residents of a town turn into rhinoceroses, and the lone holdout is the confused, unheroic Bérenger. It's usually read as a comment on conformity and the appeal of mass movements. Like Beckett, Ionesco is counted among the Theatre of the Absurd writers, even though he approached it through comedy and satire rather than through argument.[5]

Core Principles and Concepts of Absurdism

Also read: Explore metaphysics Metaphysics is the branch of philosophy concerned with the nature of reality, including existence, causation, time, and the fundamental structure of the world. Read article →

Absurdism isn't a long list of doctrines. It's closer to one problem and a handful of reactions to it. Camus works through them in a fairly fixed order: first the problem itself, then what we can honestly say about meaning and reason, then the three things he thinks follow from taking the problem seriously, which are revolt, freedom and passion.[1] The sections below take them one at a time. Read them in order the first time, because each one leans on the last.

The Absurd

The absurd is not a mood and it's not a property of the universe. It describes a relationship: a mind that demands reasons, set against a world that doesn't supply them. Camus is careful on this point. If you look only at the world, you find something indifferent but not absurd. If you look only at the human mind, you find a hunger but no contradiction. The absurd only appears when the two are held together.[1]

That's why he thinks the absurd can't be "solved." You can only end it by removing one side, either by lowering your expectations of the world or by convincing yourself the world has answered after all. Both moves, he says, are ways of looking away. The first task of absurdism is simply to keep looking, and to notice how rarely we do.

Meaning and Meaninglessness

A common misreading is that absurdism declares life meaningless. What it actually claims is narrower: there is no meaning handed to us from outside, no cosmic script we can read off and check. That leaves open whether meaning can be made, felt or chosen, and Camus never denies that people find their lives rich and worth living. His quarrel is with the idea that such meaning is guaranteed.[1]

So the useful distinction is between meaning that is given and meaning that is lived. A friendship, a piece of work, a long walk in good weather can all matter intensely without being justified by anything beyond themselves. Absurdism asks us to accept that they matter anyway, which is harder than it sounds, because we keep wanting a receipt.

Reason and Its Limits

Camus doesn't dismiss reason. He uses it relentlessly, and the whole essay is an argument. His point is that reason, pushed far enough, runs out. We can describe the world, predict it and build on it, but when we ask why anything exists at all, or whether our lives are justified, reason has no final answer to offer.[1]

What he objects to is the leap that follows. Kierkegaard turns to faith, other thinkers turn to grand systems, and Camus calls this "philosophical suicide" because it ends the inquiry by surrendering the very faculty that started it.C The absurdist position is to stay at the limit, admit that reason has reached its edge, and refuse to pretend the edge is a doorway.

Revolt Against the Absurd

Having ruled out escape, Camus asks what's left, and his answer is revolt. He doesn't mean violence or a political uprising here. He means a constant, conscious refusal: to accept the world's silence as final, and to refuse just as firmly the easy exits of belief or suicide. It's a way of living in which the tension is never resolved and never ignored.[1]

This is the part of the philosophy people often find most surprising, because it's neither cheerful nor bleak. It treats the absurd as something like a permanent opponent you stay in the ring with. The point isn't to win. The point is that the contest keeps you awake, and being awake is what Camus values.

Freedom and the Absurd

Once you stop expecting the universe to hand you a purpose, a particular kind of freedom opens up. Camus isn't talking about political liberty. He means freedom from the idea that your life has to fit some assigned pattern, or be justified by a future goal before it counts. If no one is keeping score, you can look at your own life without the pressure of someone else's rules.[1]

It's a modest freedom with real limits. You still have a body, a history and obligations, and Camus doesn't pretend otherwise. What changes is your attitude: you stop treating ordinary days as preparation for something that will finally explain them, and begin treating them as the thing itself.

Passion and the Present

If there's no promised future that redeems the present, then the present is where all the value has to be. Camus argues that the absurd person should aim for the greatest quantity of experience rather than the highest imagined quality, and should live as fully as the moment allows. He sketches figures such as the seducer, the actor and the conqueror, not as models to copy, but as people who commit completely to what's in front of them.[1]

Read uncharitably, that sounds like a recipe for chasing thrills. Read properly, it's about attention. A life lived with full attention to what is actually happening is different from one spent waiting for meaning to arrive, and that difference is what Camus is pointing at.

Albert Camus and the Philosophy of the Absurd

Most people who know absurdism know it through Camus, and three of his books carry the argument: an essay, a novel and a longer work on rebellion. They aren't three separate theories. They're three stages of one line of thought, moving from the private problem of the absurd, to a life lived inside it, to what it means for how we treat each other.[8]

The Myth of Sisyphus

The Myth of Sisyphus (1942) is a short philosophical essay that begins with a blunt claim: judging whether life is worth living is the fundamental question of philosophy, and everything else comes after it. Camus then examines why people might say no, and why he thinks they're wrong to. The essay introduces the absurd, rejects suicide and false hope as responses, and builds toward revolt, freedom and passion.[1]

It's worth reading rather than just reading about. The writing is unusually direct for philosophy, and he spends as much time on examples, such as the actor and the traveller, as on argument. The title story at the end, a retelling of the Greek myth, gives the essay its famous image.

The Stranger (The Outsider)

The Stranger (1942, sometimes titled The Outsider in English) follows Meursault, a clerk in Algiers who attends his mother's funeral without performing the expected grief, drifts into a relationship, and ends up shooting a man on a beach. At his trial the prosecution cares less about the killing than about the fact that he didn't cry at the funeral.[7]

The novel is often read as the absurd in narrative form. Society demands that Meursault perform meaning on cue, and he won't fake it. Whether you find him admirable or chilling is part of the book's design. In the last pages, facing execution, he finally opens himself to what Camus calls the "gentle indifference of the world," which is about as close to peace as the story offers.

The Rebel

The Rebel (1951) carries the argument beyond the individual. If the absurd means we must refuse both despair and false answers, what does that imply when we face injustice or oppression? Camus argues that real rebellion starts from the recognition of a limit that shouldn't be crossed, and that it contains an implicit claim of value shared with others.[9]

He summed it up in a line that became famous: "I rebel, therefore we are." The book also criticises revolutions that justify any cruelty in the name of a future goal, and that criticism helped cause his public break with Sartre and the French left. It's the most openly political thing he wrote, and it shows the absurd turning into ethics.

Sisyphus and the Meaning of Happiness

In the Greek myth, Sisyphus is condemned to roll a boulder up a hill, only to watch it roll back down, forever. Camus makes him the hero of the absurd because he is conscious of his fate. The moment of real interest for Camus isn't the climb but the walk back down, when Sisyphus sees exactly what his situation is and carries on anyway.[1]

Then comes the line everyone quotes: "One must imagine Sisyphus happy." The claim isn't that the punishment is secretly pleasant. It's that happiness doesn't depend on the task having an ultimate point. If the struggle itself can occupy a person completely, then the lack of an external reward doesn't cancel the value of the effort. You may find that convincing or you may think it's a clever way of dodging the question, and critics have said both.

Camus's Concept of Revolt

Camus uses "revolt" in a specific sense that runs through both the essay and The Rebel. In the earlier work, it's a personal stance: refusing to reconcile yourself to the silence of the world while still choosing to live in it. In the later work, it becomes a shared one, a revolt that defends human dignity and sets limits on what can be done in the name of any cause.[1][9]

The two versions are linked by the same instinct. If you accept that nothing guarantees meaning, you're less likely to sacrifice real people for an abstract future. That's why Camus's absurdism tends to end in solidarity rather than isolation, a point readers often miss when they only know the Sisyphus image.

The Metacognitive Dimension of the Absurd

Philosophers still disagree about one question that sounds technical but matters a lot: does a person have to know they're in an absurd situation for it to count as absurd? "Metacognitive" simply means thinking about your own thinking. Here it refers to the awareness of the clash between how seriously we take our lives and how little the universe seems to care. The debate is about whether that awareness is part of what makes something absurd, or just one way of noticing it.E

Awareness as Part of the Absurd

Some philosophers argue that the absurd needs a mind that can step back and see itself. Thomas Nagel's well-known essay is the usual reference: we pour effort into our projects, and we can also imagine looking at that effort from far outside, where it seems arbitrary. The absurd lives in the gap between those two viewpoints, and we can only occupy both because we're self-aware.[2][14]

There's a pleasing side effect to this view. It explains why we call human ambitions absurd but don't say the same about a rock or an ant. Neither can notice the mismatch, so neither is caught in it. Supporters also stress that awareness doesn't dissolve the problem: you can see exactly why your concerns look small from far away and still care about your Tuesday deadlines. The tension survives being understood.[4]

Objections to the Awareness Requirement

Other philosophers think the requirement makes the absurd too small. If it only applies to people who clearly see the contradiction, then most of humanity is spared, and the absurd becomes a private club for people who read philosophy. That seems backwards, since a life can be out of proportion to the world whether or not its owner has noticed.[2][14]

A sharper version of the objection says that failing to notice may be just as absurd as noticing. Someone who sincerely devotes their life to a goal and never questions it still lives in the same gap, perhaps more deeply, because they don't know it's there. On this view awareness changes how we experience the absurd, not whether it exists. Which side you prefer probably depends on whether you think absurdity is a fact about our situation or a feature of how we see it.

Existentialism vs Absurdism

Existentialism is the older and broader tradition. It grew out of the nineteenth-century writing of Kierkegaard and Nietzsche and became a major movement in mid-twentieth-century Europe through Heidegger, Sartre and Simone de Beauvoir, who made the individual's freedom and responsibility the central problem of philosophy.[15] Absurdism grew up beside it, mainly through Camus, and the two share so much that people often treat them as one. The differences are real, though, and they show up most clearly in what each says we should do about meaninglessness.

Nihilism vs Existentialism vs Absurdism

These three get confused more than any other trio in philosophy, mostly because they all start by admitting that the world doesn't hand us a purpose. They split on what to do with that admission. Nihilism tends to say the loss is total, existentialism says we must build our own meaning, and absurdism says the loss is real and so is our hunger, and that we should live inside the contradiction.

Fundamental Philosophical Differences

Nihilism, in its common form, holds that life lacks objective meaning, value or purpose, and some versions conclude that nothing matters. The word spread widely after Turgenev used it in Fathers and Sons (1862), and Nietzsche later treated nihilism as a crisis of Western culture rather than a position to adopt.[16] Existentialism replies that the absence of given meaning is where human freedom starts: we're responsible for making our lives what they are.

Absurdism differs from both. Against nihilism, it insists the desire for meaning doesn't go away just because the answer isn't there, so you can't simply declare the matter closed. Against existentialism, it is more suspicious of the confident project of creating meaning, since that can become a quiet way of making the problem vanish. Camus prefers to keep the problem open.[1]

Shared Questions About Meaning and Existence

Despite the arguments, the three traditions are asking the same basic questions. Why is there something rather than nothing? What gives a life value if no one assigned it one? How should a person act when traditional authorities no longer settle things? All three come out of the same modern situation, in which religion, science and politics no longer fit together into one story people can trust.[15]

They also share a method: begin with lived experience rather than abstract theory. Camus starts with the feeling of a world that suddenly looks strange, Sartre with nausea and freedom, and Nietzsche with the moment people notice that old values have lost their grip. That focus on what it's actually like to be a person is why all three tend to leak into novels, plays and essays.

How These Philosophies Changed Modern Thought

Taken together, these movements made it respectable to ask whether life has a point without being sure of the answer. They shaped twentieth-century literature, theatre, psychology and political thought, and they helped popularise the idea that an authentic life means facing hard questions instead of borrowing ready-made answers.[15] Existentialist ideas fed into psychotherapy and into debates about freedom and responsibility, while nihilism became a standing warning about what happens when a culture loses its shared values.

Absurdism's special contribution is tone. It showed that you can take meaninglessness seriously without sounding defeated, and that humour, irony and stubbornness are legitimate philosophical responses. A lot of the dry, self-aware way people now talk about the strangeness of modern life owes something to that stance.

Influence and Impact of Absurdism

Absurdism has had more cultural influence than its small number of core texts would suggest. Part of the reason is its form: it came as stories and plays as much as arguments, so it travelled easily. Its impact shows up in books and theatre, in later philosophy, in film and comedy, and increasingly in how people describe their own working and online lives.

Influence on Literature

Absurdist ideas helped shape a whole strand of twentieth-century fiction that treats senseless systems and unanswerable questions as ordinary life. Kafka's work is the foundation, and Camus's novels carried the mood to a wide readership. Joseph Heller's Catch-22 (1961), with its military rule that traps everyone in circular logic, and much of Kurt Vonnegut's darkly comic fiction work in the same register of humane, bitter laughter.

What these books share isn't a doctrine but a posture. They present institutions, wars and routines whose logic doesn't hold up, and they let the characters respond with irony, stubbornness or tenderness rather than clean answers. That posture is probably absurdism's most durable literary legacy.

Theatre of the Absurd

"Theatre of the Absurd" is the name critic Martin Esslin gave to a group of playwrights, mostly working in 1950s Paris, including Beckett, Ionesco, Jean Genet and Arthur Adamov. Their plays tend to drop conventional plot, realistic dialogue and clear resolution, so that the form itself expresses the feeling that human communication and purpose have broken down.[5]

The label has been useful and a bit misleading. These writers weren't a unified school and didn't share a manifesto, and some disliked being grouped together.F The movement did, however, influence later playwrights such as Harold Pinter and Tom Stoppard, whose Rosencrantz and Guildenstern Are Dead (1966) takes two minor characters from Hamlet and leaves them waiting for explanations that never arrive.

Influence on Modern Philosophy

Within academic philosophy, the idea of the absurd got a second life through Thomas Nagel's 1971 essay, which treated it as a serious question for analytic philosophers. Nagel agreed that we can step back and see our lives as arbitrary, but concluded that irony, rather than Camus's defiance, may be the more fitting response.[4]G That debate between revolt and irony is still going.

Camus's influence on ethics and political philosophy is also real, if harder to measure. His break with Sartre over political violence is still cited in arguments about whether noble ends justify brutal means, and The Rebel remains a touchstone for thinkers wary of utopian projects. Contemporary philosophical work on the meaning of life often treats absurdism as one of the major positions to be answered.

Influence on Art and Popular Culture

The sensibility of the absurd is visible well beyond books and stage plays. Dada artists responded to the First World War with deliberate nonsense, and surrealists such as Magritte made familiar objects look quietly impossible. In film, the Coen brothers' A Serious Man (2009) is a Job-like story about a man pursuing explanations that never come, and Everything Everywhere All at Once (2022) turns the problem of meaning into a multiverse comedy that ends on kindness rather than answers.

Popular comedy has absorbed absurdist habits too, whether through animated series that mix cosmic nihilism with domestic jokes or through memes about pointless routines. These works don't always follow Camus carefully, and some lean toward plain nihilism, but they inherited the idea that humour is a respectable way to face the void.

Absurdism in Contemporary Life

Many people now describe their experience of modern life in absurdist terms without using the word. Bureaucratic forms that contradict one another, jobs that exist mainly to generate reports about the job, and a constant flow of news that is both urgent and impossible to act on all produce the Kafkaesque feeling of being inside a system that doesn't explain itself.[11]

Absurdism gives that feeling a name and a stance. It doesn't promise to fix the system, but it suggests that recognising the mismatch is not the same as giving up. You can see the absurdity, say so, and still decide what's worth your effort, which is closer to a practical attitude than a theory.

Absurdism in Daily Life

At a personal level, absurdism shows up in small, ordinary moments: realising halfway through a commute that you've been doing the same thing for years, or feeling slightly dizzy when a routine suddenly looks strange. Camus describes this kind of moment in the essay, when the usual stage sets collapse and you ask "why?" for the first time in a while.[1] Most of the time we push the question away and carry on.

The practical advice that comes out of absurdism is modest. Pay attention to what you're doing. Don't postpone your life until you've found its grand purpose. Be wary of any story that offers to explain everything. And keep a sense of humour, since the ability to laugh at your own seriousness is probably the most useful thing the philosophy has to teach.

Frequently Asked Questions About Absurdism

Is absurdism the same as nihilism?

No. Nihilism concludes that life has no meaning or value and often treats that as a reason to give up on caring. Absurdism agrees that the universe offers no obvious meaning, but insists that humans keep seeking it anyway. The absurdist refuses both false comfort and despair, and chooses to live in the tension.

Is Albert Camus an existentialist?

Camus is often grouped with the existentialists, but he rejected the label. He shared their interest in freedom and lived experience, yet disagreed with their attempts to build systems of meaning. He preferred to describe his own position as the philosophy of the absurd.

Does absurdism believe life is meaningless?

Not quite. Absurdism says the universe does not supply meaning that we can find or prove. It does not say we cannot create or feel meaning. The absurd comes from our need for meaning meeting that silence, so life is better described as lacking a given meaning than as worthless.

Can an absurdist be happy?

Camus thought so. He argued that accepting the absurd frees a person from false hopes and lets them value experience for its own sake. His image of Sisyphus, who finds a kind of fulfilment in the struggle itself, is meant to show that happiness does not depend on a cosmic purpose.

What is the meaning of Sisyphus's struggle?

In Camus's essay, Sisyphus stands for the human condition: endless effort with no final reward. Camus says the struggle itself can be enough to fill a heart, and that we should imagine Sisyphus happy, because he knows his fate and carries on without hope or illusion.

Is absurdism a philosophy or a literary movement?

It is both, which is why people confuse them. Absurdism as a philosophy was developed most fully by Camus. The Theatre of the Absurd is a separate literary and theatrical movement of the 1950s and 1960s whose writers shared a mood rather than a single doctrine.

What is philosophical suicide in absurdism?

Camus used the term for escaping the absurd by an intellectual leap, such as turning to faith or an all-explaining ideology, instead of living with the tension. He distinguished it from physical suicide, which he also rejected as another way of evading the problem.
