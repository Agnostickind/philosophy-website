Rumi's Sama: Origin of the Whirling Dervish Ceremony

What sama meant to Rumi, how the Mevlevi ceremony developed, and what each element symbolizes.

By Kavya Jain · Published 2026-10-04 · Updated 2026-10-04 · Part of our complete guide: Rumi: Life, Philosophy & Masnavi Explained

Short answerSama ("listening") is the practice of hearing music and turning in meditative movement. Tradition says Rumi first turned spontaneously in Konya, but the formal whirling ceremony was codified by his followers after his death, beginning with his son Sultan Walad. See the full Rumi guide.

The traditional origin story of Rumi's sama

One of the best-known stories about the origin of Rumi's association with whirling places him in the goldsmiths' bazaar of Konya. According to the later account of Shams al-Din Aflaki, Rumi was passing the shop of Salah al-Din Zarkub when he heard the rhythmic sound of goldsmiths' hammers. The rhythm reportedly moved him into an ecstatic state, after which he began turning. The story became an important part of the Mevlevi tradition because it presents music and movement not as entertainment but as a spontaneous response to the presence of the divine.

The story, however, should not be treated as a contemporary eyewitness record. Aflaki's Manaqib al-Arifin was written after Rumi's lifetime and contains many devotional and miraculous narratives. What can be established more securely is that Rumi was associated with sama, the practice of listening to music and poetry in a spiritually charged setting, and that movement or dancing could accompany such gatherings. Rumi's followers continued and developed these practices after his death. The famous image of Rumi suddenly inventing a fully structured ceremony in the bazaar therefore simplifies a much longer historical development.

What is sama in Sufism?

The Arabic and Persian term sama broadly refers to "listening" or "audition," particularly listening to music, poetry, chanting, or other sounds in a spiritual context. In Sufi traditions, sama could become a means of awakening the heart and directing attention toward God. Its purpose was not simply to produce an emotional reaction. For practitioners, properly conducted sama could transform ordinary listening into an exercise in remembrance, contemplation, longing, and spiritual awareness.

Sama was also controversial. Muslim scholars and jurists disagreed about whether music, singing, instrumental performance, and ecstatic movement were appropriate forms of religious practice. Some permitted particular forms under controlled circumstances, while others regarded them with suspicion or rejected them altogether. Rumi and the Sufi circles around him became associated with a more permissive understanding of spiritually meaningful music and movement. This debate is important because the Mevlevi ceremony did not emerge in a religious vacuum: it developed within a larger Islamic discussion about the proper relationship between music, emotion, bodily movement, and worship.

Did Rumi actually perform the whirling ceremony?

Rumi is strongly associated with ecstatic turning, but it is misleading to imagine that he personally performed the fully standardized ceremony known today as the Mevlevi sema. Rumi lived before the Mevlevi order had developed its later institutional structure. His practice of sama appears to have been considerably more spontaneous, involving music, poetry, movement, and states of spiritual ecstasy. Contemporary visitors and later disciples remembered Rumi as someone for whom music and bodily movement could become part of mystical experience.

This distinction matters when discussing the "origin of the whirling dervishes." Rumi can reasonably be described as the central spiritual inspiration for the tradition, but the precise choreography, clothing, musical sequence, ritual etiquette, and institutional organization of the modern Mevlevi ceremony developed gradually among his followers. In other words, the historical relationship is better expressed as Rumi's sama → the continuation of his practices → the development of Mevlevi ritual, rather than "Rumi invented the ceremony exactly as it exists today."

How the Mevlevi Sema ceremony developed

After Rumi's death in 1273, his followers continued the practices and teachings associated with him. His son Sultan Walad played an important role in organizing and transmitting the community that eventually became associated with the Mevlevi tradition. Over subsequent generations, the informal practices of sama were gradually shaped into a recognizable ceremony with particular movements, musical sections, clothing, spatial arrangements, and ritual conventions. The ceremony therefore represents both continuity with Rumi's spiritual world and development beyond anything that can be securely attributed to Rumi himself.

The formal sema known today should consequently be understood as the result of centuries of institutional and ritual development. Mevlevi lodges preserved and transmitted the ceremony until the major disruption caused by the Turkish Republic's 1925 closure of dervish lodges and Sufi institutions. The tradition later re-emerged in different forms, including public cultural performances. UNESCO inscribed the Mevlevi Sema Ceremony on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2008. The modern ceremony is therefore simultaneously a religious heritage tradition, an artistic performance, and a historical continuation of practices associated with Rumi.

What happens during the Mevlevi Sema ceremony?

The Mevlevi sema is not simply a group of people spinning in circles. The ceremony follows a structured sequence in which music, recitation, movement, silence, and ritual gestures work together. The participants, commonly called semazen, enter in distinctive clothing and participate in a series of movements that symbolize stages of spiritual orientation. The ceremony includes the ney, a reed flute strongly associated with Mevlevi music, along with other instruments, vocal performance, and poetry.

The turning itself is only one component of the ritual. The semazen rotate around their own axis while also moving around the ceremonial space, often with the right hand raised and the left hand lowered. Their movement is traditionally interpreted in relation to receiving divine grace and transmitting it into the world. The ceremony's sequence and discipline are significant because the objective is not uncontrolled spinning. The carefully trained body becomes part of a ritual language in which movement expresses spiritual transformation.

What does each part of the whirling dervish ceremony symbolize?

The clothing, music, gestures, and movements of the Mevlevi ceremony have accumulated symbolic meanings within the tradition. These interpretations should be understood as traditional Mevlevi explanations rather than as independently verifiable descriptions of what every element meant to Rumi personally. The symbolism also developed over time as the ceremony became more formally organized.

Major elements of the Mevlevi Sema and their traditional symbolism
Element Traditional meaning Role in the ceremony
Tall felt hat (sikke) Traditionally interpreted as a tombstone marking the death of the ego. Forms part of the characteristic clothing of the semazen.
Black cloak (hirka) Often interpreted as the worldly or bodily state from which the participant emerges. Removed before the principal turning begins.
White skirt (tennure) Traditionally associated with the shroud and the death of the ego. Becomes visually prominent as the semazen turns.
Ney (reed flute) Represents longing and separation from the divine source. Its sound introduces and accompanies important parts of the ceremony.
Right hand raised Traditionally associated with receiving divine grace. The open hand is directed upward during the turning.
Left hand lowered Traditionally associated with transmitting what is received to the world. The hand remains directed downward while the semazen turns.
Turning Associated with spiritual movement around the divine centre and the order of the cosmos. Forms the central visual movement of the ceremony.
Circular movement Can symbolize cosmic order, unity, and the soul's movement toward God. The semazen turn around their own axis while participating in the larger circular movement of the ceremony.

Taken together, these elements create a symbolic vocabulary of death, rebirth, longing, reception, and return. The black cloak can be understood as the condition that is left behind, while the white clothing becomes associated with a transformed spiritual state. The raised and lowered hands express a movement between receiving and giving rather than a simple gesture of prayer. The turning similarly has several layers of meaning: the practitioner moves physically, but the movement is interpreted as an enactment of the soul's orientation toward God and of the ordered movement of creation.

The ney is especially important for understanding the connection between Mevlevi music and Rumi's poetry. The opening of Rumi's Masnavi famously begins with the lament of the reed, which has been separated from the reed bed and therefore sings of longing and separation. In the Mevlevi tradition, the sound of the ney can therefore evoke the soul's longing for its source. This symbolism should not be reduced to the claim that every movement has one fixed meaning: Mevlevi interpretation is a layered religious tradition, and particular explanations can vary.

Why are music and the ney important in sama?

Music is fundamental to the Mevlevi understanding of sama. The ceremony is not silent meditation accompanied by spinning; musical performance provides the framework within which the movement takes place. The ney, percussion, strings, and human voice contribute to a carefully structured musical environment. Poetry, especially verses associated with Rumi and other Persian Sufi poets, connects the ceremony directly to the literary culture of Persian mysticism.

The central idea is that sound can awaken an inner state of remembrance and longing. This explains why the Mevlevi tradition gives such importance to listening rather than treating music as mere background decoration. Sama transforms listening into a disciplined spiritual practice. The relationship between poetry, music, and movement is therefore essential to understanding why the ceremony cannot accurately be reduced to the modern phrase "whirling dervish dance." It is a ritual tradition in which artistic forms are integrated with a particular Sufi understanding of spiritual transformation.

Sama and Rumi's philosophy

Sama can be understood as an embodied expression of several themes that appear throughout Rumi's thought: longing for God, the transformation of the ego, love, remembrance, and movement from separation toward spiritual union. Rumi's mystical language repeatedly treats ordinary human consciousness as incomplete because it remains attached to the isolated self. Music and movement could provide a way of loosening that attachment and directing attention beyond the ordinary ego. This connects naturally with the Sufi concept of fana, often described as the dissolution or passing away of the ego, followed by baqa, understood as continued existence in God.

At the same time, Rumi's philosophy should not be reduced to "losing yourself by spinning." The deeper point is the transformation of the relationship between self, world, and God. The semazen does not simply escape physical existence; the ritual uses the body, music, rhythm, and disciplined movement as means of reorienting the person spiritually. In this sense, sama represents an important feature of Rumi's mystical outlook: the path toward God can involve poetry, music, love, movement, and embodied experience rather than intellectual argument alone. How the whirling dervish tradition changed over time

The ceremony seen today is the product of a long historical process rather than a practice frozen in Rumi's lifetime. After Rumi's death, his descendants and disciples helped establish institutions through which his teachings and practices were preserved. Mevlevi communities developed their own rules, musical traditions, ceremonial spaces, and training systems. The result was a ritual tradition that retained Rumi's spiritual vocabulary while also acquiring forms that belong to later generations.

The modern history of the ceremony is particularly important. The Turkish Republic's 1925 legislation closed Sufi lodges and restricted the institutional life of religious orders. Mevlevi practice therefore experienced a major interruption in its traditional setting. Public performances later became possible in different contexts, and the ceremony increasingly became known internationally as part of Turkey's cultural heritage. UNESCO's 2008 recognition helped establish the Mevlevi Sema as internationally recognized intangible cultural heritage. A modern performance may therefore take place for religious, cultural, educational, or tourist purposes, and these contexts should not automatically be treated as identical to the devotional environment of historical Mevlevi lodges.

Is the whirling dervish ceremony still practised today?

Yes. The Mevlevi Sema continues to be performed in Turkey and in other parts of the world. Some performances take place in religious or heritage-oriented contexts, while others are presented for visitors, cultural festivals, educational programs, and international audiences. The same outward form can therefore exist in different settings, and it is useful to distinguish a traditional devotional ceremony from a shortened stage presentation designed primarily for spectators.

This modern visibility has helped preserve international awareness of Rumi and the Mevlevi tradition, but it can also create misconceptions. The phrase "whirling dervish" often makes the practice sound like a dance performance whose purpose is entertainment. Within the Mevlevi tradition, however, the turning is part of a disciplined spiritual and musical practice. Understanding the history behind the performance makes it easier to appreciate why the ceremony involves particular clothing, music, gestures, ritual stages, and religious symbolism rather than treating the spinning itself as the whole practice.

Frequently asked questions

Did Rumi invent the whirling dervishes?

Rumi is the central spiritual figure associated with the Mevlevi tradition of turning, but he did not create the fully standardized ceremony in its modern form. His practice of sama was continued and organized by his followers, and the formal Mevlevi ceremony developed over subsequent generations.

What is sama in Sufism?

Sama means listening or spiritual audition and refers to Sufi practices involving music, poetry, chanting, and sometimes movement. In the Mevlevi tradition it became associated with the highly structured ceremony of music and turning known as the sema.

Why do the whirling dervishes spin?

The turning is traditionally understood as a spiritual practice expressing the soul's orientation toward God, the transformation of the ego, and the ordered movement of the cosmos. The raised right hand and lowered left hand are traditionally interpreted as receiving divine grace and passing it into the world.

What does the white skirt of a whirling dervish mean?

The white tennure is traditionally interpreted in relation to a shroud and the death or transformation of the ego. Its symbolism belongs to the developed Mevlevi ceremonial tradition rather than being something that can simply be assumed to have had an identical meaning for Rumi himself.

Why do whirling dervishes wear a tall hat?

The tall felt sikke is traditionally interpreted as a tombstone, symbolizing the death of the ego. It is part of the distinctive ceremonial clothing of the Mevlevi semazen.

Why is the ney important in the ceremony?

The ney, or reed flute, is strongly associated with Mevlevi music and with the symbolism of longing and separation. Its significance is closely connected with the opening imagery of Rumi's Masnavi, where the reed laments its separation from its original home.

Is the Mevlevi Sema still practised today?

Yes. The ceremony continues to be performed in Turkey and internationally. It exists in both traditional and cultural settings and was inscribed by UNESCO on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2008.

Is modern whirling the same as Rumi's original practice?

Not necessarily. Rumi's practice of sama appears to have been more varied and spontaneous, while the formal Mevlevi ceremony developed over generations after his death. The modern ritual preserves a connection with Rumi's spiritual tradition but should not automatically be treated as an exact reconstruction of his personal practice.
