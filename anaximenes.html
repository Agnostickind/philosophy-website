Anaximenes of Miletus: Philosophy, Air, and Theory of Nature

Explore the life and philosophy of Anaximenes of Miletus, the Pre-Socratic Greek philosopher associated with air as the fundamental principle of nature and with condensation and rarefaction as processes that explain natural change.

Who Was Anaximenes of Miletus?

Anaximenes of Miletus was a sixth-century BCE Greek philosopher associated with the Milesian school of Pre-Socratic philosophy. He followed the earlier Milesian thinkers Thales and Anaximander in asking what fundamental principle could explain the natural world.

Anaximenes is best known for the philosophical claim that air is the fundamental originating principle of nature. Ancient reports also associate his philosophy with the processes of condensation and rarefaction, through which air becomes different forms of matter.

His philosophy represents an important stage in early Greek natural philosophy because he attempted to explain the diversity and change found in nature through a unified material principle and a natural process of transformation.

Anaximenes and the Milesian School

Anaximenes was associated with Miletus, an important Ionian city on the coast of Asia Minor. Miletus became associated with some of the earliest Greek thinkers who investigated nature through questions about its underlying principles.

The Milesian tradition is commonly represented by Thales, Anaximander, and Anaximenes. Although their theories differed, all three are associated with attempts to identify an originating principle capable of explaining the natural world.

Anaximenes developed this project differently from his predecessors. Rather than choosing water, as Thales was traditionally said to have done, or the apeiron, the boundless or indefinite associated with Anaximander, Anaximenes identified air as the originating principle.

Why Is Anaximenes Important in Philosophy?

Anaximenes is important because he attempted to explain both unity and change in nature. His theory begins with a single fundamental substance, air, while also providing a process by which different forms of matter can arise.

This makes his philosophy significant in the history of Pre-Socratic natural philosophy. The question was not simply what substance exists, but how a fundamental reality could account for the many different things observed in the world.

His theory therefore connects two major philosophical problems: what is reality fundamentally made of? and how does change occur?

What Did Anaximenes Believe?

Anaximenes is traditionally associated with the view that air is the fundamental originating substance of nature. Ancient sources describe air as capable of becoming different forms of matter through processes of condensation and rarefaction.

In this account, changes in the density of air produce different natural substances. Rarefied air becomes associated with fire, while increasingly condensed air gives rise to wind, clouds, water, earth, and eventually stones.

The surviving evidence is indirect, however. Much of what is known about Anaximenes comes from later ancient writers discussing earlier philosophers. His theory should therefore be presented as an ancient reconstruction rather than as a complete philosophical system that survives in his own words.

Why Did Anaximenes Believe Air Was the Fundamental Principle?

The choice of air allowed Anaximenes to propose a principle that was both fundamental and capable of undergoing different forms of change. Air is invisible in ordinary circumstances but can be experienced through breath and wind.

Ancient testimony suggests that Anaximenes considered air especially suitable as an originating principle because it could take on different characteristics through processes of condensation and rarefaction.

The precise reasoning behind his choice cannot be reconstructed with complete certainty because no complete work by Anaximenes survives. Nevertheless, air and its transformations are central to the ancient tradition concerning his philosophy.

What Is Anaximenes' Theory of Condensation and Rarefaction?

The most distinctive feature of Anaximenes' philosophy is the attempt to explain changes in matter through condensation and rarefaction.

According to the ancient account, when air becomes more rarefied, it becomes associated with fire. As air becomes progressively more condensed, it becomes associated with wind, cloud, water, earth, and stone.

This gives Anaximenes a way of explaining how many different forms of matter can arise from one fundamental principle. Instead of requiring a completely different basic substance for every natural object, the theory uses changes in the condition or density of air to explain diversity.

How Does Air Become Different Forms of Matter?

Anaximenes' theory can be understood as a sequence of transformations produced by different degrees of condensation and rarefaction.

  • Rarefied air — associated with fire.
  • Condensed air — associated with wind.
  • Further condensation — associated with clouds.
  • Still greater condensation — associated with water.
  • Further condensation — associated with earth.
  • Extreme condensation — associated with stones.

This sequence is preserved through later reports about Anaximenes and should not be confused with a modern scientific theory of matter. Its philosophical importance lies in the attempt to explain qualitative differences in the natural world through a single underlying principle and a process of transformation.

What Is the Archê in Anaximenes' Philosophy?

The Greek concept of archê refers broadly to a beginning, source, origin, or fundamental principle. Early Greek philosophers used questions about such principles to investigate the underlying nature of the cosmos.

Anaximenes is traditionally associated with identifying air as the archê. His position therefore continues the Milesian search for an originating principle while differing from the answers traditionally attributed to Thales and Anaximander.

What makes Anaximenes particularly significant is that his theory connects the originating principle with a proposed mechanism of change: condensation and rarefaction.

Anaximenes' Cosmology and View of the Universe

Anaximenes also developed ideas about the structure of the cosmos. Ancient reports associate him with a cosmology in which the Earth and celestial bodies are explained through natural processes involving air.

Ancient testimony describes the Earth as flat and associated with air, while the heavenly bodies were also understood within a natural framework rather than solely through mythological explanations.

Some reports further describe celestial bodies as being carried by air. Because these details come through later sources, the exact reconstruction of Anaximenes' cosmology remains uncertain.

What Did Anaximenes Think About the Earth?

Ancient reports attribute a flat Earth to Anaximenes. The Earth was understood within his broader natural-philosophical account of air and the cosmos.

This cosmological model should not be judged as though it were a modern scientific explanation. Its historical importance lies in the attempt to provide a natural account of the structure of the world using principles operating within nature itself.

The reports concerning the exact shape and support of the Earth are ancient testimonia rather than surviving passages in Anaximenes' complete writings.

Anaximenes on Air, Breath, and the Soul

Anaximenes is also associated with a famous comparison between air, breath, and the soul. A surviving fragment attributed to him compares the role of air in sustaining the individual with the role of air in holding together the cosmos.

This connection has attracted considerable attention because it links air with life and animation. The comparison suggests that the principle underlying the cosmos could also be connected with the principle sustaining living beings.

Some modern discussions have interpreted this as evidence for a broader view in which air or breath has a life-giving or soul-like function. Anaximenes and Early Greek Natural Philosophy

Anaximenes belongs to the tradition now called natural philosophy, which investigated nature, matter, change, and the structure of the cosmos.

The Milesian thinkers are significant because they attempted to explain natural phenomena through principles operating within the natural world. Their explanations differed substantially from modern science, but they contributed to a developing philosophical interest in regularity, material change, and natural causes.

Anaximenes' theory is particularly important because it combines a material principle with an explicit account of transformation. Air is not merely named as the origin of things; its different states are used to explain the diversity of the physical world.

Anaximenes' Main Philosophical Ideas

Although the evidence for Anaximenes is fragmentary, several ideas are consistently associated with his place in the history of Pre-Socratic philosophy.

Air as the Fundamental Principle

Anaximenes is traditionally associated with the claim that air is the fundamental originating principle of nature.

Condensation and Rarefaction

Changes in the density of air were used to explain the emergence of different forms of matter.

Natural Explanation

His philosophy sought to explain the cosmos through processes occurring within nature rather than relying solely on traditional mythological accounts.

Unity Beneath Diversity

The theory attempts to explain the diversity of the physical world through a single fundamental principle.

Cosmological Inquiry

Anaximenes also developed ideas about the Earth, heavens, and celestial bodies as part of his broader investigation of nature.

Did Anaximenes Write Any Books?

Anaximenes is traditionally said to have written a work concerning nature, but no complete work survives today.

Our knowledge of his philosophy comes primarily from fragments and later ancient authors who reported or discussed the views of Pre-Socratic thinkers.

This means that studying Anaximenes requires careful distinction between what can be traced to ancient testimony and what later interpreters have inferred from that testimony.

How Do We Know About Anaximenes?

The evidence for Anaximenes is fragmentary. Like many Pre-Socratic philosophers, he is known mainly through later ancient authors who preserved reports about earlier thinkers.

Aristotle and Later Peripatetic Tradition

Aristotle and the later philosophical tradition surrounding him are important for the reconstruction of early Greek natural philosophy. Reports preserved through this tradition associate Anaximenes with air and with processes of condensation and rarefaction.

Simplicius

Simplicius, a much later ancient commentator on Aristotle, preserved important material concerning earlier Greek philosophers. His discussions are among the sources used to reconstruct the Milesian theories.

Ancient Testimonia

Because Anaximenes' complete writings have not survived, historians must compare fragments and testimonia rather than relying on a single complete philosophical work.

What Do We Actually Know About Anaximenes?

Anaximenes illustrates the difficulties involved in reconstructing Pre-Socratic philosophy. His ideas are important, but the surviving evidence is incomplete and comes largely through later writers.

Anaximenes and the Milesian School

Anaximenes is traditionally regarded as the third major figure of the Milesian school, following Thales and Anaximander. The three thinkers are associated with the early Greek investigation of nature and the search for an originating principle.

  • Thales — traditionally associated with water as the fundamental principle.
  • Anaximander — associated with the apeiron, the boundless or indefinite.
  • Anaximenes — associated with air and the processes of condensation and rarefaction.

Their different theories demonstrate how early Greek philosophy developed through attempts to answer similar questions with different explanations of nature and reality.

Anaximenes Compared With Thales and Anaximander

Anaximenes' theory becomes clearer when considered alongside the two earlier Milesian philosophers.

Thales

Thales is traditionally associated with water as the fundamental principle of nature.

Anaximander

Anaximander is associated with the apeiron, often translated as the boundless, indefinite, or unlimited.

Anaximenes

Anaximenes returned to a more concrete material principle, air, while also proposing condensation and rarefaction as a mechanism through which different forms arise.

In this sense, Anaximenes continued the Milesian search for an originating principle while attempting to give a more explicit account of how transformation occurs.

Anaximenes' Influence on Greek Philosophy

Anaximenes contributed to the continuing Pre-Socratic investigation of nature, matter, change, and the cosmos. His theory provided one of the early attempts to explain many forms of matter through a single underlying principle.

The philosophical problem he addressed would continue throughout ancient Greek philosophy: how can the changing world be explained in terms of something fundamental?

Later philosophers would develop radically different answers to this problem, but the Milesian tradition helped establish the importance of asking how natural change and the structure of reality can be explained.

Anaximenes of Miletus Explained Simply

Anaximenes was a Pre-Socratic Greek philosopher who wanted to explain what the natural world is fundamentally made of and how different things can arise from one underlying principle.

His answer was traditionally identified as air. He is also associated with the idea that different forms of matter arise through condensation and rarefaction.

  • Question: What is the fundamental principle of nature?
  • Answer associated with Anaximenes: Air.
  • Process of change: Condensation and rarefaction.
  • Tradition: Milesian and Pre-Socratic philosophy.
  • Other interests: Cosmology, nature, the Earth, celestial bodies, and the relationship between air and life.

The Milesian Search for Nature

Why Anaximenes Still Matters

Anaximenes occupies an important position in the history of Greek philosophy because he continued the Milesian search for an underlying principle of nature.

His answer was air, but the philosophical importance of his theory extends beyond the choice of one substance. By connecting air with condensation and rarefaction, Anaximenes attempted to explain how a diverse and changing natural world could arise from a single fundamental principle.

His philosophy therefore represents an early attempt to address one of the enduring questions of philosophy: How can the many changing things we experience arise from something fundamental?
