Not for Profit Summary: Why Democracy Needs the Humanities

The argument of Nussbaum's 2010 book, its three core capacities, and its critics.

By Kavya Jain · Updated 2026-10-06 · Part of our complete guide: Martha Nussbaum

Short answer Not for Profit: Why Democracy Needs the Humanities (2010) argues that education driven primarily by economic growth can weaken the capacities democratic societies need. Martha Nussbaum argues that education should cultivate critical thinking, the ability to see oneself as a citizen of a wider world, and narrative imagination: the capacity to understand the experiences of people whose lives differ from one's own. Her defense of the humanities is therefore ultimately a defense of democratic citizenship.

What is Not for Profit about?

Not for Profit: Why Democracy Needs the Humanities is Martha Nussbaum's defense of an education that develops the abilities required for democratic citizenship rather than treating students primarily as future economic producers. Published in 2010, the book argues that many educational systems have become increasingly focused on economic growth, employability and measurable technical skills while giving less attention to literature, philosophy, history, the arts and other humanistic subjects.

Nussbaum's argument is not that economic development is unimportant or that science and technology should be removed from education. Her concern is that economic usefulness can become the dominant measure of educational value. If schools and universities ask almost exclusively what a subject contributes to economic productivity, they risk neglecting abilities that are harder to measure but essential to democracy: questioning authority, considering evidence, understanding people from different backgrounds, recognizing one's own limitations and imagining the experiences of others.

The book therefore asks a broader question about the purpose of education: What kind of people should education help us become? For Nussbaum, the answer cannot be reduced to producing efficient workers. Education should also help produce citizens capable of participating thoughtfully in a society characterized by disagreement, diversity and political interdependence.

The "silent crisis"

Nussbaum opens with a concern that she describes as a silent crisis: educational systems can undergo major changes in priorities without those changes receiving the same public attention as an obvious political or economic crisis. When governments and institutions increasingly judge education by its contribution to economic growth, subjects whose economic benefits are difficult to measure can be treated as secondary.

Literature, philosophy, history, music, art and related disciplines can appear less immediately useful than technical or vocational training because their benefits are not always expressed in straightforward economic statistics. Nussbaum argues that this way of thinking overlooks an important function of education. Humanistic study can cultivate habits of questioning, interpretation, self-examination and attention to other people's perspectives. These abilities may not produce an immediate economic return, but they can matter greatly to the functioning of democratic institutions.

The argument continues themes developed in her earlier book Cultivating Humanity (1997), where she examined liberal education and the formation of citizens capable of thinking beyond narrow identities. It also connects to her broader philosophy of education.

Education for profit vs for democracy

Education for profit Education for democratic citizenship
Primary goal Economic growth, productivity and employability Developing capable, thoughtful and responsible citizens
Student as Future worker or economic producer Person, citizen and participant in shared political life
Knowledge valued for Immediate or measurable economic usefulness Its contribution to reasoning, understanding and human development
Important subjects Science, technology, business and vocational skills These subjects together with humanities, literature and the arts
Democratic risk Citizens may become passive, narrow or vulnerable to manipulation Citizens are encouraged to question, deliberate and understand others

Nussbaum does not present this as a choice between science and the humanities. Scientific and technical knowledge can be essential to both economic development and democratic societies. Her criticism is directed at an educational philosophy in which economic productivity becomes the overriding purpose of schooling and other purposes are treated as expendable.

The distinction is therefore between two visions of education. One sees education primarily as an instrument for producing economically useful skills. The other sees education as part of the formation of human beings and citizens. Nussbaum argues that a healthy democracy requires the second vision alongside appropriate scientific and technical education.

Why does democracy need education beyond job skills?

Democracy requires more than citizens who can perform economic tasks. It requires people who can evaluate political claims, challenge authority, listen to disagreement and recognize that other citizens may have legitimate interests different from their own. Nussbaum therefore treats education as part of the infrastructure of democratic life.

A citizen who has never been encouraged to question assumptions may be more vulnerable to propaganda or political manipulation. A citizen who cannot imagine the circumstances of people from different social, cultural or economic backgrounds may find it easier to dismiss their interests. Education, on Nussbaum's account, should therefore cultivate intellectual independence and the ability to engage seriously with other people's perspectives.

This does not mean that humanities students automatically become good citizens or that technical education automatically produces bad ones. Nussbaum's deeper claim is about what educational institutions should intentionally cultivate. Democratic citizenship requires capacities that cannot be reduced to occupational competence.

What are the three core capacities?

Nussbaum identifies several educational abilities that are especially important for democratic life. Three of the most prominent are Socratic critical examination, world citizenship and narrative imagination. They work together: citizens must be able to question their own assumptions, understand their relationship to people beyond their immediate community, and imagine the lives of others.

  • Critical thinking. The Socratic ability to examine beliefs, question authority, identify weak arguments and give reasons for one's conclusions. See Socrates.
  • World citizenship. The ability to see oneself as part of a wider, interdependent human world rather than being defined entirely by local or national identity. Nussbaum connects this to the Stoic tradition of cosmopolitanism.
  • Narrative imagination. The ability to imagine what another person may experience and to understand a life from a perspective different from one's own. Literature, drama and the arts can help develop this capacity.

These capacities are not simply academic skills. They concern how people relate to themselves, other people and political institutions. A student can memorize information without learning to question it, and can acquire professional skills without developing the ability to understand people whose lives are very different from their own. Nussbaum's educational ideal therefore concerns the formation of judgment and perspective as well as the accumulation of knowledge.

Why does Nussbaum emphasize Socratic education?

Nussbaum draws on the example of Socrates to emphasize the importance of examining one's own beliefs. The Socratic model treats questioning not as a failure of respect but as an essential part of intellectual life. Students should learn to ask why they believe something, what reasons support it, whether those reasons are consistent and whether an opposing argument exposes a weakness in their position.

This matters politically because democratic citizens routinely encounter competing claims, persuasive rhetoric and appeals to authority. An education that encourages independent examination can help citizens participate in political disagreement without simply accepting whatever claim is presented most forcefully. Nussbaum therefore sees critical inquiry as a civic capacity rather than merely an academic technique.

What does Nussbaum mean by world citizenship?

World citizenship means learning to see oneself as a member of a wider human community whose boundaries extend beyond one's immediate nation, religion, culture or social group. Nussbaum connects this ideal to ancient Stoic cosmopolitanism, which held that human beings belong to a broader moral community.

For education, the implication is that students should encounter histories, cultures and ways of life beyond their immediate surroundings. This does not require abandoning local identities. Instead, Nussbaum argues that people can retain particular attachments while also recognizing obligations to people who belong to different communities. This cosmopolitan dimension is particularly important to her later work on global justice.

What is narrative imagination?

Narrative imagination is the ability to imagine what it might be like to live the life of another person. For Nussbaum, this does not mean pretending that one can completely know another person's experience. Rather, it means developing the imaginative ability to consider another person's circumstances, emotions, vulnerabilities and aspirations seriously.

Literature and the arts are particularly important because they can place readers and audiences inside perspectives different from their own. A novel may require a reader to follow the experiences of a character with a different social position, while drama can make unfamiliar conflicts and relationships emotionally intelligible. Nussbaum links this capacity to compassion and the emotions, because understanding another person's situation can influence how we judge what happens to them.

Narrative imagination should not be confused with simply feeling sympathy. It is an intellectual and imaginative discipline that can make the circumstances of other people more visible. Nussbaum regards this as particularly valuable in pluralistic democracies, where citizens must make decisions affecting people whose lives they may never personally experience.

Tagore and other educational models

Nussbaum draws on Rabindranath Tagore as an important example of an educational philosophy that combines intellectual development, imagination, the arts and a sense of global human community. Tagore's educational experiments at Santiniketan, which developed into Visva-Bharati, emphasized learning in relation to culture, nature, creativity and international exchange.

Tagore is placed alongside other thinkers whose educational ideas challenge narrow instrumental views of schooling. Nussbaum also engages the legacy of Rousseau, Socratic education and John Dewey's account of education as connected to democratic life. These examples support her broader argument that education has historically been understood as a process of forming judgment, character and citizenship rather than merely transferring occupational skills.

Tagore is especially significant in the book because his educational philosophy provides an example outside the dominant Western institutional model. His emphasis on imagination, artistic expression and engagement with different cultures helps illustrate the kind of broad human development Nussbaum believes democratic education should protect.

How does Not for Profit connect to the Capabilities Approach?

Not for Profit applies the basic logic of the Capabilities Approach to education. Instead of evaluating education only by economic output or the amount of knowledge students acquire, the approach asks what people are actually enabled to do and to be.

Education can contribute to capabilities such as senses, imagination and thought, practical reason, affiliation and control over one's environment. A good educational system therefore does more than prepare someone for employment. It can help people develop the ability to reason, form judgments, participate in social relationships and exercise meaningful agency in their own lives.

This is why Nussbaum's defense of the humanities is connected to her wider theory of justice. If certain educational opportunities are necessary for people to develop important human capabilities, then denying those opportunities can become a question of justice rather than merely a question of curriculum preference.

What should education include according to Nussbaum?

Nussbaum does not propose replacing science, mathematics, technology or professional training with humanities courses. Her argument is for an educational system broad enough to cultivate both technical competence and democratic capacities. Students need knowledge that allows them to work and participate in modern economies, but they also need opportunities to develop independent judgment, cultural understanding and imaginative engagement with other people.

Literature, philosophy, history, music, visual art and other humanistic disciplines can contribute to these goals in different ways. Philosophy can train students to examine arguments; history can complicate simple narratives about nations and societies; literature can expose readers to unfamiliar perspectives; and the arts can cultivate attention and imagination. Nussbaum's point is not that every humanities course automatically produces these results, but that educational systems should preserve opportunities for this kind of development.

What are the criticisms of Not for Profit?

Nussbaum's argument has attracted criticism, particularly over how strongly she contrasts economic education with democratic education and over the evidence for the civic benefits she attributes to the humanities.

  • The contrast may be too sharp. Critics argue that education for economic productivity and education for democratic citizenship can reinforce one another. Scientific and technical education can itself involve creativity, reasoning and problem-solving, while humanities graduates also need economic opportunities.
  • The evidence is difficult to establish. The claim that studying literature, philosophy or the arts reliably produces more empathetic or democratic citizens is difficult to measure and may depend on how subjects are taught rather than on the subject alone.
  • Practical constraints matter. Schools and universities operate under financial, staffing and time constraints. Critics can therefore ask how Nussbaum's broad educational ideal could be implemented consistently, particularly in underfunded systems.
  • Economic needs cannot simply be ignored. Education has legitimate responsibilities to prepare people for employment and economic independence. A democratic education that neglects these needs could itself undermine the security and agency of students.

These criticisms do not necessarily defeat Nussbaum's central argument. Her strongest claim is narrower: economic usefulness should not become the sole standard by which educational subjects are judged. Even if the humanities do not automatically create better citizens, democratic societies may still have reasons to preserve forms of education that cultivate independent judgment, perspective-taking and civic understanding.

Why does Not for Profit matter?

Not for Profit matters because it reframes the debate about the humanities as a question about the purpose of education and the health of democratic institutions. Instead of defending literature, philosophy and the arts simply because they are culturally valuable, Nussbaum asks what kinds of human capacities a democratic society requires and whether its educational institutions are developing them.

The book also shows how closely Nussbaum's educational philosophy is connected to her wider work. Her concerns about dignity, capabilities, emotion, compassion, global citizenship and human flourishing all appear in the background of the argument. Education becomes one of the institutions through which people acquire the capabilities necessary to participate meaningfully in social and political life.

For that reason, the book is best read not simply as a defense of university humanities departments but as part of Nussbaum's larger political philosophy. Its central question is ultimately about what democratic societies should value when they decide what education is for.

Frequently asked questions

What is Not for Profit about?

Not for Profit: Why Democracy Needs the Humanities is a 2010 book in which Martha Nussbaum argues that education focused primarily on economic growth can neglect the capacities required for democratic citizenship, including critical thinking, world citizenship and narrative imagination.

What is Nussbaum's main argument in Not for Profit?

Nussbaum's main argument is that education should not be judged only by its ability to produce economic growth or employable workers. Democratic societies also need education that develops independent judgment, the ability to understand different perspectives and a sense of responsibility toward a wider human community.

What is the "silent crisis" in Not for Profit?

The "silent crisis" is Nussbaum's description of the gradual prioritization of economic growth and market-oriented skills over educational aims such as critical inquiry, imagination, the arts and democratic citizenship.

What are the three capacities in Not for Profit?

The three prominent capacities are critical thinking or Socratic self-examination, the ability to see oneself as a citizen of a wider world, and narrative imagination, which involves understanding the lives and perspectives of other people.

Why does Nussbaum defend the humanities?

Nussbaum defends the humanities because she believes subjects such as literature, philosophy, history and the arts can help cultivate capacities important to democratic life, including questioning assumptions, understanding different perspectives and engaging imaginatively with other people's experiences.

What does Nussbaum say about science and technology?

Nussbaum does not argue that science and technology are unnecessary. Her concern is with treating economic and technical usefulness as the only legitimate purpose of education. She argues that scientific and technical education should exist alongside forms of education that cultivate democratic and humanistic capacities.

What role does Tagore play in Not for Profit?

Rabindranath Tagore provides Nussbaum with an important educational model emphasizing imagination, the arts, cultural openness and global human connection. His educational experiments at Santiniketan are used to illustrate an alternative to narrowly instrumental education.

What is narrative imagination?

Narrative imagination is the ability to imagine the circumstances and experiences of another person whose life may be very different from one's own. Nussbaum sees literature and the arts as important ways of developing this ability.

How does Not for Profit relate to the Capabilities Approach?

The book applies the Capabilities Approach to education by asking what education enables people to do and to be. Education can develop capabilities such as thought, imagination, practical reason and affiliation, rather than merely increasing a person's economic productivity.

Is Not for Profit against vocational education?

No. Nussbaum does not reject vocational, scientific or technical education. Her argument is that these forms of education should not displace the broader educational purposes needed for democratic citizenship and human development.
