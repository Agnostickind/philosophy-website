Foucault Quotes: Famous Lines, Sources and Meanings

Well-sourced Foucault lines explained, and a warning about popular quotes that cannot be traced.

By Kavya Jain · Updated 2026-10-03 · Part of our complete guide: Michel Foucault

Short answerSome of Foucault's best-known lines are short and traceable, such as “Where there is power, there is resistance” (History of Sexuality, Vol. 1) and “Visibility is a trap” (Discipline and Punish). Many viral “Foucault quotes” online are loose paraphrases or have no clear source, so always check the book and translation.

Sourced Foucault quotes and what they mean

Quote Source Meaning
“Where there is power, there is resistance.” History of Sexuality, Vol. 1 (1976) Power relations are never total; resistance is built into them.
“Visibility is a trap.” Discipline and Punish (1975), “Panopticism” Being constantly observable is how discipline controls behaviour.
“The soul is the prison of the body.” Discipline and Punish (1975), Part 1 Modern punishment shapes the “soul”, meaning habits and inner life, as a way of controlling the body.
“My point is not that everything is bad, but that everything is dangerous.” Interview, “On the Genealogy of Ethics” (1983) Every practice carries risks, so critique is never finished.
“Knowledge is not made for understanding; it is made for cutting.” “Nietzsche, Genealogy, History” (1971) Genealogy is critical and partisan, not a neutral survey. Compare genealogy vs archaeology.

Wording differs between translations, so check the edition you are quoting. Page numbers vary.

Ideas people attribute to Foucault, in paraphrase

  • Power is everywhere, not because it covers everything, but because it comes from many points and operates through relationships rather than existing only in the hands of a single institution or ruling group (The History of Sexuality, Vol. 1). See power/knowledge.
  • Power and knowledge are closely connected: systems of knowledge can participate in the exercise of power, while relations of power influence what can be established, recorded, classified, and accepted as knowledge.
  • Modern power often works through discipline, surveillance, examination, classification, and normalization rather than relying only on direct force or physical punishment. Discipline and Punish uses the Panopticon as a model for understanding this form of disciplinary power. See our summary of Discipline and Punish.
  • The Panopticon is important to Foucault not simply as a prison design but as a model of a broader disciplinary mechanism in which the possibility of being observed can encourage individuals to regulate their own behaviour.
  • What counts as truth is connected to historically specific institutions, practices, procedures, and authorities that determine how statements are produced, tested, circulated, and recognized as true. This does not simply mean that truth is whatever someone wants it to be.
  • Categories that appear natural or self-evident can have histories. Genealogical analysis investigates how concepts, identities, practices, and institutions emerged through contingent events, conflicts, and changing relations of power rather than assuming that their present form was inevitable.
  • Madness is not only a timeless medical or psychological object; the ways societies define, classify, separate, and treat people considered mad have changed historically. Madness and Civilization examines these changing relationships between madness, reason, institutions, and social order.
  • Punishment is not merely a response to crime. Changes in penal systems also reveal transformations in how societies discipline bodies, organize institutions, classify individuals, and attempt to regulate conduct. This is a central concern of Discipline and Punish.
  • Sexuality is not simply a biological fact that modern society gradually learned to discuss more openly. Foucault examines how sexuality became an object of discourse, classification, examination, confession, and regulation, particularly in The History of Sexuality, Vol. 1.
  • Power should not be understood only as something that represses people. It can also produce categories, forms of knowledge, identities, behaviours, institutions, and ways of understanding oneself. This is why Foucault often analyzes what power produces as well as what it prohibits.
  • Resistance is not simply outside power. Because power operates through relationships, practices, and social institutions, possibilities for resistance can arise within those same relationships. Foucault therefore treats power and resistance as historically interconnected rather than imagining resistance as existing completely beyond power.
  • In the introduction to The Archaeology of Knowledge, Foucault challenges the expectation that an author must remain intellectually identical across different works and periods. His archaeological method instead encourages attention to changes, discontinuities, and the rules governing discourse.
  • In “The Subject and Power” (1982), Foucault suggests that the central problem of the present may not be to discover some fixed, authentic identity hidden inside ourselves, but to question the historical processes through which we have been made into particular kinds of subjects.
  • Individuals are not simply passive objects of power. Foucault’s later work examines how people actively relate to themselves through practices of self-formation, ethical reflection, and what he calls technologies of the self.
  • Freedom and power are not straightforward opposites in Foucault’s analysis. Power relations presuppose possibilities for action, which is why relationships of power can change and why resistance remains possible.
  • Foucault’s later work on ethics turns toward the question of how people constitute themselves as ethical subjects through practices, rules, disciplines, and relationships with themselves rather than treating morality only as obedience to universal laws.
  • Foucault’s historical analyses do not simply argue that the present is better or worse than the past. They attempt to show that institutions, categories, and forms of experience that seem inevitable today have particular histories and therefore can be questioned.
  • His historical method often emphasizes discontinuity rather than a smooth story of intellectual progress. A later way of organizing knowledge or governing people should not automatically be treated as the natural or inevitable result of everything that came before it.
  • Foucault’s work repeatedly asks how human beings become subjects through relationships involving knowledge, institutions, power, and practices of self-formation. This question connects his archaeological, genealogical, and later ethical investigations despite the changes in method across his career.

Quotes to treat with caution

Quotes attributed to Foucault online should be checked against a published work or a reliably identified interview before being presented as his exact words. Foucault is frequently quoted on social media, quotation websites, blogs, and inspirational pages, where passages may circulate without a book title, interview, lecture, page number, original language, or translator. Some of these statements may be genuine passages that have been shortened or translated differently, while others may be paraphrases of Foucault’s ideas or quotations whose wording has changed through repeated online reproduction. One widely circulated example is the statement about people knowing what they do and why they do it, but not knowing what their actions do; although it is commonly attributed to Foucault, we have not traced this wording to a securely identified primary text, so it is safer to describe it as **“attributed to Foucault”** rather than present it as a verified quotation. The same caution applies to many short, polished statements about power, truth, society, freedom, or human nature that appear under his name without a traceable source.

For a philosophy reference site, the distinction between a verified quotation, a translation or wording variant, a paraphrase, and an unverified attribution is important. A quotation should ideally be accompanied by the work in which it appears and, where practical, the relevant chapter, section, lecture, interview, or page reference. When the original source cannot be established, the wording should not be placed in quotation marks as though it were certainly Foucault’s exact language. This approach is especially important for Foucault because his arguments often depend on precise terminology and context, and a short viral sentence can make a complex argument appear much simpler or more absolute than it is. Readers looking for quotations should therefore prioritize his published books, lectures, interviews, and established scholarly editions over unsourced quotation collections.

How to check a Foucault quote

  1. Identify the original source. Look for a specific reference to one of Foucault’s books, essays, interviews, lectures, or courses rather than relying on a quotation website or social-media post. A useful citation should identify the work and, where possible, the chapter, section, lecture, or page on which the passage appears.
  2. Check the wording and translation. Foucault wrote in French, and published English editions are translations, so the exact English wording can differ between translators and editions. A quotation that cannot be found verbatim in one English edition may sometimes be recognizable as a translation variant or shortened version of a passage in the French original.
  3. Verify the passage in a reliable edition. Search the quotation or distinctive phrases in the relevant book itself or in a reputable collection such as the Foucault Reader. Check the surrounding passage rather than extracting a sentence without context, since Foucault’s arguments often depend on distinctions and qualifications that disappear when a passage is reduced to a short quotation.
  4. Distinguish quotation from paraphrase. If the wording summarizes a recognizable Foucaultian argument but cannot be confirmed as his exact words, present it as a paraphrase rather than placing it in quotation marks. This prevents a secondary summary or interpretation from being mistaken for Foucault’s original wording.
  5. Use “attributed to Foucault” when the source remains untraceable. If repeated searches do not establish a reliable primary source, do not present the statement as a verified quotation. Describing it as “attributed to Foucault” accurately communicates the uncertainty while allowing readers to understand that the statement circulates under his name.

In short, a quotation should be treated as verified only when its wording and source can be traced to a reliable edition or other identifiable primary source. This is particularly useful when dealing with popular quotations about power, truth, freedom, surveillance, or subjectivity, because short online versions can remove the qualifications and context that are important to Foucault’s arguments. Frequently asked questions

What is Foucault's most famous quote?

“Where there is power, there is resistance” is among the most frequently cited statements associated with Foucault. It appears in The History of Sexuality, Volume 1, in his discussion of power and resistance. The statement should be read in the context of his broader argument that power operates through relationships and that resistance arises within those relationships rather than existing entirely outside them.

Did Foucault say “knowledge is power”?

“Knowledge is power” is not Foucault's characteristic formulation; the phrase is commonly associated with Francis Bacon. Foucault's own analyses are more closely associated with the concept of power/knowledge, which emphasizes that relations of power and practices of knowledge are deeply interconnected. He examined how institutions and systems of expertise help produce forms of knowledge while those forms of knowledge can also participate in the exercise and organization of power.

Are online Foucault quotes reliable?

Online Foucault quotes should not automatically be treated as reliable, especially when they lack a primary source. Quotes on social media and quotation websites may be shortened, translated differently, paraphrased, taken out of context, or attributed to Foucault without a traceable source. A reliable quotation should ideally be connected to a specific book, essay, interview, lecture, or other identifiable primary source.

Where can I find reliable Foucault quotes?

The most reliable place to find Foucault's quotations is in his published books, essays, interviews, and lecture courses, preferably in a cited edition. Collections such as The Foucault Reader and Power/Knowledge can also be useful, although readers should still check the original work and the translator or editor when an exact quotation matters. Consulting the surrounding passage is also important because a short sentence can lose important qualifications when separated from Foucault's larger argument.

What is Foucault's quote about power and resistance?

Foucault writes, “Where there is power, there is resistance.” In The History of Sexuality, Volume 1, this forms part of his argument that resistance is not located completely outside power. Because power operates through relationships and practices, those relationships also contain possibilities for opposition, modification, and struggle.

What did Foucault say about truth?

Foucault examined how societies establish procedures through which certain statements are accepted, circulated, and recognized as true. His concept of a “regime of truth” concerns the institutions, practices, authorities, and procedures that help determine how truth is produced and authorized within a society. This should not be reduced to the claim that facts are simply whatever people choose to believe.

What did Foucault say about surveillance?

Foucault used surveillance to explain how modern disciplinary power can encourage individuals to regulate their own behaviour. In Discipline and Punish, his discussion of Bentham's Panopticon illustrates a system in which the possibility of observation can lead individuals to behave as though they are continually being watched. Surveillance is therefore connected to discipline, examination, normalization, and the production of self-regulating subjects.

What is Foucault's most important idea?

Foucault's work does not reduce to one single idea, but a recurring concern is how knowledge, power, and practices of subject formation shape the ways people understand themselves and others. His different works approach this problem through archaeology, genealogy, and later studies of ethics and the self. Concepts such as discourse, power/knowledge, discipline, biopower, governmentality, and technologies of the self belong to different stages and contexts of this wider investigation.

Did Foucault believe that everything is about power?

Foucault did not simply argue that every aspect of human life is reducible to power. His analyses examined how power relations operate through institutions, practices, knowledge, and relationships, but he also studied discourse, historical forms of knowledge, ethics, and practices of the self. His later work in particular explores how people actively participate in shaping themselves rather than treating them only as passive objects of power.
