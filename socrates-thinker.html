Overview

Who Was Socrates?

Socrates was an ancient Greek philosopher who lived in Athens during the fifth century BCE, traditionally dated to approximately 469–399 BCE. He became one of the central figures of Western philosophy through a distinctive practice of questioning people about knowledge, virtue, justice, piety, courage, and how a human being should live.

Unlike Plato and Aristotle, Socrates left no surviving philosophical writings of his own. What we know about him comes primarily from Plato, Xenophon, and Aristophanes, whose portrayals differ in purpose and character. This creates the Socratic problem: the historical Socrates must be reconstructed from sources that are not straightforward transcripts or biographies.

In one sentence: Socrates made philosophical examination a practice of questioning beliefs, testing claims, and asking what it means to live well.

Historical setting

Socrates and Ancient Athens

Socrates lived during the fifth century BCE, when Athens was a major political and cultural center associated with democratic institutions, public debate, drama, rhetoric, and intellectual inquiry. His adult life overlapped with the Peloponnesian War between Athens and Sparta, which lasted from 431 to 404 BCE.

He lived through war, plague, political crisis, the temporary rule of the Thirty Tyrants, and the restoration of democracy. Ancient sources portray him engaging people in conversation within this environment of intense public argument, including discussions involving education, persuasion, expertise, and civic life.

Socrates is often contrasted with earlier Greek thinkers who investigated nature and the cosmos, but this contrast should not be overstated. What distinguishes the Socratic tradition is the sustained attention given to human questions: what justice is, what virtue requires, what knowledge amounts to, and how a person should live.

Philosophy

Socrates' Core Philosophical Ideas

Because Socrates wrote nothing, these are best treated as major themes associated with the ancient sources that portray him, rather than as a complete system recorded in his own words.

Examination of beliefs

Beliefs should be tested through questions and reasons rather than accepted merely because they are traditional, popular, or confidently asserted.

Recognition of ignorance

Recognizing the limits of one's knowledge can be the beginning of inquiry and a safeguard against false confidence.

Search for definitions

Socratic conversations repeatedly ask what justice, courage, virtue, piety, or other concepts actually are, rather than settling for examples.

Virtue and moral knowledge

The Socratic tradition places ethical character at the center of philosophical inquiry and repeatedly explores the relationship between virtue and knowledge.

Care of the soul

In Plato's portrayal, the condition of the soul and moral character matter more than external advantages such as wealth, status, or reputation.

The examined life

Philosophy is presented as an ongoing practice of examining one's beliefs, values, actions, and reasons for living.

Method

What Is the Socratic Method?

The Socratic method is a form of philosophical inquiry associated with Socrates in which questioning is used to examine a person's claims. Rather than beginning with a finished doctrine, Socrates asks a person to clarify, define, and defend what they believe.

01Question — identify the concept under discussion.
02Define — ask what the concept actually means.
03Examine — draw out implications of the proposed answer.
04Test — look for contradiction or inconsistency.
05Reconsider — revise the claim or acknowledge that the problem remains unresolved.

In scholarship on Plato's shorter ethical dialogues, this process is often discussed using the Greek term elenchus, commonly translated in terms of examination, testing, or refutation. The phrase “Socratic method” is partly a modern generalization, and the precise form of examination varies among dialogues.

AporiaA state of puzzlement or impasse reached when a proposed answer cannot adequately resolve the philosophical problem.
Ethics

The Examined Life, Self-Knowledge & Virtue

One of the most famous ideas associated with Socrates is the examined life. In Plato's Apology, philosophical examination is presented as central to his life's work. The point is not simply private introspection; it is the critical examination of beliefs, values, choices, assumptions, and reasons for acting.

Socratic ethics repeatedly returns to justice, virtue, courage, moderation, piety, wisdom, and the care of the soul. External success does not by itself make a person good. The deeper question is whether a person's judgments and actions are directed toward what is genuinely just and good.

The phrase “Know thyself” was an ancient maxim associated with the sanctuary of Apollo at Delphi and should not be presented as a securely documented quotation invented by Socrates. Its association with the Socratic tradition is better understood through the broader emphasis on self-examination and awareness of the limits of one's knowledge.

“The unexamined life is not worth living.”
Plato, Apology — Socrates as represented by Plato
Evidence

How Do We Know About Socrates?

The central difficulty in studying Socrates is that no surviving text was written by him. The ancient evidence therefore consists of later portrayals with different genres, aims, and perspectives.

Plato

Plato makes Socrates the central speaker in many philosophical dialogues, including Apology, Crito, Euthyphro, and Phaedo. His dialogues are philosophical and literary works, not verbatim transcripts.

Xenophon

Xenophon presents Socrates in works including Memorabilia, Apology, and Symposium. His portrait often emphasizes practical conduct and ethical guidance.

Aristophanes

Aristophanes portrays Socrates in the comedy Clouds, first produced in 423 BCE. Its comic and satirical character makes it unsuitable as a straightforward biography, but it is important evidence for Socrates' public image during his lifetime.

The Socratic problem

The Socratic problem is the difficulty of determining which ideas and characteristics in the surviving accounts belong to the historical Socrates. Plato, Xenophon, and Aristophanes present different portraits, and no single source can simply be treated as a complete historical record.

399 BCE

Socrates' Trial, Death & Refusal to Escape

399 BCE

Trial in Athens

Socrates was prosecuted on charges traditionally described as impiety and corrupting the young.

Conviction

Death sentence

An Athenian jury convicted Socrates and sentenced him to death. Plato's Apology provides the most famous philosophical account of his defense.

Prison

Crito

In Plato's Crito, Socrates rejects a proposed escape and argues that one should not answer injustice by committing another injustice.

Execution

Phaedo

Plato's Phaedo presents Socrates' final hours and his death after drinking a preparation traditionally identified as hemlock.

The political and social circumstances of the trial were complex. Athens had recently experienced defeat in the Peloponnesian War and political conflict including the Thirty Tyrants. These circumstances provide historical context, but they do not by themselves establish a single explanation for the prosecution.

Legacy

Socrates' Influence on Philosophy

Socrates' influence was transmitted largely through people who knew him, wrote about him, or developed different interpretations of his philosophical example. Plato made him the central speaker in many dialogues, while other members of the wider Socratic circle developed distinct philosophical traditions.

Plato

Developed an extensive philosophical literature in which Socrates became a central figure for questions about knowledge, virtue, justice, politics, reality, and the soul.

Socratic traditions

Later traditions associated with the Socratic circle included Cynic, Cyrenaic, and Megarian developments, which did not all share one philosophical doctrine.

Philosophical practice

The demand for clearer definitions, examination of assumptions, consistency, and moral reflection became enduring features of the Socratic legacy.

Socrates is therefore remembered not simply for a list of doctrines, but for an image of philosophy as a practice: question what seems obvious, examine what you claim to know, and ask how a human being ought to live.

Historical note

What Is Historically Secure — and What Is Uncertain?

Relatively well established

  • Socrates was an Athenian philosopher of the fifth century BCE.
  • He became associated with philosophical conversations and ethical inquiry.
  • No surviving philosophical writings by him are known.
  • He was tried and executed in Athens in 399 BCE.
  • Plato, Xenophon, and Aristophanes provide major ancient evidence about him.

Historically uncertain

  • The exact wording of conversations reported by ancient authors.
  • Which Platonic arguments reproduce historical Socratic views.
  • The exact content and development of Socrates' doctrines.
  • The precise relationship between the historical Socrates and Plato's literary-philosophical character.
Quick answers

Frequently Asked Questions About Socrates

Who was Socrates?

Socrates was an ancient Greek philosopher from Athens who lived approximately from 469 to 399 BCE and became especially associated with philosophical questioning, ethical inquiry, and the examined life.

What is Socrates famous for?

He is famous for philosophical questioning, ethical investigation, the Socratic method, and his influence on Plato and later philosophy.

What did Socrates believe?

He is associated with the pursuit of wisdom, examination of beliefs, virtue, ethical inquiry, and care of the soul. Because he wrote nothing, the precise content of his historical philosophy remains disputed.

What is the Socratic method?

It is a form of inquiry in which questions, definitions, reasoning, and tests of consistency are used to examine claims and assumptions.

Did Socrates write any books?

No surviving philosophical writings by Socrates are known. Our knowledge of him comes primarily from ancient authors such as Plato, Xenophon, and Aristophanes.

Who wrote about Socrates?

Plato, Xenophon, and Aristophanes are the three major surviving ancient sources used to reconstruct Socrates, and they portray him differently.

Why was Socrates put on trial?

He was prosecuted in Athens in 399 BCE on charges traditionally described as impiety and corrupting the young.

How did Socrates die?

According to Plato's Phaedo, Socrates died in prison after drinking a preparation traditionally identified as hemlock following his conviction and death sentence.

Why did Socrates refuse to escape?

In Plato's Crito, Socrates rejects escape and argues that one should not respond to injustice by committing another injustice.

Was Socrates the first philosopher?

No. Earlier Greek thinkers such as Thales, Anaximander, Anaximenes, Pythagoras, Heraclitus, and Parmenides predated him. Socrates became especially important for the ethical and human questions associated with his philosophical practice.
