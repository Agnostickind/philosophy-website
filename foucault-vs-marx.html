Foucault vs Marx: Power, Class and Ideology Compared

Explore Michel Foucault’s and Karl Marx’s different approaches to power, class, ideology, and social relations. This comparison examines how Marx analyzes power largely through class and economic structures, while Foucault investigates the dispersed forms of power operating through institutions, knowledge, discipline, and everyday practices.

By Kavya Jain · Updated 2026-10-03 · Part of our complete guide: Michel Foucault

Short answerMarx locates power mainly in class relations and the economy, with ideology hiding exploitation. Foucault sees power as dispersed and productive, operating through institutions, knowledge and everyday practices, and he is wary of the concept of ideology. Both criticise capitalism's effects, but they differ on where power sits and how to resist it.

Foucault vs Marx at a glance

Marx Foucault
Where is power? In class relations and ownership of production In networks of institutions, practices and knowledge
Type of power Mainly exploitative and repressive Productive as well as repressive. See power/knowledge
Ideology Ideas that disguise class interests Suspicious of the concept; prefers “regimes of truth”
The state Instrument of the ruling class One site among many; power is “capillary”
History Driven by class struggle toward socialism Contingent struggles with no single direction
Resistance Revolution by the working class Local, plural resistances

Marx and Foucault both analyze how social institutions and historically specific forms of power shape human lives, but they locate the central mechanisms of domination differently. Marx's analysis places economic organization, private property, class relations, and the control of the means of production at the center of modern society. Capitalist production creates a relationship between classes, particularly between capitalists who own productive resources and workers who sell their labor power. From this perspective, institutions and ideas can be understood in relation to the material organization of society and the conflicts produced by class interests. Foucault does not deny that economic relations and class structures matter, but he argues that power cannot be adequately explained by reducing it to ownership, the state, or a single ruling class. His genealogical studies instead examine the concrete practices through which people are classified, observed, examined, disciplined, normalized, and governed. Schools, prisons, hospitals, psychiatric institutions, administrative systems, and forms of expert knowledge therefore become important sites for analyzing modern power.

The difference is especially clear in their approaches to ideology, history, and resistance. Marx's concept of ideology is closely connected to the way dominant social relations can be represented as natural, universal, or legitimate, thereby obscuring underlying class interests. Foucault was more reluctant to explain domination through ideology and instead examined how historically specific regimes of truth determine which statements become accepted as true, who is authorized to speak, and which forms of knowledge receive institutional support. He also rejected a single, predetermined historical direction in favor of genealogies that trace contingent struggles and transformations. Their accounts of resistance consequently differ: Marx's political theory gives a central role to collective working-class struggle and revolutionary transformation of capitalist relations, whereas Foucault emphasized that resistance can arise at numerous local points wherever power operates. Despite these differences, Foucault's work developed partly in a intellectual environment strongly influenced by Marxism, and his analyses of capitalism, labor, class relations, and social institutions frequently intersect with questions raised by Marx. The two thinkers are therefore better understood as offering different analytical approaches to power, social conflict, and historical change than as presenting completely unrelated theories.

Where they agree

Marx and Foucault both reject the idea that social institutions, forms of behavior, and relations of power are simply natural or inevitable. Both investigate how historically specific social arrangements are produced and maintained, although they explain those processes differently. Their work also shares an interest in the ways institutions shape human conduct rather than merely restricting people through explicit laws or physical force. This creates an important point of contact between Marx's analysis of capitalism and class relations and Foucault's analyses of discipline, normalization, institutions, and power/knowledge.

  • Both reject the idea that social arrangements are natural or inevitable. Marx treats capitalism, private property, and class relations as historically produced social arrangements rather than permanent features of human society. Foucault similarly examines practices such as imprisonment, psychiatric classification, sexuality, and disciplinary institutions as historical formations that could have developed differently.
  • Both examine how institutions shape human behavior. Marx analyzes institutions and social relations in connection with the organization of labor and the reproduction of class society. Foucault focuses more closely on institutions such as prisons, schools, hospitals, barracks, and factories, showing how observation, examination, classification, training, and normalization can produce disciplined and useful forms of conduct.
  • Both connect social organization with the production of useful and obedient workers or subjects. Marx's analysis emphasizes how capitalist production depends upon the organization and reproduction of labor power. Foucault's account of disciplinary power examines how institutions increase the capacities, efficiency, regularity, and usefulness of individuals while simultaneously making their behavior easier to supervise and regulate.
  • Both take economic organization seriously when explaining modern social institutions. Marx places the capitalist mode of production and class relations at the center of his social analysis. Foucault does not reduce power to economics, but he repeatedly considers how disciplinary practices developed alongside changing economic and social requirements. In Discipline and Punish, he describes the relationship between the accumulation of capital and what he calls the accumulation of men, connecting the management of populations and individual bodies to the development of modern economic systems.
  • Both analyze domination as something reproduced through social practices. Marx shows how class relations are reproduced through economic structures and relations of production. Foucault investigates the everyday procedures through which power is exercised and reproduced, including surveillance, examination, classification, normalization, and institutional routines. Neither approach treats domination as depending solely on the conscious decisions of individual rulers.
  • Both use historical analysis to challenge present social arrangements. Marx's historical materialism examines social formations through changing modes of production and class struggles. Foucault's genealogical method traces the historical emergence and transformation of practices, institutions, and forms of knowledge. Their historical methods are different, but both use history to show that contemporary social arrangements have particular origins rather than being timeless necessities.
  • Both leave room for social transformation and resistance. Marx's theory centers on collective class struggle and the possibility of transforming capitalist social relations. Foucault does not propose the same revolutionary historical model; instead, he emphasizes multiple forms of resistance wherever power operates. In both cases, existing relations of domination are treated as historically changeable rather than permanently fixed.
  • Both are concerned with the relationship between knowledge and social power, although they formulate it differently. Marx examines how dominant ideas can be connected to material and class relations, particularly through his analysis of ideology. Foucault instead develops the concept of power/knowledge, examining how institutions and systems of expertise participate in establishing categories, norms, and accepted forms of truth. This difference is substantial, but both challenge the assumption that knowledge and social power can always be treated as completely separate.

Where they differ

The main difference is that Marx places class relations and the capitalist mode of production at the center of his analysis, whereas Foucault investigates a wider range of historically specific practices through which power is exercised. For Marx, the ownership and control of the means of production, the relationship between capital and labor, and the conflicts between social classes are fundamental to understanding modern society. Foucault does not deny the importance of capitalism or economic relations, but he argues that power cannot be reduced to economic ownership or understood only as something possessed by a ruling class or exercised through the state. His studies therefore examine the detailed operation of power in institutions such as prisons, schools, hospitals, psychiatric institutions, military organizations, and administrative systems. His analysis of discipline is developed particularly in Discipline and Punish; see the Discipline and Punish summary.

  • Power. Marx's analysis connects power closely to class relations, property, labor, and the economic structure of capitalist society. Foucault argues that power is more dispersed and operates through numerous relationships, institutions, practices, and forms of knowledge. Power is therefore not simply something that one class or the state possesses and imposes from above; it is exercised through everyday social practices and institutional mechanisms.
  • Ideology and truth. Marx's concept of ideology is concerned with how ideas can represent social relations in ways that obscure or legitimize material and class interests. Foucault is cautious about explaining domination through ideology because the concept can imply a distinction between false appearances and an underlying truth that has simply been concealed. Instead, he investigates how historically specific regimes of truth establish what counts as legitimate knowledge, who is authorized to speak, and which categories or explanations become institutionally accepted.
  • The state. Marx gives the state an important role in the organization and reproduction of class society, particularly in relation to the interests and power of dominant classes. Foucault does not treat the state as the single center from which modern power originates. His analyses examine the many institutions and practices through which power operates, while also recognizing that these mechanisms can become coordinated with state institutions and governmental objectives.
  • History. Marx develops a historical materialist account in which changes in modes of production and struggles between classes are central to historical transformation. Foucault rejects a single, predetermined historical direction and instead uses genealogy to examine contingent developments, conflicts, institutional changes, and shifts in practices and forms of knowledge. He is therefore less interested in locating history within one overarching progression toward a particular social order.
  • The subject. Marx's analysis is strongly concerned with people as members of social classes and with their position within relations of production. Foucault places greater emphasis on how institutions, norms, classifications, and forms of knowledge help constitute individuals as particular kinds of subjects. This includes the ways people come to understand themselves through categories such as the criminal, the patient, the mentally ill person, or the sexual subject.
  • Discipline and social control. Marx explains exploitation primarily through the organization of production and the relationship between capital and labor. Foucault adds a detailed account of disciplinary techniques such as surveillance, examination, hierarchical observation, training, and normalization. These mechanisms can operate even when there is no obvious act of physical coercion, producing individuals who are both more capable and more closely regulated.
  • Politics and resistance. Marx's political analysis gives a central role to collective class organization and the transformation of capitalist relations of production. Foucault does not develop an equivalent theory in which one revolutionary class represents the universal historical agent. Instead, he emphasizes multiple and often localized forms of resistance arising within particular power relationships. Resistance for Foucault is therefore not necessarily concentrated in a single revolutionary movement or directed toward one predetermined historical outcome.
  • Capitalism. Marx makes capitalism the central object of a systematic critique of modern political economy, focusing on commodities, labor, surplus value, capital accumulation, and class conflict. Foucault studies capitalism from a different angle, asking how particular forms of discipline, administration, population management, and government became connected with changing economic and social conditions. He therefore does not replace Marx's economic analysis with another theory of capitalism; rather, he investigates dimensions of modern power that he considered difficult to explain through economic categories alone.

These differences do not mean that Foucault simply rejects Marx. Foucault frequently engaged with Marxist thought and continued to analyze questions involving capitalism, labor, class, and economic organization. The disagreement is primarily about how broadly power should be analyzed and whether economic relations provide a sufficient foundation for explaining all forms of modern domination. Marx provides a powerful framework centered on production, property, and class struggle, while Foucault shifts attention toward the dispersed practices, institutions, norms, and forms of knowledge through which individuals and populations are governed.

Foucault's own relationship to Marxism

Foucault's relationship with Marxism changed considerably over the course of his intellectual career: he was influenced by Marxist thought, studied under Louis Althusser, and was briefly associated with the French Communist Party in the early 1950s, but he later maintained a critical distance from orthodox Marxism. His early intellectual environment was strongly shaped by postwar French debates involving Marxism, structuralism, and philosophy, and Marx remained an important reference point even when Foucault disagreed with particular Marxist explanations. Rather than treating economic class relations as the foundation from which every form of power could be derived, Foucault increasingly investigated institutions, disciplinary practices, knowledge, sexuality, and the historical formation of subjects. This led him to question interpretations of Marxism that reduced political power to the interests of an economically dominant class or treated the state as the primary location of power.

Foucault nevertheless continued to engage seriously with questions raised by Marx, particularly those concerning capitalism, labor, social organization, and forms of domination. His later lectures at the Collège de France, especially The Birth of Biopolitics (1978–79), examined classical liberalism, political economy, and the emergence of modern forms of neoliberal government. These lectures generated considerable scholarly debate about how Foucault's analysis of biopolitics and governmentality relates to Marxist theories of capitalism. Foucault's approach does not amount to a simple rejection of Marx; instead, it expands the field of analysis by asking how economic systems interact with governmental rationalities, institutions, knowledge, and techniques for governing individuals and populations.

Criticisms of Foucault from a Marxist view

From a Marxist perspective, a major criticism of Foucault is that his analysis of dispersed power can understate the importance of class relations, private property, and economic exploitation. Marxist critics have argued that institutions and disciplinary practices cannot always be understood adequately without examining the capitalist economic structures in which they operate. If power is analyzed primarily through local practices, institutions, norms, and forms of knowledge, critics contend that it can become difficult to explain why certain institutions have particular economic functions or how broader class structures organize social inequalities. Some critics have also questioned whether Foucault's emphasis on multiple and localized struggles provides a sufficiently developed account of collective political organization and large-scale structural transformation.

A related criticism concerns the normative basis of Foucault's critique of domination. Nancy Fraser, among others, questioned whether Foucault's genealogical method provides clear normative grounds for determining why particular forms of power or domination ought to be opposed. If claims about truth, morality, and justice are themselves historically situated, critics ask what standards can be used to justify resistance beyond identifying particular practices as oppressive or undesirable. Defenders of Foucault respond that his purpose was not to construct a universal moral theory but to expose how apparently natural forms of authority and normalization are historically produced, thereby opening them to criticism and transformation. His approach can therefore complement rather than simply replace class analysis by drawing attention to forms of disciplinary and normalizing power that may not be fully captured by an analysis centered on economic exploitation. Frequently asked questions

Was Foucault a Marxist?

No. He studied with Marxists and was briefly a Communist Party member, but later criticised Marxism's focus on class, the state and ideology.

What is the main difference between Foucault and Marx?

Marx sees power rooted in class and the economy; Foucault sees it as dispersed through institutions and knowledge.

Did Foucault criticise ideology?

He was wary of the term because it assumes a truth hidden by distortion. He preferred studying how truth is produced.

Are Foucault and Marx compatible?

Some thinkers combine them, particularly on discipline and capitalism, while others see deep conflicts.
