Nussbaum vs Singer: Two Philosophies of Animal Ethics

Utilitarian suffering vs species-specific flourishing, and what each means for how we treat animals.

  2. Thinkers
  3. Martha Nussbaum

Contents

By Kavya Jain · Updated 2026-10-09 · Part of our complete guide: Martha Nussbaum

Short answer Peter Singer and Martha Nussbaum both argue that animals deserve serious moral consideration, but they ground that conclusion in different philosophical frameworks. Singer's utilitarianism focuses on the equal consideration of the interests of sentient beings, especially their capacity to experience suffering and enjoyment. Nussbaum's Capabilities Approach asks whether individual animals have genuine opportunities to flourish according to the characteristic forms of life of their species. The difference matters because Singer evaluates actions largely by their consequences for interests, while Nussbaum treats animals as individual subjects of justice whose basic capabilities should be protected.

Where they agree

Nussbaum and Singer begin from a rejection of the traditional idea that animals exist primarily for human use. Neither philosopher accepts the assumption that human beings may ignore animal interests simply because animals belong to other species. Both therefore challenge forms of exploitation that cause substantial and avoidable harm, including intensive confinement and many practices associated with factory farming.

They also share a broader ethical insight: the fact that an animal is not human does not by itself make its interests morally irrelevant. Singer expresses this through the principle of equal consideration of interests, while Nussbaum develops a theory of justice that gives animals claims to opportunities for flourishing. Their conclusions can therefore overlap even when their philosophical reasons are different.

The important disagreement is not whether animals matter. It is what it means for animals to matter morally, what kinds of harms count, and what political or legal obligations follow from that moral status.

Why do animals have moral status?

For Singer, the central threshold is sentience: the capacity to experience pleasure, suffering or other forms of conscious experience. If a being can suffer, that suffering gives us a moral reason to take its interests seriously. Rationality, language, intelligence or membership in the human species are not necessary conditions for having morally relevant interests.

Nussbaum also gives animals substantial moral importance, but she asks a broader question. An animal is not merely a being that can experience pain; it is an individual with characteristic abilities and activities through which its life can go well or badly. Justice should therefore be concerned with whether the animal has an adequate opportunity to exercise its basic capabilities rather than merely whether its suffering has been minimized.

This produces an important distinction. Singer's approach begins with the interests of sentient beings. Nussbaum's approach begins with the conditions required for an individual animal to flourish.

Singer's view

In Animal Liberation (1975), Peter Singer develops an argument against the systematic mistreatment of animals using a broadly utilitarian framework. His central principle is that the interests of beings affected by an action should receive equal consideration. This does not mean that humans and animals must always be treated identically. It means that similar interests should not receive different moral weight merely because they belong to different species.

Singer draws on Jeremy Bentham's famous question about whether animals can suffer. The ability to reason or communicate in a human-like language is not the relevant test. If an animal can experience suffering, then that suffering is morally significant.

This leads Singer to his influential criticism of speciesism. Just as racism and sexism can involve giving unfair preference to members of one's own group, speciesism involves treating human interests as automatically more important simply because humans are human. Singer's argument does not require saying that every human and every animal have exactly the same interests. Rather, it requires us to take comparable interests seriously.

For example, an animal's interest in avoiding severe pain should not be dismissed simply because the animal cannot speak, vote or reason like a human. At the same time, a human interest that has no animal equivalent may legitimately receive different treatment. Equality for Singer therefore means equal consideration, not identical treatment.

Because Singer evaluates actions in terms of their consequences, his approach can be powerful when comparing competing interests. But this feature also creates one of the central points of disagreement with Nussbaum: if interests can be compared and consequences assessed, how far may one individual's interests be sacrificed for the benefit of others?

Nussbaum's view

Martha Nussbaum extends her Capabilities Approach beyond human beings and argues that animals should be understood as subjects of justice. Her concern is not limited to reducing suffering. She asks what an animal needs in order to live a life appropriate to its characteristic form of existence.

A capability is, broadly, a genuine opportunity to do or be something important. Applied to animals, the approach therefore asks whether an individual has meaningful opportunities for bodily health, movement, sensory experience, social interaction, play, practical agency and other forms of activity appropriate to its species.

The species-specific element is crucial. A dolphin, elephant, dog, bird and human being do not flourish in identical ways. Justice does not require giving every animal the same environment or the same list of activities. Instead, it requires understanding the distinctive capacities of the animal and protecting a sufficient opportunity to exercise them.

In Frontiers of Justice (2006), Nussbaum identifies the treatment of animals as a major frontier for theories of justice. In Justice for Animals (2023), she develops the argument further and emphasizes the responsibilities of human beings and institutions toward animals.

Nussbaum's approach therefore moves the discussion from the question "How much suffering does this action produce?" toward a broader question: "What kind of life is this individual animal being prevented from living?"

This difference can matter even where an animal appears to be free from obvious physical pain. An animal kept in an environment that prevents movement, social interaction, exploration or other characteristic activities may have its flourishing seriously restricted even if its immediate suffering is relatively low.

Speciesism and equal consideration

Both philosophers challenge the idea that humans automatically deserve greater moral consideration simply because they belong to the human species, but they formulate the problem differently. Singer makes the equal consideration of interests the central corrective to speciesism. Nussbaum instead emphasizes the distinctive capabilities and forms of life of individual animals.

This means that Nussbaum's position should not be confused with the claim that all species should be treated identically. Treating a fish, dog, elephant and human being identically would often make little sense because their needs and capabilities differ. Her argument is that justice must be responsive to those differences rather than imposing a single human-centered model of flourishing.

The contrast can be summarized simply: Singer asks whether an interest deserves consideration despite the species of the being who has it; Nussbaum asks what that particular animal needs in order to flourish as the kind of animal it is.

What counts as harm?

Singer's framework gives suffering a particularly prominent role because suffering is a direct negative experience for a sentient being. Pain, fear, distress and other forms of suffering therefore carry substantial moral weight. Depriving animals of positive experiences or frustrating important preferences can also matter, depending on the interests involved.

Nussbaum's account is broader. Harm can occur when an animal is prevented from exercising central capabilities even when the harm cannot be reduced neatly to a moment of conscious suffering. Confinement, isolation, inability to move freely, deprivation of social relations or the destruction of a suitable habitat can interfere with an animal's characteristic activities.

Consider a bird kept permanently in a tiny cage. A Singer-style analysis would ask about the bird's suffering, preferences and other welfare interests and then consider those interests alongside the interests affected by the human practice. Nussbaum would additionally ask what capabilities the cage prevents the bird from exercising and whether the animal has a genuine opportunity to live the kind of life appropriate to its species.

Individuals, trade-offs and justice

One of the deepest differences concerns the moral importance of individual claims. Utilitarian reasoning evaluates actions by their consequences for those affected. This makes it possible to compare competing interests and, in principle, to accept a loss to one individual when the overall balance of consequences strongly supports it.

Nussbaum is more resistant to this kind of aggregation when fundamental capabilities are at stake. Her concern is that an individual animal should not simply become a number in a calculation of total welfare. If an animal is denied a basic opportunity that is central to its flourishing, producing benefits for other beings does not automatically erase that individual's claim.

This is one reason Nussbaum describes her approach in terms of justice rather than merely welfare. The question is not only how much good or bad an action produces overall, but whether each individual is being given an adequate opportunity to live a minimally flourishing life.

Rights, law and political justice

Nussbaum places a stronger emphasis than Singer on the relationship between animal ethics and legal institutions. If animals are subjects of justice, then their protection cannot depend entirely on private kindness or voluntary changes in consumer behavior. Laws and institutions should recognize duties toward animals and provide mechanisms through which those duties can be enforced.

Her discussion therefore raises questions about legal standing, representation and the political treatment of animals. Legal standing does not mean that animals must participate in courts in the same way human adults do. Rather, legal systems can provide ways for human representatives to bring claims concerning serious harms done to animals.

Singer's work has also had major political and practical consequences, particularly through its criticism of factory farming and animal experimentation. But the philosophical route is different. Singer's primary framework asks what our actions ought to be given their consequences for sentient interests, whereas Nussbaum places those obligations within a broader theory of justice and institutional rights.

How their approaches handle real cases

Factory farming

Both approaches provide strong reasons to criticize intensive factory farming. Singer focuses on the enormous amount of suffering imposed on animals for relatively modest human benefits, especially when alternatives are available. Nussbaum emphasizes the way intensive confinement can systematically prevent animals from exercising basic capabilities such as movement, social interaction, exploration and species-typical behavior.

Animal experimentation

Singer's approach asks whether the expected benefits of an experiment are sufficiently important to justify the suffering and other interests sacrificed by the animals involved. The strength of the case therefore depends partly on the seriousness of the human benefits and the degree of animal harm.

Nussbaum raises an additional justice concern: even if an experiment promises substantial benefits, using an individual animal in a way that seriously violates its central capabilities may be unjust. The moral question is therefore not exhausted by calculating the expected benefits.

Animals kept as pets

Neither philosopher's position can be reduced to the claim that keeping animals is always wrong. The ethical question concerns the conditions under which animals live. A relationship with a companion animal may support important forms of flourishing, but confinement, neglect, breeding practices or treatment that frustrates central capabilities can create serious moral problems.

Wild animals

Wild animals introduce a particularly difficult problem. If suffering matters morally, then the enormous amount of suffering that may occur in nature raises questions about whether humans have any responsibility to intervene. Singer's framework can generate such questions because the suffering of wild animals is still suffering.

Nussbaum's approach also recognizes responsibilities toward wild animals, but the problem is more complicated than simply maximizing welfare. Interventions can disrupt ecosystems and the characteristic forms of life of animals themselves. The challenge is to determine when human intervention protects animals and when it instead imposes another form of human control.

Where they disagree

  • What matters morally? Singer gives central importance to sentient interests, especially suffering and enjoyment. Nussbaum argues that justice must also account for the wider capabilities through which animals flourish.
  • Can welfare be aggregated? Singer's consequentialist framework permits comparison of interests and consequences across individuals. Nussbaum argues that fundamental claims of individual animals should not simply disappear into an aggregate calculation.
  • Is suffering the central measure of harm? Singer gives suffering a particularly important place. Nussbaum argues that an animal can be seriously wronged by being prevented from exercising characteristic capabilities even when immediate suffering is not the only or most obvious harm.
  • Why do species differences matter? Singer rejects species as a basis for discounting comparable interests. Nussbaum considers species differences ethically important because justice must be adapted to different forms of animal life.
  • What does justice require? Singer's approach evaluates what we should do in light of consequences for interests. Nussbaum argues for a threshold of central capabilities that every individual animal should have an adequate opportunity to exercise.
  • What should the law do? Nussbaum gives a particularly prominent role to legal standing, rights and institutional reform. Singer's central argument is more directly framed in terms of moral consideration and consequences.

Criticisms and limitations

Criticisms of Singer

A major concern about utilitarian animal ethics is the problem of aggregation. Critics worry that if benefits and harms can be compared across individuals, the framework may permit serious harm to one individual when enough benefits are produced for others. Even when the calculation is theoretically impartial, critics question whether some individual rights should be protected against such trade-offs.

Another difficulty concerns the practical problem of comparing different forms of suffering, enjoyment and preference satisfaction. Animal minds are difficult to understand, and consequences are often uncertain. This can make real-world utilitarian calculation much less precise than the theory might initially suggest.

Criticisms of Nussbaum

Nussbaum's approach faces a different challenge: identifying the capabilities appropriate to different species can be difficult. Humans must interpret animal behavior and determine which activities are genuinely central to a particular form of life. There can also be disagreement about what constitutes an adequate threshold and how far human institutions should go in securing it.

Her approach can also raise difficult questions about conflicts between species, ecosystems and individual animals. Protecting one animal's flourishing may sometimes affect other animals or ecological systems. Determining the appropriate limits of intervention therefore remains a complex practical problem.

These criticisms do not necessarily defeat either theory. Instead, they show why animal ethics remains difficult even after the basic principle that animals matter morally has been accepted.

What the disagreement ultimately means

The difference between Singer and Nussbaum is ultimately a disagreement about the structure of moral concern. Singer asks us to overcome species prejudice and give the interests of sentient beings impartial consideration. His framework is especially powerful when exposing the enormous suffering that humans can impose on animals for relatively small benefits.

Nussbaum agrees that animals deserve serious moral consideration but argues that the moral picture is incomplete if animals are viewed primarily as containers of pleasure and suffering. Animals are living individuals with characteristic abilities, relationships and forms of activity. Justice therefore requires more than reducing pain: it requires creating conditions in which animals have a genuine opportunity to flourish.

The two approaches can consequently reach similar conclusions while giving different reasons for them. A factory-farmed hen, for example, may be wronged in Singer's framework because she experiences suffering and has interests that are given inadequate consideration. For Nussbaum, the same hen is wronged because her conditions systematically prevent her from exercising central capabilities characteristic of her form of life.

The deepest contrast can therefore be stated as follows: Singer's question is primarily "How should we weigh the interests affected by this action?" Nussbaum's is "What does justice require for this individual animal to have a flourishing life?"

For students of animal ethics, the comparison is valuable precisely because neither question can be dismissed easily. Singer provides a powerful argument for impartiality and against speciesism, while Nussbaum broadens the discussion toward dignity, capabilities, individual claims and political justice.

Frequently asked questions

What is the main difference between Nussbaum and Singer on animals?

Singer uses a utilitarian framework centered on the equal consideration of the interests of sentient beings, especially their suffering and enjoyment. Nussbaum uses the Capabilities Approach and asks whether each individual animal has an adequate opportunity to flourish according to the characteristic capabilities of its species.

Do Nussbaum and Singer agree on animal rights?

They both argue that animals deserve strong moral protection, but Nussbaum gives a more explicit role to rights, justice and legal standing. Singer's principal argument is framed through the moral consideration of animal interests and the consequences of our actions.

Do Singer and Nussbaum both oppose speciesism?

Yes, although they approach the issue differently. Singer argues that species membership should not be used to discount comparable interests. Nussbaum emphasizes that justice should respect the distinctive capabilities and forms of life of different species.

Why does Nussbaum criticize utilitarianism?

Nussbaum argues that utilitarianism can make animal justice too dependent on the calculation of pleasure and suffering or other welfare consequences. She also worries that aggregate calculations can overlook the claims of individual animals and the importance of capabilities such as movement, social interaction and species-specific activity.

What is species-specific flourishing?

Species-specific flourishing means that different animals have different characteristic ways of living well. A bird may need opportunities for flight, a social animal may need meaningful social relationships, and a dolphin may require an environment that allows it to exercise its distinctive aquatic capabilities.

What does Singer mean by equal consideration of interests?

It means that similar interests should receive comparable moral consideration regardless of the species of the individual who has them. It does not mean that humans and animals must always receive identical treatment, because their interests can differ.

What is Nussbaum's Capabilities Approach?

The Capabilities Approach evaluates justice partly by asking whether individuals have genuine opportunities to do and be things that are important to a flourishing life. Nussbaum extends this framework to animals by identifying capabilities appropriate to different forms of animal life.

Which philosopher focuses more on suffering?

Singer places a particularly strong emphasis on suffering because the capacity to suffer is central to his account of moral consideration. Nussbaum also treats suffering as important but argues that justice must address a wider range of capabilities and forms of flourishing.

Which philosopher focuses more on legal rights?

Nussbaum places greater emphasis on animals as subjects of justice and on legal and political mechanisms capable of protecting their claims. Singer's work has also influenced animal-protection law and public policy, but his philosophical framework is primarily utilitarian.
