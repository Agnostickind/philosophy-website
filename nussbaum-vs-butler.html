Nussbaum vs Butler: The "Professor of Parody" Debate Explained

A detailed comparison of Martha Nussbaum and Judith Butler on gender, power, political action, theory, material change, and the purpose of feminist philosophy.

By Kavya Jain · Updated 2026-10-08 · Part of our complete guide: Martha Nussbaum

Short answer The Nussbaum–Butler debate is a disagreement about the purpose and method of feminist theory. In her 1999 essay "The Professor of Parody", Martha Nussbaum criticized Judith Butler for emphasizing symbolic and linguistic forms of resistance while, in Nussbaum's view, offering insufficient guidance for confronting material problems such as poverty, violence and legal inequality. Butler's approach, however, was not simply a theory of voluntary performance or symbolic play: her concept of performativity describes how social norms are repeatedly reproduced and can therefore also be challenged. The deeper disagreement concerns whether changing the norms and categories through which people are understood is itself a form of political action, or whether feminist theory must translate more directly into institutional and material reform.

What was the Nussbaum–Butler debate?

The Nussbaum–Butler debate is best understood as a disagreement over what feminist philosophy should do and how it should communicate its arguments. The most famous moment came in 1999, when Martha Nussbaum published "The Professor of Parody" in The New Republic. The essay criticized Judith Butler's increasingly influential approach to gender, especially the emphasis on performativity, parody, resignification and the instability of identity.

Nussbaum's concern was not simply that Butler had a different theory of gender. She believed that Butler's theoretical vocabulary risked separating feminist intellectual work from the material conditions of women's lives. For Nussbaum, feminist philosophy should ultimately help people confront concrete forms of deprivation and injustice through arguments about rights, capabilities, law, institutions and social policy.

Butler's project began from a different question. Rather than assuming that categories such as "woman" are politically neutral starting points, Butler asked how such categories are produced through language, social norms and relations of power. If the categories themselves help determine whose lives are recognized, then challenging those categories can also have political consequences.

The disagreement therefore goes deeper than a dispute between two individual philosophers. It represents a larger tension between liberal and capabilities-oriented feminism on one side and poststructuralist and queer approaches on the other. It also raises a continuing question: must political theory provide a direct program for reform, or can changing the concepts through which society understands people itself be an important form of political resistance?

The intellectual background

Judith Butler's Gender Trouble, published in 1990, challenged the assumption that gender simply expresses an underlying biological or psychological identity. Butler argued that gender is performative: it is constituted through repeated acts, conventions and norms rather than being the straightforward expression of a fixed inner essence.

Butler's argument developed through engagement with several philosophical traditions. Michel Foucault influenced her understanding of power, discourse and the production of social categories, while Simone de Beauvoir provided an important earlier feminist reference point. Butler also engaged critically with psychoanalysis, structuralism and theories of language.

Nussbaum came from a substantially different intellectual tradition. Her work combines ancient Greek philosophy, especially Aristotle, with contemporary political philosophy and liberal theories of justice. Her Capabilities Approach asks what people are actually able to be and to do, and what social and political conditions are necessary for a life worthy of human dignity.

That difference explains much of their disagreement. Butler begins by questioning the categories and norms through which identities become intelligible. Nussbaum is more interested in identifying the substantive conditions people need in order to flourish. One begins more strongly with the construction of social categories; the other with the conditions of human flourishing and justice.

What is Nussbaum's feminist philosophy?

Nussbaum's feminism focuses strongly on human capabilities, dignity, equality and material opportunity. Her central question is not only whether people are formally recognized as equal but whether they actually possess meaningful opportunities to live lives they have reason to value.

This leads her toward issues such as education, health, bodily integrity, political participation, economic opportunity, freedom of expression and freedom from violence. Her Women and Human Development (2000), for example, develops the Capabilities Approach as a framework for evaluating women's lives across different social and cultural contexts.

Nussbaum therefore tends to judge political theories partly by what they can do for people living under conditions of inequality. A feminist theory that explains how gender categories are constructed may be intellectually valuable, but Nussbaum wants to know what follows for someone facing domestic violence, poverty, discrimination or lack of access to education.

This does not mean that Nussbaum thinks culture and ideas are irrelevant. Her broader work on emotions, literature, education and political culture shows that she considers imagination, public narratives and social attitudes important. Her criticism of Butler is better understood as a disagreement over political priority and practical orientation than as a claim that culture has no political significance.

What is Butler's feminist philosophy?

Butler's feminist philosophy questions the assumption that gender identities are stable foundations from which politics can simply begin. In Gender Trouble, she examines how repeated social practices create the appearance of stable identities and how norms determine which forms of gender are considered natural, coherent or acceptable.

The concept of performativity is central here. It does not mean that individuals freely choose a gender performance in the same way that an actor chooses a costume. Rather, social norms precede individuals and constrain the ways in which identities can be expressed and recognized. Repeated acts reproduce those norms, but repetition also creates possibilities for variation and disruption.

Butler therefore places considerable importance on subversion, resignification and the disruption of established categories. Practices such as drag became philosophically significant because they can expose the constructed character of supposedly natural gender norms. The political point is not merely that parody is entertaining; it is that destabilizing a norm can reveal its contingency.

Her later work also makes clear that Butler's interests extend far beyond gender parody. Precarious Life examines vulnerability, mourning and political violence, while later writings address war, assembly, precarity and the conditions under which people are recognized as grievable or politically visible. Butler's intellectual project therefore cannot be reduced to the claims Nussbaum selected for criticism in 1999.

What do Nussbaum and Butler think about gender?

Nussbaum and Butler both reject the idea that gender inequality can be explained simply by biological difference, but they approach gender from different philosophical directions. Nussbaum is primarily concerned with the ways gender norms affect people's opportunities and capabilities. Butler is more concerned with how the category of gender itself is produced and maintained.

For Butler, asking who counts as a "woman" is not a preliminary question that can be settled before feminist politics begins. The category has a history and is shaped by relations of power. Some people may be excluded or marginalized when political movements treat the category as unified and self-evident.

Nussbaum is more willing to work with substantive claims about human needs and capabilities. She argues that feminist politics requires enough shared ground to identify harms and defend people against them. Without some account of what people should be able to do and be, she worries that critique may become better at exposing problems than at explaining what justice requires.

The disagreement can therefore be summarized as a difference between questioning the category and evaluating the conditions of life within and around that category. These approaches can conflict, but they can also illuminate different dimensions of the same problem.

What counts as political action?

This is arguably the deepest disagreement between Nussbaum and Butler. Nussbaum's criticism is that feminist theory should remain connected to action that changes people's material and legal circumstances. Political action includes organizing, legislation, litigation, institutional reform, education and policies that expand people's capabilities.

Butler's approach asks us to widen the meaning of political action. If institutions depend upon social categories and norms to determine who is recognized as a legitimate person, citizen, worker, parent or victim, then challenging those norms may also alter political possibilities. Changing the language through which a group is understood can affect what kinds of claims become visible and whose suffering is taken seriously.

This does not establish that Butler's approach is sufficient on its own, nor does it prove Nussbaum's criticism. It reveals two different theories of how change happens. Nussbaum emphasizes institutional transformation and substantive opportunity; Butler emphasizes the transformation of norms, recognition and intelligibility.

The practical question is whether these forms of change should be treated as alternatives. A change in law may fail if social norms remain hostile to the people the law protects, while cultural transformation may remain incomplete if institutions continue to reproduce material inequality. The two approaches can therefore be seen as competing priorities but also as potentially complementary levels of political change.

Material change vs symbolic change

Nussbaum's strongest concern is the gap between symbolic resistance and material improvement. Someone can challenge a gender norm through language, identity or performance, but that alone does not necessarily provide food, housing, medical care, physical safety, education or legal protection.

But treating symbolic change as politically irrelevant would also be too simple. Social categories influence how institutions interpret people, whose testimony is believed, which identities receive recognition and which forms of suffering are publicly acknowledged. A change in social meaning can therefore affect material life indirectly and sometimes profoundly.

The disagreement is consequently not simply "material politics versus language." It is about causal priority. Nussbaum asks whether a theory has enough substantive content to guide action against injustice. Butler asks whether supposedly neutral concepts such as "woman," "normal," "human" or "citizen" already contain exclusions that political theory must expose before it can determine what justice should look like.

Power, institutions and social norms

Nussbaum and Butler also differ in how they tend to conceptualize power. Nussbaum's political philosophy often focuses on institutions and social arrangements that either enable or restrict people's capabilities. Laws, economic structures, education systems and political institutions can create conditions in which people have greater or fewer opportunities to flourish.

Butler's approach, influenced strongly by Foucault, pays closer attention to the productive character of power. Power does not only operate by saying "no" or by imposing restrictions from above. It can also produce categories, identities and standards of normality through which people become recognizable subjects in the first place.

This distinction matters for feminism. If power operates only through explicit discrimination, changing discriminatory laws may appear to be the central task. If power also operates through norms that determine whose identity is intelligible or whose life is considered legitimate, then political critique must also examine language, culture and social recognition.

Nussbaum's response is that identifying these mechanisms is valuable but does not by itself answer the normative question of what institutions should do. Her Capabilities Approach attempts to supply such standards by asking which opportunities and forms of human functioning justice should secure.

Why did Nussbaum criticize Butler's writing style?

One of the most memorable parts of "The Professor of Parody" concerns Butler's prose. Nussbaum argued that Butler's writing was unnecessarily difficult and that its complexity could obscure rather than clarify philosophical claims. For Nussbaum, political philosophy has a public responsibility: arguments about justice should be understandable to people outside specialized academic circles.

This criticism is connected to Nussbaum's broader conception of philosophy as a form of public reasoning. If feminist philosophy is concerned with injustice affecting ordinary people's lives, then Nussbaum believes its arguments should be accessible enough to participate in public debate and democratic decision-making.

Butler's defenders have a different view of difficult theoretical writing. Butler argues that conventional language can itself carry assumptions that need to be disrupted. Complex or unfamiliar formulations may sometimes be an attempt to prevent readers from comfortably returning to familiar categories.

The disagreement therefore involves more than literary taste. It raises a philosophical question about clarity and critique: should political theory primarily make its conclusions easier to understand, or can deliberately difficult language sometimes be part of the critical method itself?

What were Nussbaum's main criticisms of Butler?

Nussbaum's 1999 essay makes several connected criticisms. They should be understood as Nussbaum's interpretation of Butler's project, rather than as neutral descriptions of everything Butler believes.

  • Insufficient material politics. Nussbaum argued that parody, resignification and symbolic resistance could seem remote from the concrete needs of people experiencing poverty, violence and legal inequality.
  • Political quietism. She worried that Butler's emphasis on working within existing norms and destabilizing them through repetition could encourage political passivity rather than organized institutional change.
  • An overly negative conception of agency. Nussbaum objected to accounts in which social structures and discourse seem so powerful that individual agency becomes difficult to locate.
  • Obscure philosophical prose. She argued that Butler's language made her arguments unnecessarily difficult to evaluate and accessible mainly to specialists.
  • Insufficient normative guidance. Nussbaum wanted feminist theory to explain not merely how existing categories are constructed but what institutions and societies should do differently.

The common thread is Nussbaum's concern with political efficacy. She wanted feminist philosophy to connect diagnosis to practical judgment: if a theory identifies injustice, what should citizens, lawmakers and institutions do about it?

How did Butler and her defenders respond?

Butler's response can be understood partly through her clarification of performativity. The idea that gender is performative does not mean that people freely choose identities each morning or that gender oppression can be solved simply through theatrical performance. In Bodies That Matter (1993), Butler emphasized the constraints imposed by norms and the material consequences of being recognized or excluded by them.

Butler's defenders therefore argue that Nussbaum set up too sharp a contrast between symbolic critique and material politics. Categories, language and recognition are not detached from material life. They can influence who receives legal recognition, whose body is treated as legitimate, whose testimony is believed and which lives are considered worthy of protection.

Butler also defended the value of theoretical difficulty in her response to criticism. Her position is not that obscurity is automatically good writing. Rather, difficult theoretical language can sometimes be necessary when familiar concepts themselves are part of the problem being analyzed.

Her later work further complicates the portrait presented by Nussbaum's 1999 essay. In Precarious Life and subsequent writings, Butler addresses vulnerability, mourning, war, precarity, assembly and public forms of resistance. These works show a sustained concern with concrete political conditions alongside her earlier analysis of norms and identity.

What does the debate get right — and what does it miss?

The Nussbaum–Butler dispute identifies a genuine problem in political philosophy: a theory can become so focused on conceptual critique that it becomes difficult to determine what practical changes it recommends. Nussbaum's demand for clearer normative and institutional guidance remains important, especially when philosophy addresses poverty, violence, discrimination and unequal access to basic opportunities.

But the reverse danger is also real. A theory can concentrate so heavily on laws and institutions that it overlooks the social meanings that determine how those institutions actually operate. Formal equality does not necessarily eliminate stigma, exclusion or assumptions about whose identity is legitimate. Butler's analysis helps explain why changing a law does not always immediately transform the norms surrounding it.

The strongest reading of the debate therefore does not require choosing between "material change" and "cultural change." Legal reform, economic redistribution, institutional design and changes in social recognition can interact. A feminist politics may need both substantive standards of justice and critical analysis of the categories through which injustice is produced and understood.

This is also why the debate should not be reduced to the claim that Nussbaum is "practical" while Butler is "theoretical." Both are theorists of power and social change. Their disagreement is over where political philosophy should place its emphasis and what kind of explanation is required before effective political action is possible.

Why does the Nussbaum–Butler debate still matter?

The debate remains relevant because contemporary feminist politics continues to move between questions of material inequality, institutional reform, identity, recognition and social norms. Debates about gender identity, reproductive rights, workplace discrimination, violence, poverty and political representation often require both an account of people's material conditions and an analysis of the categories through which those conditions are interpreted.

Nussbaum's approach asks a practical question: What must people actually be able to do and be if they are to live lives of dignity? Butler's approach asks a different but related question: What norms determine whose lives, identities and claims become recognizable in the first place?

These questions can pull political philosophy in different directions, but neither can easily be ignored. A politics concerned only with recognition may fail to address material deprivation, while a politics concerned only with measurable capabilities may overlook the ways in which social norms determine whose needs are seen, whose identity is recognized and whose suffering receives public attention.

That makes the Nussbaum–Butler disagreement valuable beyond the original 1999 exchange. It is a case study in a much larger philosophical problem: how should theory connect explanation, criticism and political action?

Frequently asked questions

What is the Nussbaum–Butler debate?

It is a debate about feminist theory, political action and the relationship between material reform and critique of social norms. It became especially well known after Martha Nussbaum's 1999 essay "The Professor of Parody," which criticized Judith Butler's approach.

What is "The Professor of Parody"?

"The Professor of Parody" is an essay by Martha Nussbaum published in The New Republic on 22 February 1999. Nussbaum criticized Butler's feminist theory, particularly its emphasis on performativity, parody and resignification, as well as Butler's writing style.

What was Nussbaum's main criticism of Judith Butler?

Nussbaum argued that Butler's theoretical approach placed too much emphasis on symbolic and linguistic resistance and did not provide enough guidance for confronting concrete material problems such as poverty, violence and legal inequality.

Did Nussbaum think Butler's theory was completely useless?

No. Nussbaum's criticism was that Butler's approach was insufficient as a model of feminist political action. Her concern was that critique of norms and symbolic resistance did not adequately explain what institutions should do to address material injustice.

What does Butler mean by gender performativity?

Gender performativity means that gender is constituted through repeated acts and social norms rather than simply expressing a fixed inner identity. Butler does not mean that individuals freely choose gender in the same way an actor chooses a role.

Did Butler believe gender was simply a performance?

No. This is a common oversimplification. Butler's theory of performativity emphasizes that gendered acts occur within social norms that constrain individuals. Repetition reproduces those norms, but variation and disruption can also create possibilities for change.

How are Nussbaum and Butler different?

Nussbaum emphasizes human capabilities, dignity, institutions and material conditions, while Butler emphasizes norms, power, identity, recognition and the social production of categories.

Do Nussbaum and Butler disagree about feminism itself?

They share opposition to gender hierarchy, but they disagree about the conceptual foundations and methods of feminist politics. Nussbaum seeks substantive standards for evaluating justice, while Butler is more skeptical of treating identity categories as stable foundations of politics.

Is Butler's philosophy only about parody and drag?

No. Parody and drag are important examples in Butler's early work because they can reveal the instability of gender norms, but her wider philosophy examines power, recognition, vulnerability, precarity, mourning, war and public assembly.

Why did Nussbaum criticize Butler's writing style?

Nussbaum believed Butler's dense prose made her arguments unnecessarily difficult to understand and evaluate. She also believed that feminist political philosophy should be accessible to a wider public rather than primarily to academic specialists.

Did Butler respond to Nussbaum?

Butler responded to criticism of her writing in "A 'Bad Writer' Bites Back," published in The New York Times in 1999. More broadly, her subsequent work continued to develop the relationship between norms, material vulnerability, recognition and political action.

Who is more practical, Nussbaum or Butler?

Nussbaum's philosophy is more directly oriented toward institutional reform and substantive standards of justice, while Butler's work is more focused on critique of norms, categories and relations of power. Calling one simply "practical" and the other "impractical," however, oversimplifies the disagreement.

Can Nussbaum and Butler's approaches be combined?

They can be read as addressing different dimensions of political change. Nussbaum provides a framework for evaluating people's substantive opportunities and capabilities, while Butler helps analyze the norms and categories that shape recognition and exclusion. Combining the two is philosophically difficult but can produce a broader account of social justice.
