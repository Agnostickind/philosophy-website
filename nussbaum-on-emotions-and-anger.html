Martha Nussbaum on Emotions and Anger: Key Ideas Explained

From emotions as judgments in Upheavals of Thought to the case against payback in Anger and Forgiveness.

By Kavya Jain · Updated 2026-10-06 · Part of our complete guide: Martha Nussbaum

Short answer Martha Nussbaum argues that emotions are not blind surges that simply disturb reason. They contain evaluative judgments about what matters to our flourishing, which means they can be examined, criticized and educated. In Anger and Forgiveness (2016), she applies this approach to anger, arguing that its characteristic desire for payback is irrational or counterproductive. She instead defends transition-anger: an initial response to wrongdoing that moves toward preventing injustice and creating constructive change.

What is Nussbaum's theory of emotions?

Nussbaum's theory of emotions holds that emotions are forms of evaluative judgment: they involve ways of seeing something in the world as important to our well-being or flourishing. An emotion is therefore not merely a physical sensation or an irrational force that takes control of the mind. Fear, grief, love, anger and compassion involve assessments about what matters and about how circumstances affect things or people we value.

This position is developed most systematically in Upheavals of Thought: The Intelligence of Emotions (2001). Nussbaum draws on ancient Greek and Hellenistic philosophy, especially Aristotle and the Stoics, while developing her own account. Her central insight is that human beings are vulnerable creatures whose lives depend on people, relationships, projects and circumstances that they cannot completely control. Because we care about these things, events affecting them can generate emotions.

The theory has an important ethical consequence. If emotions contain judgments, then they can sometimes be mistaken, distorted or influenced by prejudice. But this does not mean that emotions should simply be eliminated. Instead, they can be examined and educated. Nussbaum's philosophy therefore rejects both the idea that emotions are always irrational and the idea that every emotional response is automatically trustworthy.

Emotions as judgments

In Upheavals of Thought, Nussbaum defends a cognitive-evaluative view of emotion. Grief involves seeing a loss as significant: something important to one's life has been taken away. Fear involves seeing something valuable as threatened. Anger involves seeing a person or group as having suffered an unjustified wrong. The emotion is therefore directed toward an object that the person regards as significant.

This helps distinguish an emotion from a purely bodily reaction. A racing heart can occur during fear, but the physical sensation by itself does not explain what the person is afraid of or why it matters. Nussbaum's account emphasizes the person's interpretation of the situation. The emotion contains an evaluation: this matters to me, this is threatening, this loss is serious, or this person has been wronged.

The judgment involved in an emotion need not be consciously formulated as a sentence. A grieving person does not necessarily think explicitly, "I judge that this person was an important constituent of my flourishing." The evaluation can instead be embedded in the emotion itself. This is one reason Nussbaum describes emotions as forms of intelligence: they disclose what the person values.

Because these evaluations can be mistaken, emotions can be subject to criticism. Someone might fear a harmless group because of prejudice, feel shame about something that should not be shameful, or become angry because of a false belief about what happened. Rational reflection can therefore interact with emotion rather than simply opposing it.

Emotion, flourishing and vulnerability

Nussbaum's account of emotion is closely connected to the ancient idea of eudaimonia, usually translated as human flourishing or living well. Emotions reveal what a person regards as important for their life. Love makes another person's well-being significant to us; grief reveals the importance of what has been lost; fear reveals our vulnerability to threats; and hope reflects our investment in possible future goods.

This connection means that emotions expose something fundamental about human life: we are not completely self-sufficient. Friendship, family, health, security, love and other external conditions can matter deeply to our ability to flourish. Nussbaum's disagreement with the Stoic ideal of eliminating vulnerability is important here. She thinks that a life containing genuine love and attachment will inevitably expose a person to the possibility of loss.

Vulnerability is therefore not simply a weakness that philosophy should teach us to overcome. It is partly a consequence of having things that matter. The same attachments that make grief possible also make love and friendship valuable. Nussbaum's philosophy asks how people can live with this vulnerability without pretending that they can become completely invulnerable.

This theme connects her work on emotions to her earlier study of Greek tragedy in The Fragility of Goodness. Tragedy repeatedly shows good people suffering because important aspects of life depend on luck, circumstance and the actions of others.

What is compassion for Nussbaum?

Nussbaum treats compassion as a central moral and political emotion. In her analysis, compassion involves judgments that another person's suffering is serious, that the suffering is not primarily the person's own fault, and that the observer recognizes the possibility that similar misfortune could affect people like themselves. Compassion therefore involves both an evaluation of another person's situation and an awareness of human vulnerability.

Compassion matters because it can motivate people to take another person's suffering seriously. A political community may have laws and institutions designed to protect people, but sustaining concern for others requires more than abstract rules. Citizens must also be capable of seeing another person's suffering as something that matters.

At the same time, Nussbaum does not assume that compassion is automatically reliable. People can feel compassion selectively, showing greater concern for members of their own group while ignoring outsiders. Stories, stereotypes and political rhetoric can also influence whose suffering appears serious. Compassion therefore needs to be educated and connected to principles of justice.

Compassion, empathy and imagination

Nussbaum's account distinguishes the moral importance of understanding another person's situation from simply sharing that person's feelings. Empathy can mean imaginatively entering another person's perspective or emotionally resonating with them, whereas compassion involves an evaluative judgment that the person's suffering is serious and matters morally.

This distinction helps explain why Nussbaum gives literature, drama and the arts an important role. Reading a novel does not automatically make someone morally better, but it can provide practice in attending to lives and perspectives different from one's own. This connects to her idea of narrative imagination, which is also central to Not for Profit.

For Nussbaum, the imagination can therefore have political significance. Citizens who can recognize the humanity and vulnerability of people unlike themselves may be better prepared to deliberate about laws and policies that affect diverse populations.

What does Nussbaum think about anger?

In Anger and Forgiveness: Resentment, Generosity, Justice (2016), Nussbaum analyzes anger as a response to perceived wrongdoing. Anger normally involves the belief that a significant wrong has occurred, that someone has been wronged, and that the wrong deserves a response. Her distinctive concern is with the additional desire for payback: the idea that the wrongdoer should suffer because they caused suffering.

Nussbaum argues that this payback element is problematic. Punishing someone cannot literally undo a past injury, restore what was lost or return the victim to the condition they occupied before the wrong. If the purpose of the response is simply to make the offender suffer because the victim suffered, the response can become a form of backward-looking retaliation.

She also argues that anger can contain a problematic concern with relative status. Sometimes the injured person's anger is partly about the idea that the wrongdoer has placed themselves above the victim and that punishment will restore a sense of equality. Nussbaum worries that this desire for status reversal does not provide a rational basis for responding to wrongdoing.

Her argument does not mean that injustice should be ignored. The crucial question is what anger should lead us to do. Nussbaum wants the recognition of wrongdoing to move quickly from outrage toward prevention, protection, compensation, reform and other constructive responses.

What is transition-anger?

Transition-anger is Nussbaum's term for an anger that moves beyond the desire for payback toward constructive action. It begins with a legitimate recognition that something wrong has happened, but it does not make the suffering of the wrongdoer the ultimate goal. Instead, it asks what can be done to prevent the injustice from happening again and how the underlying situation can be repaired.

The idea can be expressed through a shift in questions. Retributive anger asks, "How can the wrongdoer suffer for what they have done?" Transition-anger asks, "What should we do now to address the wrong and prevent its repetition?" The second question directs emotional energy toward reform, protection and future-oriented action.

Nussbaum associates this possibility with traditions of nonviolent resistance. Figures such as Gandhi and Martin Luther King Jr. can be read as examples of resistance that strongly condemns injustice without making revenge the ultimate objective.

The concept is important because Nussbaum does not simply tell people to stop being angry. She is attempting to preserve the morally useful perception that a wrong has occurred while rejecting the retaliatory desire that can make anger destructive.

How does Nussbaum understand forgiveness?

Nussbaum's discussion of forgiveness follows naturally from her criticism of payback. She is skeptical of forms of forgiveness that treat the wrongdoer's apology, confession or repentance as a kind of payment that must be completed before the victim can release resentment. She worries that this can preserve the underlying logic of a moral transaction: the offender suffers or performs the appropriate ritual, and the victim then grants forgiveness.

Her preferred ideal is closer to a generous movement away from resentment. This does not require pretending that the wrong never happened or declaring that the offender is innocent. A person can reject the desire for revenge while still demanding accountability, compensation or institutional punishment where appropriate.

This distinction is especially important politically. Forgiveness of an individual does not mean that courts should abandon law, that institutions should ignore abuse, or that victims should be required to reconcile with people who harmed them. Nussbaum's argument concerns the emotional orientation toward wrongdoing, not the abolition of accountability.

How do emotions function in politics?

Nussbaum argues that political communities cannot survive on abstract principles alone. Citizens need emotional attachments to democratic values and to one another if institutions of justice are to remain stable. This becomes the central concern of Political Emotions: Why Love Matters for Justice (2013), where she examines how emotions such as love, compassion and concern can support a political culture committed to equality and justice.

She is particularly interested in the problem of creating solidarity among citizens who do not know one another personally. A nation or democracy may require people to make sacrifices for strangers, support public institutions and accept obligations toward people outside their immediate families. Political emotions can help make these abstract relationships psychologically meaningful.

In The Monarchy of Fear (2018), Nussbaum examines fear as a powerful political emotion. Fear can lead people to search for someone to blame, intensify anger and encourage exclusionary political responses. Understanding these emotional dynamics can therefore help explain why political movements sometimes turn insecurity into hostility toward particular groups.

Her earlier Hiding from Humanity (2004) examines disgust and shame in relation to law, arguing that these emotions can become dangerous when used to justify the exclusion or degradation of particular people.

Can emotions be educated?

Yes. Nussbaum argues that emotions can be shaped through education, culture, institutions and public practices. Because emotions contain evaluative beliefs about what matters, changing the way people understand themselves and others can influence the emotional responses they develop.

Literature and the arts are particularly important in this respect. Stories can expose people to lives they might otherwise never encounter, while historical and philosophical education can challenge prejudices and encourage reflection on inherited assumptions. This is one reason Nussbaum connects her philosophy of emotion to her defense of the humanities in Not for Profit.

Political institutions also have a role. Public rituals, national symbols, laws and civic narratives can encourage solidarity, but they can also encourage exclusion and hatred. Nussbaum's political philosophy therefore asks not only what institutions should legally require but also what kinds of emotional culture democratic societies should cultivate.

What are the criticisms of Nussbaum's theory of emotions and anger?

Nussbaum's account has generated important debate, especially over whether emotions can be adequately understood as judgments and whether her criticism of anger gives insufficient weight to anger's moral and political functions.

  • Too cognitive. Critics argue that emotions involve bodily reactions, feelings and affective states that cannot be fully captured by describing them as judgments. A judgment-based account may explain the content of an emotion while underplaying what it feels like to experience one.
  • Anger can be morally appropriate. Critics argue that anger is not valuable only when it immediately produces constructive action. Anger can itself be an appropriate response to injustice because it recognizes that a person has been wronged and can communicate the seriousness of that wrong.
  • Anger can motivate political resistance. Amia Srinivasan's "The Aptness of Anger" (2018) argues that anger can be fitting even when it is not straightforwardly useful or constructive. Audre Lorde's "The Uses of Anger" (1981) similarly presents anger as a potential source of clarity, energy and resistance against oppression.
  • The burden of restraint. Some critics worry that asking oppressed or injured people to move beyond anger can place an additional moral burden on those who have already suffered injustice. The question is whether the rejection of revenge should be distinguished from criticism of anger itself.
  • Institutional anger may differ from personal anger. Political movements and institutions sometimes need strong public condemnation of wrongdoing. A complete rejection of anger could therefore make it harder to express moral urgency or mobilize people against injustice.

Nussbaum's position is more specific than the claim that anger is simply bad. Her main target is the payback tendency within anger. The debate therefore concerns whether anger's recognition of wrongdoing can be separated from its retaliatory dimension and whether transition-anger provides an adequate alternative.

Why does Nussbaum's theory of emotions matter?

Nussbaum's theory matters because it challenges a familiar opposition between reason and emotion. If emotions contain evaluations about what matters to us, then emotions are not simply obstacles that rational people must suppress. At the same time, because those evaluations can be false or prejudiced, emotions are not automatically reliable guides to action.

This gives emotion a distinctive place in ethical and political philosophy. We need emotions because human beings care about people, projects and values, but we also need reflection because what we care about and how we evaluate situations can be distorted. The task is therefore not to eliminate emotion but to cultivate better emotional responses.

Her work on anger extends this idea into the problem of political injustice. A person can recognize wrongdoing intensely without making revenge the goal. Transition-anger represents an attempt to preserve moral seriousness while redirecting emotional energy toward justice, reform and prevention.

This also explains why Nussbaum's work on emotions connects so closely to her wider philosophy. Her Capabilities Approach asks what people need to live lives worthy of human dignity, while her work on education, political emotions and animal justice asks how institutions and cultures can respond to vulnerability and flourishing. Emotion is one part of that larger picture of human life.

Frequently asked questions

What is Nussbaum's theory of emotions?

Nussbaum argues that emotions contain evaluative judgments about things that matter to a person's flourishing. They are therefore not merely irrational bodily reactions; they can be examined, criticized and educated.

What does Nussbaum mean by emotions as judgments?

She means that an emotion presents something as important in a particular way. Fear presents something as threatening, grief presents a loss as significant, and anger presents a perceived wrong as serious.

What is eudaimonistic judgment?

A eudaimonistic judgment connects an emotion to the person's own flourishing. An emotion typically concerns something that the person sees as important to living well, including relationships, projects, people they love and other goods.

What are the three judgments of compassion?

Nussbaum's analysis identifies the judgment that the suffering is serious, that it is not primarily the sufferer's own fault, and that the observer recognizes the possibility of a similar misfortune affecting people like themselves.

What does Nussbaum think about anger?

Nussbaum argues that anger can appropriately recognize wrongdoing, but she criticizes its characteristic desire for payback. She thinks responses to injustice should move toward constructive action rather than making the suffering of the wrongdoer the goal.

What is transition-anger?

Transition-anger is Nussbaum's term for an anger that moves from recognition of wrongdoing toward constructive action. Instead of asking how to make the wrongdoer suffer, it asks what can be done to repair the harm, protect victims and prevent similar injustice.

Does Nussbaum think anger is always wrong?

No. Her criticism focuses primarily on anger's retaliatory or payback component. She allows for an initial response of justified anger at wrongdoing but argues that this response should transition toward constructive action.

How does Nussbaum distinguish anger from compassion?

Anger responds to perceived wrongdoing, while compassion responds to serious suffering that is not primarily the person's own fault and that the observer sees as connected to shared human vulnerability. Both involve evaluations, but they direct attention toward different aspects of a situation.

Why does Nussbaum criticize payback?

She argues that payback cannot undo the original harm and can turn justice into a desire to make the offender suffer simply because the victim suffered. She prefers responses aimed at repair, protection, reform and future prevention.

Does Nussbaum support forgiveness instead of justice?

No. Nussbaum's criticism of resentment and payback does not require abandoning accountability. A person can reject revenge while still supporting legal punishment, institutional accountability and other appropriate responses to wrongdoing.

What is Nussbaum's view of emotions in politics?

Nussbaum argues that democratic societies need emotions such as compassion, love and concern for others to sustain commitments to justice. She also studies destructive political emotions, especially fear, disgust and anger.

Which book explains Nussbaum's theory of emotions?

Upheavals of Thought: The Intelligence of Emotions (2001) is her major systematic work on emotions. Political Emotions (2013) applies related ideas to democratic politics, while Anger and Forgiveness (2016) focuses on anger, resentment, forgiveness and justice.
