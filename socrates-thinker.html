Socrates

Ancient Greek philosopher (c. 469–399 BCE) whose relentless questioning of everyday beliefs about justice, virtue, and knowledge became the founding gesture of Western philosophy.

The Socratic problem

INTERMEDIATE

Why we can never be fully sure what Socrates himself believed.

KEY IDEA

Socrates left no writings. Every account of him is secondhand, filtered through authors with their own literary and philosophical agendas.

PlatoXenophonAristophanes

HISTORICALLY SECURE VS. UNCERTAIN

  • Secure: he lived in Athens, questioned people publicly, was tried and executed in 399 BCE.
  • Uncertain: the exact content of his doctrines, and where Plato's own philosophy ends and Socrates' begins.

Three ancient authors give us Socrates, and they disagree. Plato makes him the central speaker of philosophical dialogues that grow more elaborate over time. Xenophon gives a more practical, everyday moral adviser. Aristophanes, writing satire during Socrates' own lifetime, gives a comic caricature entangled with natural philosophy and rhetoric.

No method has settled the problem definitively. The responsible approach treats each source on its own terms rather than collapsing them into one seamless biography.

Athens in his lifetime

BEGINNER

The war-torn, democratic, argument-loving city that shaped him.

KEY IDEA

Socrates practiced philosophy in a city defined by public debate — and by the Peloponnesian War, plague, and political upheaval.

Ancient GreeceAthenian democracy

TIMELINE

  • 431–404 BCE — Peloponnesian War against Sparta; Socrates serves as a hoplite.
  • 404–403 BCE — brief, violent rule of the Thirty Tyrants.
  • 399 BCE — trial and execution, under restored democracy.

Athenian citizens argued cases in court, debated policy in the Assembly, and prized the ability to defend a position in public. Socrates' habit of buttonholing people and testing their claims fit — and provoked — this culture of argument.

His adult life spans defeat, plague, and civil conflict. That instability forms the backdrop to a trial that cannot be understood through philosophy alone.

The Socratic method

BEGINNER

Testing a claim to destruction, one question at a time.

KEY IDEA

Elenchus: a chain of questions that exposes contradictions in a stated belief, often ending in aporia — productive puzzlement rather than an answer.

LogicEpistemologyAporia

THE PATTERN

  • Ask what a concept (justice, courage) means.
  • Take the offered definition seriously.
  • Draw out its consequences.
  • Find a case it can't explain.
  • Revise — or admit the question is harder than it looked.

The goal isn't to win. It's to show that confident, unexamined answers rarely survive contact with their own implications — and that this discomfort is where real inquiry begins.

Knowledge & the examined life

BEGINNER

Recognizing what you don't know as the start of wisdom.

KEY IDEA

"The unexamined life is not worth living" (Plato, Apology) — human life requires ongoing scrutiny of one's own beliefs and choices.

EpistemologyPopular myths about Socrates

COMMON MISQUOTE

"I know that I know nothing" — a popular summary, not a line that appears this way in Plato's Apology.

In the Apology, Socrates investigates the Delphic oracle's claim that no one is wiser than him. Questioning politicians, poets, and craftsmen, he finds people confident in knowledge they can't actually justify — and concludes his edge is simply not mistaking ignorance for wisdom.

Virtue and ethics

INTERMEDIATE

Why he thought wrongdoing is a kind of ignorance.

KEY IDEA

Often summarized as "virtue is knowledge" — a person who truly understands the good is guided toward acting well; wrongdoing stems from a mistaken view of what's beneficial.

EthicsPlato

RECURRING QUESTIONS

  • What is courage? (Laches)
  • What is piety? (Euthyphro)
  • Can virtue be taught? (Meno, Protagoras)

The condition of the soul, not wealth or reputation, is what Socrates treats as the real measure of a life. This is the ethical core beneath the examined life: a life is good only insofar as it's been examined and found just.

Socrates and the Sophists

INTERMEDIATE

Why he refused to be called a teacher — or take a fee.

KEY IDEA

Sophists were paid professional teachers of rhetoric and argument. Socrates distinguished himself by refusing payment and denying he possessed transferable wisdom.

RhetoricPhilosophy of education

CONTRAST

  • Sophists: taught persuasion for a fee; success-oriented.
  • Socrates: shared inquiry, no fee, no claim to finished wisdom.

The contrast shouldn't be flattened into "truth-seeker vs. con artist" — that image comes largely from Plato's own dialogues, which had their own argumentative stakes. Still, Socrates' refusal to sell wisdom became central to his identity.

Socrates and democracy

ADVANCED

A citizen of Athens who questioned rule by majority.

KEY IDEA

Socrates served Athenian democracy, but is portrayed asking whether decisions about justice should rest on expertise rather than popular vote.

Political philosophyThe trial

THE ARGINUSAE EPISODE

On the Council, Socrates was the lone member who refused an illegal proposal to try war generals collectively, despite an angry Assembly — a stand for lawful process over mob sentiment.

This isn't a simple case for or against democracy. It's a narrower, recurring worry: that important collective decisions are best made by people with relevant expertise, not by whoever is most persuasive.

Sources: Plato, Xenophon, Aristophanes

BEGINNER

Three writers, three different Socrates.

KEY IDEA

No single ancient source is a neutral transcript — each shaped Socrates for its own literary or philosophical purpose.

Plato

AT A GLANCE

  • Plato — philosophical dialogues; Socrates as central speaker.
  • Xenophon — Memorabilia; a practical, everyday moral adviser.
  • Aristophanes — Clouds (423 BCE); comic satire, written in Socrates' lifetime.

Aristophanes is the earliest surviving portrait, and its satire suggests Socrates was already a recognizable — and controversial — public figure well before his trial.

The trial (399 BCE)

BEGINNER

Impiety, corrupting the young, and a city still recovering from civil war.

KEY IDEA

Formal charges: impiety (asebeia) and corrupting the young. Political context: Athens had just emerged from defeat and the rule of the Thirty Tyrants.

Death of SocratesDemocracy

CHARGES

  • Impiety toward the city's recognized gods
  • Introducing new divine beings
  • Corrupting the young

Plato's Apology shows Socrates defending himself against both the formal charges and an older public reputation — the one Aristophanes had already caricatured decades earlier.

The last days

BEGINNER

Prison, refusal to escape, and hemlock.

KEY IDEA

Convicted and sentenced to death, Socrates refused an offered escape (Crito) and died by drinking hemlock after a final conversation about the soul (Phaedo).

Crito's argumentPlato

READING PATH: THE LAST DAYS OF SOCRATES

  • Euthyphro — just before the trial
  • Apology — the defense speech
  • Crito — the refused escape
  • Phaedo — the final hours

In Crito, Socrates argues that answering injustice with injustice is never justified — not even to save his own life. That principle, more than the hemlock itself, is what later readers found unforgettable.

The Last Days of Socrates is a modern Penguin Classics title collecting these four dialogues — not a separate ancient text.

The Three Filters — is it real?

BEGINNER

A viral parable, and why it isn't Socrates.

KEY IDEA

No. The "Triple Filter Test" does not appear in Plato, Xenophon, or any ancient source. It's a modern internet-era anecdote.

Apocryphal quotes

THE STORY, IN SHORT

  • Truth — is it certain?
  • Goodness — is it kind?
  • Usefulness — is it useful to hear?

It's a good piece of everyday advice on its own terms — which is likely why it circulates so widely, especially in Spanish as los tres filtros de Sócrates. But treat it as folklore, not philosophy.

Influence and legacy

INTERMEDIATE

How a man who wrote nothing shaped everything after him.

KEY IDEA

Socrates' influence was transmitted entirely through others — chiefly Plato — rather than through his own texts.

PlatoAristotle

SCHOOLS TRACED TO HIS CIRCLE

  • Platonism (via Plato)
  • Cynicism
  • Cyrenaicism
  • The Megarian school

His lasting model isn't a doctrine but a posture: philosophy as an ongoing practice of questioning, rather than a settled body of answers to be memorized.

Was Socrates the first philosopher?

INTERMEDIATE

No — and the "Pre-Socratics" label can be misleading.

KEY IDEA

Thales, Anaximander, Heraclitus, Pythagoras, and Parmenides all predate or overlap with him. His distinction is subject matter — ethics and human life — not chronological priority.

ThalesHeraclitus

BEFORE SOCRATES

  • Thales — water as first principle
  • Heraclitus — flux and change
  • Pythagoras — number and cosmos

"Pre-Socratic" is a chronological convenience, not a claim that ethics began with Socrates — several earlier thinkers addressed human life too.

Key terms at a glance

BEGINNER

The vocabulary this entry relies on.

  • Elenchus Cross-examination by questioning that tests a claim's consistency.
  • Aporia A state of puzzlement reached when a definition fails under questioning.
  • Arete Excellence or virtue — the quality that lets a person or thing perform its function well.
  • Socratic problem The difficulty of separating the historical Socrates from his literary portraits.
  • The examined life Continual critical reflection on one's beliefs and choices, as opposed to living by unexamined habit.

FAQ

BEGINNER

Short, direct answers to the most common questions.

  • Who was Socrates? An Athenian philosopher (c. 469–399 BCE) known for ethical questioning and the examined life.
  • What is the Socratic method? Testing a claim through repeated questions until it holds up or breaks down.
  • Did he write any books? No. He's known only through Plato, Xenophon, and Aristophanes.
  • Why was he executed? Convicted in 399 BCE on charges of impiety and corrupting the young.
  • What did he think of democracy? He served it, but questioned decisions made by majority vote over expertise.
  • Are the Three Filters real? No — a modern parable, not an ancient source.
