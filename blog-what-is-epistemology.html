What Is Epistemology, Actually?

How philosophers investigate knowledge, belief, justification, and the difference between being right and actually knowing.

Author

KKavya Jain

Date

September 2026 · Updated November 2026 · 16 min read

    Epistemology examines what knowledge is, how beliefs are justified, and when a true belief counts as knowledge.

    You probably do epistemology more often than you realize. Epistemology is the branch of philosophy concerned with knowledge: what it means to know something, how beliefs are justified, which sources of information deserve trust, and where the limits of certainty lie.[1]

    When you ask How do you know that?, you have already reached the central epistemological question. The subject becomes especially important when a claim is persuasive but its evidence is weak, when a true belief seems to be true only by luck, or when two people have access to the same information and still disagree.

    Short answer: Epistemology is the philosophical study of knowledge and justified belief. It asks questions such as: What is knowledge? What makes a belief justified? Can perception, memory, reason, and testimony give us knowledge? And how much certainty can human beings reasonably expect?

    What does epistemology study? The core questions

    At first glance, epistemology can sound like a specialized academic subject. In practice, it begins with a remarkably ordinary problem: people believe things, but not everything people believe is true, and not every true belief seems to qualify as knowledge. A person can guess the right answer, repeat something they heard, remember an event incorrectly, or reach a conclusion from evidence that happens to point in the right direction.

    That gives epistemology several connected tasks. It studies knowledge itself, the justification of belief, the sources from which beliefs are formed, the reliability of those sources, and the possibility of error. Philosophers therefore do not ask only whether a proposition is true. They also ask what connects the believer to that truth.

    The word comes from the Greek episteme, meaning knowledge or understanding, and logos, meaning an account or study. The English term is a nineteenth-century coinage, but the questions are as old as philosophy itself: they run from Plato’s dialogues to the debates of present-day philosophers.[a]

    The central epistemological problem is not simply: “Is this true?” It is also: “What gives me the right to believe that it is true?”

    Knowledge vs. true belief: why being right isn’t enough

    Suppose someone asks you the capital of a country and you confidently give an answer. If the answer is correct, you have said something true. But imagine that you chose the answer by guessing. You were right, yet it would be strange to say that you knew the answer merely because your guess happened to succeed.

    This distinction between truth and knowledge is one of the reasons epistemology is difficult. Knowledge appears to require some appropriate connection between a person’s belief and the fact that makes the belief true. Exactly what that connection must be has been debated for centuries.

    The issue also explains why epistemology is not simply a catalogue of facts. It is concerned with the conditions under which a person is entitled to regard a belief as knowledge.

    Types of knowledge: knowing that, knowing how, and acquaintance

    The word “know” covers more than one kind of achievement. The kind that epistemologists study most is propositional knowledge, or knowing that something is the case: knowing that Paris is the capital of France, or that water boils at a lower temperature at altitude. Propositions can be true or false, which is why this kind of knowledge connects so directly with belief, truth, and justification.[b]

    A second kind is knowing how: knowing how to ride a bicycle, play the piano, or speak a language. Gilbert Ryle argued that this is a distinct ability and cannot simply be reduced to knowing a long list of facts. Other philosophers dispute that, and hold that knowing how is a form of knowing that. The question is still open, and it matters for debates about skill, expertise, and learning.

    A third kind is knowledge by acquaintance: knowing a person, a place, or a sensation directly rather than through description. Bertrand Russell drew a famous contrast between this and knowledge by description. You may know a great deal about a city from books without knowing the city itself. Most of what follows concentrates on propositional knowledge, because that is where the classic definition and its problems arise.

    Justified true belief: the classic definition of knowledge

    A traditional way of analysing propositional knowledge is the formula “justified true belief.” In its simplest form, three conditions are involved: you believe the proposition, the proposition is true, and you have adequate justification for believing it.

    Belief

    You actually accept the proposition rather than merely considering it or accidentally stating it.

    Truth

    The proposition corresponds to the facts; a false proposition cannot be known simply because you are convinced of it.

    Justification

    Your belief has an appropriate epistemic basis rather than being a completely unsupported guess.

    For a long time, this framework looked like a natural starting point.[2][c] It also made intuitive sense: knowing something should involve believing it, getting it right, and having a reason for believing it. But a famous set of counterexamples showed that these three conditions may not always be sufficient.

    The Gettier problem: when a justified true belief isn’t knowledge

    In 1963, Edmund Gettier published a short paper presenting cases in which a person has a justified true belief, yet the truth of the belief depends on an element of luck. These cases became known as Gettier problems.[3]

    In one of Gettier’s own cases, Smith has strong evidence that Jones will get a job and that Jones has ten coins in his pocket. From this Smith reasonably concludes that the man who will get the job has ten coins in his pocket. In fact Smith himself gets the job, and he happens to have ten coins in his own pocket. His belief is true and well supported, yet it seems wrong to say that he knew it, because it is true for reasons he had no idea about.

    A simpler illustration is a broken clock. Imagine that a clock has stopped at 3:00. You look at it at exactly 3:00 and form the justified belief that it is 3:00. Your belief is true, and you have a reason for it: the clock appears to provide evidence. Yet the clock is not functioning as a reliable timekeeper. Your belief is true partly because of coincidence.[4][d]

    The philosophical importance of the example is not the clock itself. The deeper problem is that justification, truth, and belief can all be present while the truth of the belief is connected to the justification in the wrong way. Gettier’s paper therefore changed the direction of twentieth-century epistemology and encouraged philosophers to investigate what, if anything, must be added to the traditional analysis of knowledge.[e]

    Proposed repairs take different forms. One is to add a fourth condition: for instance, that the belief must not depend on a false assumption. (Smith’s reasoning ran through the false belief that Jones would get the job.) Others replace or supplement justification with a requirement that the belief be reliably formed, or that it be “safe,” meaning it could not easily have been false. None of these has won general agreement, and the search for a definition of knowledge remains an open project.

    Important distinction. Gettier did not show that the three conditions are useless. He showed that justified true belief does not appear to be sufficient for knowledge in every case.

    What makes a belief justified? Foundationalism, coherentism, and reliabilism

    Saying that knowledge requires justification invites an obvious question: what is justification? Ask someone for a reason, and then ask for a reason for that reason, and a problem soon appears. The chain of reasons can go on forever, it can loop back on itself, or it can stop somewhere. This is the regress problem, and the main theories of justification can be read as three different responses to it.[1][f]

    Foundationalism holds that the chain stops at basic beliefs, which are justified without depending on other beliefs. Candidates include immediate perceptual experience and self-evident truths. Other beliefs are then supported by these foundations, like the upper floors of a building. The challenge is to explain why the basic beliefs deserve their special status. Descartes’ search for beliefs that cannot be doubted is a classic foundationalist project.

    Coherentism rejects the building picture in favour of a web. A belief is justified by how well it fits with a larger system of beliefs, and no single belief is the foundation of the rest. Critics reply that a perfectly coherent system could still be a fairy tale with no connection to reality. A third option, infinitism, accepts the endless chain and holds that it is enough for good reasons to be available, though it remains a minority view.

    Reliabilism, associated with Alvin Goldman, changes the question. On this view a belief is justified if it is produced by a reliable process, such as normal vision or careful memory, even if the believer cannot cite any reasons for it. That makes reliabilism an externalist theory, since what justifies the belief lies partly outside the believer’s own perspective. Internalists insist that justification must depend on factors the believer can reflect on and access. A well-known objection to externalism imagines a person who reliably forms true beliefs through an undetected clairvoyant power: does he really know what his hunches tell him? Virtue epistemology adds a further angle, treating knowledge as success that comes from the believer’s intellectual virtues, such as careful and open-minded inquiry.

    Sources of knowledge: where does knowledge come from?

    Once philosophers ask what knowledge is, another question follows: how do we acquire it? Human beings do not have a single source of information. We perceive the world, remember previous experiences, reason from premises, and depend heavily on what other people tell us.

    Each source creates both possibilities and problems. A visual experience can provide evidence, but perception can be affected by lighting, distance, perspective, or illusion. Memory allows us to retain information, but memories can be incomplete or distorted. Reason can produce conclusions that follow from premises, but a valid argument can still begin with a false premise. Testimony lets us learn things we could never investigate ourselves, but speakers can be mistaken, uninformed, biased, or dishonest.

    A further difficulty is that checking one source usually means relying on another. You can test a doubtful memory by consulting a photograph, but you then trust your eyes to read it. This raises the question of whether our basic sources can be vindicated without some circularity, or whether we are entitled to trust them by default until we have a reason for doubt.

    Epistemology therefore asks not only whether a source is useful, but under what conditions it is reliable and what kind of justification it can provide.

    Perception, reason, memory, and testimony compared

    Perception

    What you learn through seeing, hearing, touching, tasting, and other forms of sensory experience.

    Reason

    What can be established through thinking, inference, mathematics, logic, or other forms of rational reflection.

    Memory

    What you retain from previous experiences or previously acquired information.

    Testimony

    What you learn from other people, institutions, texts, teachers, experts, and other communicative sources.

    Testimony deserves particular attention because much of ordinary human knowledge is socially acquired. You do not personally observe every historical event, conduct every scientific experiment, inspect every distant country, or independently verify every fact you use. Epistemology therefore has to explain how dependence on other people can sometimes produce knowledge rather than mere secondhand belief.[5][g]

    The list is not meant to be exhaustive, and some philosophers add introspection (awareness of one’s own mental states) or intuition. The sources also work together in practice. A scientific result depends on careful perception, reasoning about what the data mean, memory of earlier work, and trust in the testimony of other researchers. Understanding how they combine is part of understanding how knowledge is actually built.

    A priori and a posteriori knowledge: knowing without and through experience

    Epistemologists also classify knowledge by how it is justified. A priori knowledge is justified independently of particular experience: that seven plus five equals twelve, or that nothing can be red all over and green all over at once. You do not need to survey the world to check such claims. A posteriori knowledge depends on experience: that it is raining outside, or that the Moon has no atmosphere. (A priori does not mean that experience plays no role at all, since you must learn concepts before you can think with them. It means that observation does not supply the justification.)

    The distinction connects directly to the historical dispute between rationalists and empiricists, discussed next. Rationalists hold that substantial knowledge of the world can be gained a priori, while empiricists doubt this. Hume, for example, divided reasoning into “relations of ideas,” such as mathematical truths, and “matters of fact,” which depend on experience.[8]

    Immanuel Kant pushed the discussion further by asking whether there can be claims that extend our knowledge and yet are known a priori, which he called synthetic a priori judgments. He argued that mathematics and the basic principles of natural science are examples. Whether he was right is still debated, but his question reshaped modern philosophy.[h]

    Rationalism vs. empiricism: reason, experience, and knowledge

    The history of modern epistemology also contains a major dispute about the role of experience and reason. Rationalist philosophers such as René Descartes emphasized the power of reason and the possibility of knowledge that does not depend entirely on sensory experience. Empiricist philosophers such as John Locke and David Hume placed much greater emphasis on experience in explaining how human beings acquire ideas and knowledge.[6][7][8][i]

    The contrast should not be reduced to the claim that one side believed in reason and the other believed in the senses. Both traditions contain more complicated positions, and both use reasoning. The important issue is how different kinds of justification arise, what their limits are, and whether some knowledge can be obtained independently of particular experiences.

    This debate helped shape modern questions about certainty, innate ideas, causation, perception, and the limits of human understanding. Kant is often described as attempting a synthesis, arguing that knowledge needs both the raw material supplied by experience and the structuring contribution of the mind.

    Skepticism: how much can we really know?

    Epistemological skepticism pushes the problem further. Instead of asking whether a particular belief is justified, the skeptic may ask whether our ordinary methods of forming beliefs could ever provide the certainty we assume they provide.

    Classic skeptical arguments exploit the possibility of error. Dreams, perceptual illusions, misleading appearances, and radical doubt about the external world can all be used to ask whether our experiences guarantee that things are as they seem. The point is not necessarily that the skeptical scenario is true. Rather, it challenges us to explain what justifies confidence in our ordinary beliefs.[6][j] Modern versions replace the dream or the deceiving demon with the image of a brain in a vat, fed experiences by a computer.

    Philosophers respond to skepticism in different ways. Some try to identify foundations for knowledge; others argue that knowledge does not require absolute certainty; still others examine how justification works within ordinary practices of inquiry. The debate reveals an important lesson: knowing something may not require eliminating every imaginable possibility of error.

    Many contemporary epistemologists are fallibilists: they accept that you can know something even though your evidence does not rule out every possibility of being wrong. G. E. Moore offered a famous reply of a different kind. He argued that he was more certain of ordinary facts, such as that he had hands, than he could ever be of the premises of a skeptical argument. Critics respond that this simply assumes what the skeptic questions.[9]

    Why epistemology matters in everyday life

    Epistemology becomes practical whenever you decide whether evidence is good enough to support a belief. Consider a headline that makes a dramatic claim. You might ask who reported it, what evidence was offered, whether the source has a record of accuracy, whether independent sources agree, and whether the conclusion goes beyond what the evidence establishes.

    The same structure appears in ordinary situations. You may evaluate a friend’s account of an event, decide whether to trust your memory of a conversation, judge whether an expert’s testimony is relevant to a question, or reconsider a belief because new evidence has appeared. These are not formal philosophical arguments, but they involve the same underlying questions about evidence, reliability, justification, and error.

    Good epistemology does not mean becoming suspicious of everything. It means learning to distinguish different levels of evidential support and to calibrate confidence accordingly. Sometimes the rational response is certainty; sometimes it is provisional acceptance; sometimes it is simply “I don’t know yet.”

    Contemporary epistemology: social, formal, and applied questions

    Much current work in epistemology starts from the observation that knowing is rarely a solitary activity. Social epistemology studies how knowledge is shared and tested across communities: how we should treat expert testimony, how institutions such as courts and laboratories produce reliable conclusions, and what you should do when an equally informed person disagrees with you. Is the right response to hold firm, or to give their view real weight?

    A closely related area examines how prejudice can damage knowledge. Miranda Fricker’s work on epistemic injustice describes cases in which a person is wronged specifically as a knower, for example when their testimony is given less credibility because of who they are.[10] These discussions show that epistemology is not only about individual minds but about fairness and trust within a shared practice of inquiry.

    Formal epistemology takes a different route, using probability theory to model belief. Instead of treating belief as all-or-nothing, it works with degrees of confidence and asks how these should change when new evidence arrives. This approach links epistemology closely to logic and to the philosophy of science, where the same questions arise about how evidence supports theories.

    The limits of epistemology: what it can’t promise

    It is tempting to imagine that philosophy can provide a final method that guarantees certainty about every important question. Epistemology does not offer such a universal shortcut. Philosophers disagree about the nature of justification, the requirements for knowledge, the reliability of different epistemic sources, and the appropriate response to skepticism.

    That disagreement is not evidence that the subject has failed. Many epistemological questions are difficult precisely because they concern the standards by which beliefs themselves are evaluated. Any proposed standard can generate a further question: why should that standard count as sufficient?

    The result is a discipline that is less about collecting a final list of unquestionable beliefs and more about understanding the structure of inquiry. It teaches us to separate truth from confidence, evidence from assumption, and knowledge from lucky success.

    Epistemology vs. metaphysics and logic: how the branches differ

    Beginners often mix epistemology up with its neighbours, so it helps to separate them. Epistemology asks how we know and what counts as knowing. Metaphysics asks what exists and what reality is like. Logic studies what makes reasoning valid. A simple example shows the division: whether numbers exist is a metaphysical question, how anyone could know mathematical truths is an epistemological one, and whether a particular proof is valid is a question for logic.

    In practice the branches overlap constantly. George Berkeley argued that to be is to be perceived, a claim that is metaphysical and epistemological at once. Kant’s Critique of Pure Reason asks what the mind can know and, in doing so, what kind of reality we can know about. The philosophy of mind and the philosophy of science also depend on epistemological ideas about evidence, perception, and justification.

    Key epistemology terms at a glance

    These are the terms that appear most often in textbooks and encyclopedia entries on the subject.

    • Belief: accepting that a proposition is true.
    • Justification: the epistemic standing that distinguishes a well-grounded belief from a guess. Some authors use “warrant” for whatever turns a true belief into knowledge.
    • Propositional knowledge: knowing that something is the case, as opposed to knowing how or knowing by acquaintance.
    • A priori / a posteriori: knowledge justified independently of particular experience, or dependent on it.
    • Foundationalism: the view that justification rests on basic beliefs.
    • Coherentism: the view that a belief is justified by fitting into a coherent system of beliefs.
    • Reliabilism: the view that justified beliefs are those produced by reliable processes.
    • Internalism / externalism: the dispute over whether justification depends only on what the believer can access by reflection.
    • Skepticism: the challenge that we lack knowledge or justified belief in some domain.
    • Fallibilism: the view that one can know something even while admitting some possibility of error.

    Epistemology in summary: what it is and why it matters

    In the simplest terms, epistemology is the philosophy of knowledge. But that short definition opens into a much larger set of questions: What is knowledge? What makes a belief justified? Can a person know something without being absolutely certain? Which sources of information are reliable? How should testimony and memory be evaluated? What happens when a belief is true only by luck?

    The subject matters because human beings are constantly forming beliefs. We cannot investigate everything personally, and we cannot eliminate every possibility of error. Epistemology gives us a disciplined way to think about those limitations while asking what separates responsible belief from mere confidence.

    So the next time you hear a claim and instinctively ask, “How do you know?”, you are asking one of philosophy’s oldest and most persistent questions.

    Where to start reading epistemology

    You can approach the subject through the classic texts or through the modern debate. A sensible order moves from the original questions to the historical dispute and then to the contemporary problem.

    • Plato, Theaetetus. An early dialogue that asks what knowledge is and tries several definitions. Start with our page on Plato.
    • Descartes, Meditations on First Philosophy. The best-known exercise in radical doubt. See the Meditations entry.
    • Locke, An Essay Concerning Human Understanding. The founding text of British empiricism. See the Essay entry.
    • Hume, An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding. A shorter, more readable route into empiricism and skepticism. See the Enquiry entry.
    • Kant, Critique of Pure Reason. Difficult but central. See the Critique entry and our page on Kant.
    • Gettier, “Is Justified True Belief Knowledge?” Only three pages long, and the starting point of the modern debate.

    Frequently asked questions about epistemology

    What is epistemology in simple terms?

    Epistemology is the branch of philosophy that studies knowledge. It asks what it means to know something, what makes a belief justified, how knowledge is acquired, and what limits there are on human knowledge.

    What is the traditional definition of knowledge?

    A traditional analysis describes knowledge as justified true belief: the person believes the proposition, the proposition is true, and the person has adequate justification for believing it. Gettier cases are famous for challenging the idea that these three conditions are always sufficient.

    What is a Gettier problem?

    A Gettier problem is a case in which someone has a justified true belief but appears not to have knowledge because the truth of the belief depends on luck or coincidence.

    What are the main sources of knowledge?

    Commonly discussed sources include perception, reason, memory, and testimony. Epistemology investigates the reliability and limits of each source rather than assuming that any source is infallible.

    What is the difference between rationalism and empiricism?

    Rationalism gives a stronger role to reason in explaining knowledge, while empiricism gives a stronger role to experience. The historical positions are more nuanced than this simple contrast, and both traditions use reasoning.

    Why is epistemology important in everyday life?

    Whenever you evaluate evidence, decide whether to trust a source, reconsider a memory, or distinguish a well-supported belief from a guess, you are dealing with questions that epistemology studies systematically.

    What makes a belief justified?

    Epistemologists offer competing theories. Foundationalists say some beliefs are basic and support others. Coherentists say a belief is justified by fitting into a coherent system of beliefs. Reliabilists say a belief is justified if it is produced by a reliable process. Internalists and externalists also disagree about whether the believer must be able to access the grounds of justification.

    What is the difference between a priori and a posteriori knowledge?

    A priori knowledge is justified independently of particular experience, as with logic and mathematics. A posteriori knowledge depends on experience, as with most claims in everyday life and the empirical sciences.

    What is skepticism in epistemology?

    Skepticism questions whether we have knowledge or justified belief in some area, such as the external world. Skeptical arguments use possibilities of error, such as dreams or deceiving demons, to challenge the idea that our beliefs are secure, and philosophers respond to them in several ways.

    What is the difference between epistemology and metaphysics?

    Epistemology studies knowledge and justified belief, asking how we know. Metaphysics studies the nature of reality, asking what exists and what it is like. The two overlap, since claims about what exists raise questions about how we could know them.

    Notes

    1. The word was used in English by the Scottish philosopher James Frederick Ferrier in his Institutes of Metaphysic (1854), where he divided philosophy into epistemology and ontology. The Greek episteme is used by Plato and Aristotle for knowledge or scientific understanding, and its precise sense is itself debated. ↩

    2. Epistemologists also discuss knowing how to do something, a distinction Gilbert Ryle developed in The Concept of Mind (1949), and knowing a person or place by acquaintance, which Bertrand Russell contrasted with knowledge by description in The Problems of Philosophy (1912). This article concentrates on propositional knowledge, which is where the debate about justified true belief takes place. ↩

    3. The label “traditional” should be used with care. Plato discusses a similar proposal in the Theaetetus, but the dialogue leaves the question unresolved, and some historians think the idea of a long-settled “traditional” analysis was simplified by twentieth-century textbooks. ↩

    4. The stopped-clock case is usually credited to Bertrand Russell, who used it before Gettier’s paper appeared. Gettier’s own examples are different, involving two men applying for a job and a man who owns, or is believed to own, a Ford. The clock is a common teaching example of the same kind of problem. ↩

    5. Proposed responses include adding a fourth condition, such as the requirement that the belief not rest on a false assumption, and replacing justification with reliability, safety, or other conditions. Timothy Williamson has argued that knowledge should be treated as basic rather than analysed at all. There is no consensus among epistemologists. ↩

    6. The regress problem is often traced to the ancient skeptics, who stated it as part of a set of modes known as Agrippa’s trilemma: an argument can regress without end, move in a circle, or rest on an unsupported assumption. Alvin Goldman’s reliabilist proposal appeared in his 1979 paper “What Is Justified Belief?” Laurence BonJour’s case of Norman the clairvoyant is the standard objection to simple reliabilism. ↩

    7. Philosophers disagree about whether testimony is a basic source of justification or whether it must be backed by independent evidence that the speaker is reliable. Hume is often read as favouring the second view and Thomas Reid the first. C. A. J. Coady defends the view that testimony is a basic source. ↩

    8. The a priori / a posteriori distinction concerns how a claim can be known. The analytic / synthetic distinction concerns what makes a claim true: its meaning alone, or how the world is. The two can come apart. Kant held that some synthetic claims are knowable a priori, and Saul Kripke later argued that some necessary truths, such as that water is H2O, can only be known a posteriori. Kant’s discussion is in the Critique of Pure Reason (1781/1787). ↩

    9. “Rationalism” and “empiricism” are labels applied by later historians, and many of the philosophers grouped under them would not have used the terms. The groups also overlap. Descartes did experimental work, and Locke accepted that reasoning plays a major part in knowledge. ↩

    10. Skepticism has ancient roots in the Pyrrhonian and Academic traditions. Descartes’ dream and evil-demon arguments in the first Meditation are a famous modern version, but he used doubt as a method for finding secure foundations and did not himself accept skepticism as a conclusion. The brain-in-a-vat version was developed in the twentieth century, notably by Hilary Putnam. ↩
