🏛 Ancient Greek Philosophy

Where Western philosophy began — reason replacing myth, and the first great questions about truth, virtue, and the nature of reality.

🌊 Pre-Socratics 👤 Socrates 👤 Plato 👤 Aristotle

The Birth of Western Philosophy

Around the 6th century BCE, thinkers in the Greek city-states began asking something new: what if the world could be explained not through the will of the gods, but through reason, observation, and argument? That shift — from myth to logos — marks the traditional starting point of Western philosophy.

Over roughly three centuries, this tradition grew from scattered natural philosophers asking what the universe is made of, to Socrates turning the question inward toward ethics and the examined life, to Plato and Aristotle building the first comprehensive philosophical systems — systems still taught, debated, and built upon today.

🌊 The Pre-Socratics

Thales of Miletus

c. 624 BC – c. 546 BC

Widely regarded as the first philosopher in the Western tradition. Rather than explaining nature through myth, Thales searched for a single natural substance underlying everything, proposing that water was the fundamental principle of the universe.

Why He Matters

  • First to seek natural, not divine, explanations
  • Founder of natural philosophy
  • Bridged philosophy, mathematics, and early science
"Water is the principle of all things."

Heraclitus

c. 540 BC – c. 480 BC

Known for his doctrine of universal flux — the idea that everything is constantly changing, famously captured in his observation that no one steps in the same river twice. He also proposed the Logos, a rational structuring principle underlying the chaos of change.

Why He Matters

  • Introduced the philosophy of constant change
  • Unity of opposites
  • Concept of the Logos
"No man ever steps in the same river twice."

Democritus

c. 460 BC – c. 370 BC

Along with his teacher Leucippus, Democritus founded atomism — the theory that reality consists of indivisible particles (atoms) moving through empty space, with all phenomena explained by their motion and arrangement rather than divine will.

Why He Matters

  • Founder of atomism
  • Early materialist worldview
  • Influenced later scientific and Epicurean thought
"Nothing exists except atoms and empty space."
👤 Socrates

Socrates

c. 470 BC – 399 BC

Socrates turned philosophy's focus from nature to human life — ethics, virtue, and how to live well. He wrote nothing himself; everything we know comes through his students, chiefly Plato. His method was relentless questioning, exposing contradictions in what people claimed to know.

The Socratic Method

Rather than lecturing, Socrates asked question after question, guiding his conversation partner to discover the weaknesses in their own beliefs. This technique — elenchus — remains a foundational tool in philosophy, law, and education today.

Trial and Death

In 399 BCE, Socrates was tried in Athens on charges of corrupting the youth and impiety. Found guilty, he was sentenced to death by drinking hemlock. He accepted the sentence rather than flee, arguing that a citizen owes obedience to the laws of his city even when he believes them unjust.

Why He Matters

  • Father of Western ethics
  • The Socratic Method
  • Model of philosophical integrity to the point of death
"The unexamined life is not worth living."
👤 Plato

Plato

c. 428 BC – c. 347 BC

A student of Socrates, Plato founded the Academy in Athens — one of the first institutions of higher learning in the Western world. He wrote in dialogue form, using Socrates as his central character to explore justice, knowledge, love, and the ideal state.

Theory of Forms

Plato argued that the physical world we perceive is only a shadow of a higher, unchanging reality made up of perfect "Forms" — the true nature of things like Justice, Beauty, and the Good, which physical objects only imperfectly resemble.

The Republic

Plato's most famous work imagines an ideal city ruled by philosopher-kings, and uses it to explore justice, education, and the structure of the human soul — still one of the most influential works in the history of political philosophy.

Why He Matters

  • Theory of Forms
  • Founder of the Academy
  • The Republic — foundational political philosophy
"Knowledge is the food of the soul."
👤 Aristotle

Aristotle

384 BC – 322 BC

A student of Plato, Aristotle broke from his teacher's emphasis on abstract Forms toward careful observation of the physical world. He founded the Lyceum in Athens and wrote foundational works across logic, biology, ethics, politics, and metaphysics — later tutoring Alexander the Great.

Virtue Ethics

In the Nicomachean Ethics, Aristotle argued that living well means cultivating virtues — habits of character struck as a "golden mean" between excess and deficiency — and that human flourishing (eudaimonia) is the ultimate goal of a good life.

Formal Logic

Aristotle created the first systematic study of logical reasoning, developing the syllogism as a tool for valid argument — a framework that dominated Western logic for over two thousand years.

Why He Matters

  • Founder of formal logic
  • Virtue ethics
  • Foundational contributions to biology, politics, and metaphysics
"Knowing yourself is the beginning of all wisdom."

From Athens Onward

By the time Aristotle's students carried his teaching across Alexander's empire, Greek philosophy had already laid the foundations for logic, ethics, political theory, and science. Meanwhile, on the other side of the world, entirely different philosophical traditions were asking equally profound questions.
