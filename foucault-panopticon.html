Foucault's Panopticon Explained: Surveillance, Discipline and Power

Michel Foucault’s Panopticon explains how Jeremy Bentham’s prison design became a model for understanding surveillance, discipline, and power. In Discipline and Punish, Foucault used the Panopticon to show how the possibility of constant observation can encourage self-regulation and shape behavior in modern institutions and society.

Short answerThe panopticon is a prison design by Jeremy Bentham in which a central tower can see every cell while inmates cannot tell when they are watched. What is the panopticon?

The Panopticon was a prison and institutional design proposed by the English philosopher and utilitarian Jeremy Bentham in the late eighteenth century. Bentham developed the idea in letters written in 1787 and subsequently published it as Panopticon; or, The Inspection-House in 1791. The basic design consists of a circular building in which individual cells are arranged around the outer circumference, facing inward toward a central inspection tower. The architectural arrangement is designed so that an inspector in the central position could potentially see the occupants of the cells, while the occupants could not reliably see the inspector or determine whether they were being watched at any particular moment. Lighting, windows, shutters, partitions, and the spatial separation of the cells are therefore essential to the design because they create an asymmetry of visibility: the person being observed is visible, while the observer remains difficult to see. Bentham believed that this uncertainty could make supervision more efficient because the inspector would not need to intervene physically or constantly monitor every prisoner. Instead, each person would have reason to behave as though observation could occur at any time. Bentham also conceived the Panopticon more broadly than a prison, imagining that a similar principle of organized inspection could be applied to institutions such as schools, hospitals, factories, and other establishments in which supervision, order, and disciplined conduct were important. The Panopticon is therefore both a specific architectural proposal and a general model for organizing people through continuous possibilities of observation.

Michel Foucault gives the Panopticon a much broader philosophical significance in the chapter “Panopticism” in Part Three of Discipline and Punish (1975). Foucault does not treat Bentham's design simply as a proposed prison or as an example of nineteenth-century architecture; he uses it as a model of disciplinary power. Its importance lies in the way the arrangement of visibility can encourage individuals to regulate their own conduct because they cannot know with certainty when they are being observed. The possibility of surveillance can therefore become more important than constant physical surveillance itself: once individuals behave as though they may be watched, external supervision can be partially transformed into self-monitoring and self-discipline. Foucault connects this mechanism with a wider set of disciplinary techniques, including observation, examination, classification, comparison, normalization, and the organization of space. The Panopticon consequently becomes what Foucault calls a “diagram” of a form of power that can operate across different modern institutions rather than merely a particular prison structure. Its significance in Foucault's philosophy is therefore the relationship between visibility, surveillance, knowledge, and the regulation of conduct: by making individuals potentially visible to an authority while making the operation of that authority difficult to determine, disciplinary systems can encourage people to participate in their own regulation. How the panopticon works

The Panopticon works through asymmetric visibility and uncertainty about observation: the person being observed can be seen by an observer but cannot reliably see the observer or know when observation is actually taking place. In Jeremy Bentham's original design, prisoners are placed in individual cells arranged around the inside of a circular building, while an inspection tower stands at the center. The arrangement of the cells, windows, lighting, and screens is designed so that the inspector can potentially observe each prisoner while remaining difficult for the prisoners to see one another or determine whether the inspector is watching at any particular moment. This uncertainty is crucial because the system does not require a guard to intervene every time someone breaks a rule. Instead, prisoners are encouraged to behave as though they might be observed at any time. The possibility of surveillance therefore becomes sufficient to influence conduct, making visibility itself a mechanism of control. The Panopticon's efficiency, in Bentham's conception, comes from producing disciplined behavior with relatively little continuous physical intervention: the individual modifies their conduct because they cannot confidently distinguish moments of surveillance from moments when they are not being watched.

Foucault uses this mechanism in Discipline and Punish as a model for understanding a broader form of disciplinary power. For Foucault, the significance of the Panopticon is not simply the prison building itself but the way surveillance can become incorporated into the behavior of the person being observed. When individuals know that they may be observed without knowing exactly when observation occurs, they can begin to regulate themselves even when an authority is not visibly intervening. Power consequently becomes more continuous and less dependent on direct force: the possibility of surveillance encourages people to monitor, correct, and constrain their own actions. Foucault describes this effect by arguing that the individual becomes the principle of his own subjection. The Panopticon therefore illustrates how disciplinary power can operate through the organization of space, visibility, observation, examination, and self-regulation. Foucault extends the model beyond prisons to institutions such as schools, hospitals, factories, and other settings in which individuals can be observed, compared, assessed, and trained. The central principle is that effective discipline does not necessarily require constant punishment; it can work by arranging conditions in which people come to regulate their own behavior under the possibility of being observed.

The plague town and the leper: two models

Foucault opens “Panopticism” in Discipline and Punish with two contrasting historical models of managing disease and social disorder. In his account of a plague-stricken town in the seventeenth century, the population was subjected to detailed spatial organization and continuous surveillance: the town was divided into districts, movement was restricted, inhabitants were registered and accounted for, households were inspected, and officials monitored compliance with the regulations. The individual was therefore kept visible, identified, and located within an organized system of observation. Foucault contrasts this disciplinary model with the older treatment of lepers, in which those considered infected were separated from the healthy population and excluded from ordinary social life. For Foucault, the contrast represents two different ways of exercising power: the leper model works primarily through exclusion and separation, whereas the plague model works through surveillance, classification, registration, spatial partitioning, and the continuous observation of individuals. The Panopticon subsequently combines these principles into a more general diagram of disciplinary power, in which individuals are not simply expelled from society but are placed within an organized field of visibility where their behavior can be observed, examined, and regulated.

Aim Cast out the diseased Control and examine everyone
Method Banishment Records, inspection, partitioning
Result A pure and an impure group Individuals known and managed

The panopticon is the plague model made into architecture.

A figure, not just a building

For Foucault, the Panopticon is significant not simply as a particular prison design but as a “figure of political technology”: a model for organizing people, space, visibility, and information so that individuals can be observed and their conduct can be regulated. In Discipline and Punish, he argues that this logic can extend beyond prisons to institutions such as schools, hospitals, barracks, factories, and asylums, where individuals may be separated, observed, examined, classified, compared, and corrected according to established norms. The important feature is therefore not the physical presence of a circular building or a central watchtower, but the underlying arrangement of visibility and discipline. Individuals are placed within a system in which their behavior can be recorded and evaluated, while the possibility of observation encourages them to regulate themselves. Foucault's point is consequently not that society is literally a prison, but that techniques resembling the Panoptic model can operate across different institutions and forms of social organization. Modern examples of panoptic logic

  • CCTV and facial recognition can create conditions in which people know that they may be identified or observed without knowing exactly when or whether a particular system is monitoring them.
  • Workplace monitoring, including keystroke logging, productivity dashboards, access records, screen monitoring, and call recording, can make employees' activities continuously measurable and subject to evaluation.
  • Schools and examination systems use attendance records, examinations, assessments, rankings, and other forms of measurement to observe, compare, classify, and evaluate students.
  • Online platforms collect and analyze information about user activity, interactions, and preferences, while algorithmic systems may use this data to classify, recommend, rank, or personalize content.

These examples share some features with the Panoptic model, particularly visibility, measurement, data collection, and the possibility that people modify their behavior because they know they may be observed or evaluated. However, modern digital systems are not simply literal versions of Bentham's centralized prison design. Observation can be decentralized, automated, reciprocal, or carried out through data systems rather than a single visible observer. Thomas Mathiesen's concept of the “Synopticon”, developed in his 1997 work Viewer Society, describes a different arrangement in which the many watch the few, particularly through mass media. Later, Gilles Deleuze argued in his 1990 essay “Postscript on the Societies of Control” that the enclosed, institution-based disciplinary mechanisms associated with Foucault were increasingly being supplemented by more continuous and flexible forms of control. These concepts are therefore useful for comparing different forms of modern surveillance and social regulation, rather than treating them as interchangeable terms.

Criticisms of Foucault's use of the panopticon

  • The Panopticon was primarily a proposed design. Bentham developed the scheme extensively, but his complete Panopticon prison was never constructed according to his original plan in Britain. Critics therefore caution against treating the Panopticon as a straightforward description of how actual prisons historically operated. Foucault's argument is less dependent on the building having been widely implemented than on the design functioning as a model of disciplinary power, but the distinction remains important. A proposed architectural system should not automatically be treated as evidence that real institutions operated according to the same principles.
  • Foucault's interpretation is broader than Bentham's original purpose. Foucault uses the Panopticon as a model for analysing disciplinary power, while Bentham presented it as a practical institutional design concerned with efficient inspection, management, reform, and the prevention of abuse. Bentham's proposal also reflected his utilitarian concern with arranging institutions so that supervision could be made effective and economical. Critics therefore argue that Foucault's philosophical use can understate some of Bentham's own stated objectives and the specific historical context of the proposal. The Panopticon in Foucault should consequently be understood as a reinterpretation and analytical model, not simply as a summary of everything Bentham intended the design to accomplish.
  • The Panopticon can make power appear too centralized. The classical Panopticon presents a relatively simple relationship: an inspector occupies a central position while individuals around the perimeter are potentially observed. Modern systems of power are often more dispersed. Surveillance and discipline can involve governments, employers, schools, corporations, algorithms, platforms, institutions, peers, and individuals simultaneously. There may be no single observer who possesses complete knowledge or occupies a literal equivalent of Bentham's central tower. This makes the traditional Panopticon useful as a model of visibility and uncertainty, but less adequate as a complete description of how contemporary power is organized.
  • Modern surveillance is often decentralized and reciprocal. Digital monitoring can involve governments, companies, platforms, automated systems, and users observing one another rather than a single central observer. Social media is particularly complicated because users voluntarily produce information about themselves while also observing and being observed by other users. Recommendation systems, advertising networks, data brokers, workplace software, smartphones, and automated analytics can collect and process information without a human observer continuously looking at each individual. These arrangements do not fit neatly into the image of a single guard watching from a central tower.
  • The model can overemphasize visibility as the source of discipline. Foucault's Panopticon places visibility and the possibility of observation at the center of disciplinary power, but institutions can regulate conduct through many other mechanisms. Rules, incentives, examinations, rankings, rewards, punishments, professional norms, economic dependence, social expectations, and bureaucratic procedures can shape behavior even when direct surveillance is limited. A person may therefore comply with an institutional norm not because they believe they are being watched, but because the norm has become socially expected, materially advantageous, or embedded in an administrative procedure.
  • People do not always respond to surveillance with passive obedience. The Panoptic model can sometimes make individuals appear more disciplined and predictable than they actually are. People can resist, evade, manipulate, ignore, or challenge systems of surveillance. They may also develop strategies for presenting themselves differently to different observers. In workplaces, schools, prisons, and online environments, surveillance can therefore produce resistance as well as compliance. This is important because disciplinary power is not necessarily complete or uncontested simply because observation is possible.
  • Surveillance can produce participation as well as constraint. Contemporary digital platforms complicate the traditional Panopticon because people frequently participate in systems that collect their information. Users post photographs, location information, opinions, purchases, professional histories, and personal preferences, sometimes because participation provides social, economic, or practical benefits. This means that surveillance cannot always be explained as something imposed entirely from above. Individuals may actively contribute to the production of the information through which they are monitored or categorized. The relationship between surveillance and consent is therefore more complicated than the one-way observation represented by Bentham's architecture.
  • The Panopticon does not fully explain automated forms of surveillance. In contemporary digital systems, information can be collected, classified, scored, and acted upon by automated processes without a person directly observing the individual. Algorithms may detect patterns, assign categories, estimate risk, personalize content, or determine what information is shown to a user. In such cases, the important mechanism may not be uncertainty about whether a human is watching, but uncertainty about how data are being interpreted and what consequences an automated system may produce. The Panopticon can still illuminate the experience of being potentially monitored, but additional concepts are needed to explain algorithmic and data-driven power.
  • The model may understate differences between institutions and historical contexts. A prison, school, hospital, factory, and digital platform do not exercise power in identical ways. Their purposes, rules, technologies, forms of authority, and relationships with the people they regulate differ substantially. Applying the Panopticon too broadly can therefore make very different institutions appear to share one underlying structure when their actual mechanisms of control may be quite different. The concept is most useful when treated as an analytical model that highlights particular features of power rather than as a universal explanation of every institution.
  • The Panopticon can obscure the importance of knowledge and classification. Surveillance is not only about seeing people; institutions also record, compare, classify, measure, and interpret what they observe. Foucault's broader discussion of disciplinary power includes examination and the production of knowledge, but readings focused exclusively on the Panopticon can reduce the argument to simple watching. In practice, a system may become powerful not merely because an individual is visible, but because information about that individual is converted into records, categories, rankings, diagnoses, assessments, or administrative decisions.
  • The Panopticon should not be treated as Foucault's complete theory of power. Foucault does not reduce all power to surveillance or claim that every modern institution is literally a Panopticon. The Panopticon is one particularly influential model within his analysis of disciplinary power. His wider work also examines practices of normalization, examination, knowledge, governmentality, biopolitics, and the ways in which power operates through multiple social relationships. Treating the Panopticon as a universal theory of surveillance can therefore simplify Foucault's broader and more complex account of how power operates.

Frequently asked questions

What is the panopticon in simple terms?

A circular prison where one guard in a central tower can watch all inmates, who cannot see when they are being watched.

Who invented the panopticon?

Jeremy Bentham described it in 1787, and it was published in 1791. Foucault made it famous in Discipline and Punish (1975).

What does Foucault say about the panopticon?

He treats it as a diagram of disciplinary power: constant possible observation leads people to regulate their own behaviour.

Is social media a panopticon?

Some scholars say it shares panoptic features, such as tracking and self-monitoring, while others argue it differs because the watching is decentralised and often voluntary.
